The conventional treatment of Carthage opens with the foundation:
Dido and her companions, the Tyrian colony of 814 BCE (give or
take a contested decade), the new city slowly growing into
prominence on its African coast. The framing makes Carthage the
subject of a colonial-foundation story whose principal arc is the
city's own development. What that framing under-represents is
that Carthage was not the first Phoenician foundation in the
western Mediterranean, was not for centuries the most important,
and emerged into metropolitan dominance not through founding
intent but through structural factors operating across a network
that substantively predated it and that for several centuries
treated it as one node among many.

This theme treats the network as the analytic subject and
Carthage as the node within it that eventually grew larger than
the system itself. The framing is network-first: the 9th-c. BCE
Tyrian western expansion was a coherent enterprise with its own
internal logic, its own coordinating infrastructure, its own
resilience patterns; individual foundations operated as nodes
within this enterprise rather than as autonomous colonial acts;
Carthage was one foundation among many until the structural
factors of the late 7th and 6th centuries BCE shifted the
network's center of gravity westward. The story is the
transformation: network → emergent dominant node → metropolitan
center with periphery.

The site treats this as the structural opposite end of the
[continuity-into-Roman-Africa narrative](/narratives/punic-continuity-in-roman-africa).
That work establishes that Punic culture did not end at 146 BCE;
this theme establishes that Punic civilization did not begin at
the 814 BCE Dido foundation either. Both bookends push past the
conventional closure-and-opening framings to reveal a longer
and more substantively coherent cultural arc.

The discipline throughout: tangible and well-attested evidence
leads, legendary material is explicitly bracketed as such. The
network's institutional infrastructure (the Melqart cult, the
annual delegations, the archaeologically attested sanctuary
distribution) is the load-bearing evidence; the foundation
legends (Dido at Carthage, the Tyrian Hercules at Gadir, the
specific dates and circumstances of individual foundations) are
treated as legendary tradition with historicizable kernels
rather than as direct attestation.

## The Phoenician homeland background

The network's western expansion built on the existing structure
of the Phoenician homeland city-state system. [Tyre](/places/tyre)
and [Sidon](/places/sidon) — alongside [Byblos](/places/byblos),
Arwad, Berytus, and others — were politically independent city-
states that operated as a substantively integrated commercial-
cultural network across the Levant from at least the early Iron
Age. The homeland was already a network *before* the western
expansion: shared script (the 22-character Phoenician alphabet
that the Greeks would later adapt), shared language with regional
dialectal variation, shared religious practices coordinated
through the major sanctuaries (the Melqart cult of Tyre, the
Astarte cult of Sidon, the Eshmun cult also at Sidon), shared
commercial conventions, regular elite intermarriage and political
coordination, internal competition for trade and political-
diplomatic position but external coordination when facing larger
powers.

The homeland network's relationship with the Levantine imperial
powers — Egyptian, Assyrian, subsequently Persian — was
substantively commercial-tributary across centuries. The
Phoenician cities paid tribute to the dominant power; they
retained substantial internal autonomy in commerce, religion, and
city governance; they participated in the imperial economic
system as the principal Mediterranean maritime-commercial
specialists. This is the network configuration the western
expansion grew out of.

## The 9th-c. BCE western expansion

The substantive western Phoenician expansion is conventionally
dated to the late 9th century BCE on the basis of:

- The earliest reliable archaeological foundation horizons at
  the major western sites (Gadir's earliest Phoenician
  occupation level; the early-9th-c. levels at Lixus and Mogador
  on the Atlantic; the late-9th-c. foundation horizon at
  Carthage itself).
- The Nora Stone from Sardinia, a 9th-c. Phoenician inscription
  that is the earliest dated Phoenician text outside the
  Levant and documents the network's commercial-religious
  reach to the central Mediterranean at this period.
- The Greek-tradition foundation dates for Carthage (conventional
  814 BCE per Timaeus), Gadir (conventional 1100 BCE per
  Velleius Paterculus, almost certainly too early), and other
  western foundations — these dates are treated as legendary
  tradition with historicizable kernels rather than as direct
  attestation, and the archaeological horizons are the
  principal evidence for the actual chronology.

The economic drivers were multiple. **Silver demand:** the
Iberian silver mining region behind Gadir produced silver at a
scale that no Mediterranean source could match, and the
Phoenician network's coordination of silver export from Iberia to
the Levant (and subsequently to the broader Near East) was the
principal commercial logic of the western reach. **Metals more
broadly:** copper from Cyprus and Sardinia, tin from the Iberian
and Atlantic sources, iron from various nodes — the network's
metals trade was substantially the entire Mediterranean's metals
trade for several centuries. **Agricultural land:** the Phoenician
homeland's limited agricultural hinterland constrained its
demographic growth; the western foundations established
substantial agricultural extensions (the Carthaginian *chora* most
prominently; the Sicilian and Sardinian agricultural zones) that
supported population growth the homeland could not sustain.

The strategic drivers were also multiple. **Assyrian pressure:**
from the early 9th c. BCE forward, the Assyrian imperial expansion
toward the Phoenician homeland imposed increasing tribute demands
and political constraint on Tyre and the other homeland cities;
western expansion was partly an exit strategy that allowed the
network to maintain commercial and political viability outside
the Assyrian sphere. **Homeland city-state competition:** the
homeland cities competed for commercial and political position
in the Levant; colonial foundations served as competitive
instruments that extended individual cities' (principally Tyre's)
reach without requiring direct Levantine confrontation.

## The network's geographic logic

The Phoenician colonial network's geographic distribution
followed coherent strategic-commercial logic that the surviving
archaeological and literary record allows partial reconstruction
of. The principal route ran along the southern Mediterranean
shore: from the Levantine homeland, west to Cyprus (Kition and
other Phoenician-occupied Cypriot sites), to Crete (the
Kommos sanctuary among others), to the central Mediterranean
(Sicily's western coast — Motya, Solunto, Panormus; Sardinia's
southwest — Sulci, Tharros, Nora), to North Africa (the
Tripolitanian coast — Lepcis, Sabratha; the Tunisian zone —
Utica, Carthage, Hadrumetum; the broader North African coast
westward), to Iberia (Gadir, Malaca, Sexi, the broader southern
Iberian Phoenician zone), and to the Atlantic (Lixus, Mogador
on the Moroccan coast — the network's western edge).

The route's logic was navigational and commercial. The
one-day-sailing distances between principal nodes meant ships
could move along the network with predictable stopping points;
the network's commercial coordination meant ships could provision
and trade at each node; the religious infrastructure (the
Melqart sanctuaries at the principal stops) meant ships could
participate in shared ritual practice as part of the commercial
operation. The network was a sea-route as well as a colonial
system; the foundations were as much harbor-and-provisioning
infrastructure as they were settlements.

The deeper inland reach was limited to specific high-value
locations. **Iberian silver mining behind Gadir:** the network's
single most important inland extension, with Phoenician trading
posts running deep into the Iberian interior to access the silver
sources directly. **The Carthaginian African *chora*:** the
substantial agricultural hinterland that Carthaginian
administration would develop into the network's largest
inland extension. **Various other specific extensions:** the
Lixus river-valley inland reach, the Sicilian western interior
under Punic control, the Sardinian agricultural zones.

The network was not territorial in the way later Greek and
Roman colonial-territorial systems would become. It was a system
of nodes along a maritime route, with each node controlling
substantively limited hinterland and the network as a whole
controlling the route between them. This is the network logic
that defined its first several centuries.

## The Melqart cult as coordinating infrastructure

The single most directly attested element of the network's
substantive coordination is the [Melqart cult](/deities/melqart).
The site treats this in detail under the claim
[Melqart cult as network infrastructure](/claims/melqart-cult-as-network-infrastructure);
the summary here:

The Melqart sanctuary at [Tyre](/places/tyre) was the network's
metropolitan religious center — the *peripatos* (sacred precinct)
that the network's western colonies sent annual delegations to
across at least six centuries. The delegation is firmly attested
in [Diodorus 20.14](/sources/diodorus-library), in the
pseudo-Aristotelian *On Marvellous Things Heard*, and in
scattered other Greco-Roman literary references. The
[Carthage-Tyre annual delegation](/events/annual-tyre-delegation)
is the best-documented case; analogous delegations from other
western nodes (Gadir, the Sicilian and Sardinian foundations)
are inferred from the broader pattern but are less directly
attested.

Melqart sanctuaries are attested archaeologically at every major
Phoenician colonial foundation: the famous Heracleion at
[Gadir](/places/gadir) (the principal western sanctuary, which
Hannibal would visit before the Italian campaign); the
[Cippi of Melqart](/artifacts/cippi-of-melqart) from Malta
documenting the cult's diffusion to the central Mediterranean;
the Lixus Melqart cult on the Atlantic; the Motya cult; the
Tharros cult; and at Carthage itself. The cult's geographic
distribution is the same as the colonial network's: every
major node has a Melqart sanctuary.

The cult's institutional features document substantive
coordination rather than merely shared culture. The
*egersis* (annual ritual death and resurrection of Melqart by
fire) was a network-wide observance with regional adaptations;
the *peripatos* of the Tyrian sanctuary was the principal
metropolitan religious destination; the cult had specific
priestly offices (the *rab kohanim* attested in multiple
contexts) and specific cultic practices that the surviving
inscriptional record documents across the network. The cult
functioned, in Bonnet's
[*Les enfants de Cadmos*](/sources/bonnet-cadmos-2015)
framing, as substantive coordinating institution.

## Other coordinating mechanisms

Beyond the Melqart cult, several other mechanisms operated as
network coordination:

**Shared script and language.** The Phoenician alphabet — the
22-character consonantal script — was used across the network
with regional dialectal variation but with substantive mutual
intelligibility. The
[Punic-language inscriptional record](/themes/punic-inscriptional-record)
documents this shared linguistic infrastructure across centuries
of network operation.

**Commercial conventions.** Weight standards (the silver shekel
of approximately 7.5 grams as the network's principal currency
unit, with regional variations), measurement systems, contract
forms, and commercial vocabularies were shared across the
network. The fragmentary commercial evidence supports
substantive standardization rather than independent local
practice.

**Elite intermarriage and political coordination.** The
homeland-colony marriage patterns are attested in the surviving
record (most explicitly in the foundation-legend traditions but
also in scattered prosopographic evidence); inter-colonial elite
recognition was substantive enough that elite mobility across
the network is firmly attested.

**Astarte and Eshmun cults as secondary coordinating
infrastructure.** Where Melqart was the principal network
coordinating cult, the Astarte cult (homeland centered on
Sidon) and the Eshmun cult (also Sidon-centered) provided
additional shared religious-cultic infrastructure. The two
deities' cults across the network are documented in the
inscriptional and archaeological evidence.

The cumulative picture is of a network with multiple
substantive coordinating mechanisms — religious, commercial,
linguistic, social — operating in mutually reinforcing ways
across centuries.

## The Assyrian crisis and the network's resilience

The homeland's situation across the 8th and 7th centuries BCE
was constrained by increasing Assyrian imperial pressure. Tyre
was besieged by Shalmaneser V in the 720s, by Sennacherib in
the 700s, by Esarhaddon in the 670s, by Ashurbanipal in the
660s. Each siege produced tribute and political constraint;
the cumulative effect across the period was substantial
diminution of Tyrian autonomy in commerce and politics. Sidon
was destroyed by Esarhaddon in 677 BCE in a particularly
severe episode; the broader homeland's political-commercial
position was substantively constrained.

What is striking in the surviving record is that the network
*did not collapse* during this period. The western colonies
continued operating; the commercial network continued
functioning; the Melqart cult's coordinating practices
continued. The homeland's constraint affected the metropolitan
center but the network's substantive operations had become
sufficiently distributed that the system survived without it
operating at full capacity.

Carthage's particular trajectory across this period is part
of the network's resilience pattern. The conventional
foundation date (814 BCE) places Carthage's founding before
the Assyrian crisis reaches its severe phase; the
archaeologically documentable 8th-c. occupation horizons at
Carthage are consistent with foundation in this period. Across
the 7th c. BCE — exactly as the homeland's political-commercial
position was most constrained — Carthage's demographic and
commercial scale grew substantially. By the 6th c. BCE Carthage
had emerged as the western network's principal commercial-
political center, taking over substantial elements of the
coordinating role the homeland had previously played.

This was not a sudden shift but a structural transformation
across a century or more: the network's center of gravity
migrated westward as the eastern center's position weakened,
producing by the late 6th c. BCE a network with Carthage as
its dominant western node and the homeland as one substantive
center among multiple. The Melqart cult's institutional
practices — including the annual delegations — were maintained
through this transition, with Carthage and the other western
nodes continuing to send delegations to a Tyrian sanctuary
whose metropolitan-religious centrality was preserved even as
its political-economic centrality declined.

## Carthaginian consolidation (6th-4th centuries BCE)

From the 6th c. BCE forward, Carthage's position in the network
transformed from "important western node" to "metropolitan
center of the western network." The transformation is
documented in several lines of evidence:

**Military leadership.** The Carthaginian state's leadership of
western Phoenician military operations — against the Greek
expansion into Sicily and the western Mediterranean, against
the Etruscans at sites like the [Battle of Alalia](/events/battle-of-alalia)
in 537 BCE — established Carthage as the network's principal
military-strategic actor. Other western Phoenician
foundations contributed to these operations but Carthage
coordinated them.

**Religious-cultic centrality.** The growing prominence of the
Carthaginian Tophet and the Carthaginian Tanit cult through
the 6th-5th centuries BCE made Carthage substantively central
to the network's evolving religious infrastructure, in
addition to the existing Tyrian Melqart centrality. Tanit
itself emerges as a substantive western network deity from
the 5th c. BCE forward, with the Tanit cult diffused to the
other major western nodes (Motya, Sulci, Tharros) under
substantively Carthaginian initiative.

**Commercial coordination.** Carthaginian commercial agreements
with Rome (the first Carthaginian-Roman treaty of c. 509 BCE)
and with other Mediterranean powers were substantively
negotiated on behalf of the broader western Phoenician network
rather than just Carthage itself. Carthage became the network's
diplomatic-commercial spokesperson.

**Inscriptional and administrative diffusion.** Carthaginian
administrative practices, magistracy titles (the
[suffeteship](/institutions/suffetes) attested across the
western network), and inscriptional conventions diffused from
Carthage to the other western nodes through the 6th-5th
centuries BCE — the reverse of the earlier homeland-to-colonies
diffusion pattern.

By the 4th century BCE the network had substantively become
"Punic civilization" with Carthage as its metropolitan core
and the other foundations as its periphery — the same
network, reorganized around a new center, the original node
grown larger than the system that produced it.

## The transformation to Hellenistic-Punic

From the 5th c. BCE forward, the Carthaginian-led network
substantively engaged with the broader Hellenistic
Mediterranean cultural system. Greek-style coinage, Hellenistic
court forms at the Carthaginian aristocratic level, the 396
BCE [Demeter-Kore adoption](/events/demeter-kore-adoption)
as state cult, the Hellenistic-pattern Barcid state-building
in Iberia of the 3rd c. BCE — all are features of the network
transitioning from substantively Phoenician to substantively
Hellenistic-Punic in its institutional and cultural forms
while maintaining its Phoenician core. This is where the
network arc connects to the Hellenistic-Carthage question that
the site treats separately (and that the surviving evidence
documents in substantial detail).

The transformation was not displacement of Phoenician identity
by Hellenistic forms but absorption of Hellenistic forms into
a Phoenician institutional core that continued operating. The
Melqart cult continued; the annual Tyrian delegation continued;
the Punic language and script continued; the
[inscriptional formulae](/themes/punic-inscriptional-record)
continued. What changed was the supplementary cultural
vocabulary the network operated alongside its Phoenician core.

## Legends and what they preserve

The conventional foundation tradition treats Carthage as founded
by Dido in 814 BCE in a specific narrative of escape from Tyrian
political conflict. The Gadir foundation tradition places its
founding at c. 1100 BCE in the Heraclean tradition of the
Tyrian Hercules's western journey. Various other foundations
have analogous foundation-legend traditions.

The site treats these as legendary tradition with historicizable
kernels — there was a Phoenician foundation at Carthage in
approximately the late 9th c. BCE; there was a Phoenician
foundation at Gadir in approximately the late 9th c. BCE
(despite the conventional date being centuries earlier); there
were Phoenician foundations at the other major sites in
approximately datable periods. The specific narrative content
of the legends (Dido's escape, the precise circumstances of
individual foundations, the named founders) is legendary
elaboration rather than direct attestation.

What the legends preserve historically is the *colonial
framework* of the foundations: that they were undertaken from
the Phoenician homeland, that they were associated with specific
political or commercial circumstances, that they were
understood by the participants as substantively Phoenician
ventures, that the network's religious-cultic infrastructure
(the Melqart association, the Tyrian metropolitan relationship)
was constitutive rather than incidental. The kernel framework
is consistent with the archaeological record; the specific
narrative content is the legendary tradition's contribution.

The site's
[foundation narrative](/narratives/founding-of-carthage)
treats the Carthage-specific legendary tradition in detail
with explicit confidence-tagging on what's attested versus
what's legendary. This theme's treatment of the network as
a system is the broader structural framework within which
individual foundation traditions sit.

## What the network was, and what it was not

The Phoenician colonial network was a substantive integrated
system across the 9th through 4th centuries BCE — religious,
commercial, linguistic, and to a meaningful degree political.
It was not a centralized empire under Tyrian rule; the
homeland's authority over the colonies was substantive in
religious-cultic terms (the Melqart cult's metropolitan
centrality) but loose in political-administrative terms
(the colonies operated with substantial autonomy). It was
not a free trade zone with no shared institutions; the
substantive coordinating mechanisms documented in the
surviving record argue against this minimalist reading. It
was not a uniform civilization across geographic dispersion;
regional adaptations of religious, linguistic, and
commercial practice are documented across the network.

What it was: a substantively integrated maritime-commercial-
religious-cultural system with multiple coordinating
mechanisms operating in mutually reinforcing ways, with
Tyrian metropolitan centrality across the early centuries
and Carthaginian metropolitan centrality across the later
centuries, with substantial regional variation in the
periphery but substantive coherence in the core institutional
practices. The network's eventual transformation into "Punic
civilization with Carthage at the center" is one phase of a
longer integrated cultural arc that began with the 9th c.
Tyrian expansion and continued through the Roman period and
beyond, treated in this theme's structural-system framing and
in the [continuity narrative](/narratives/punic-continuity-in-roman-africa)
for the network's post-146 BCE persistence.

The framing the theme takes is therefore both bookends of
the conventional Carthage story. The story did not begin in
814 BCE with Dido's foundation — it began earlier and more
gradually, in a network Carthage was one node among many of.
The story did not end in 146 BCE with the destruction — it
continued, in transformed form, through the persistence of
the Punic cultural arc into late antiquity. Both bookends
push past Greco-Roman closure-and-opening framings to reveal
the longer and more substantively coherent cultural arc
that Carthage was one phase of.
