## The script

The Phoenician alphabet is a consonantal writing system of
twenty-two characters, developed in the Levantine cities by
approximately the 11th century BCE and adopted at Carthage as
part of the Tyrian colonial inheritance. The script writes from
right to left, represents only consonants (vowels are not
distinguished, though some final vowels are sometimes marked by
the letters *aleph*, *waw*, and *yod* used as *matres lectionis*),
and shows minor regional and chronological variation across the
Phoenician-Punic world.

Punic-script inscriptions on the site appear in the Phoenician
Unicode block (U+10900 to U+1091F), rendered with the Noto Sans
Phoenician font where available. The Carthaginian name in
Phoenician script is 𐤒𐤓𐤕𐤇𐤃𐤔𐤕 (*Qart-Hadasht*, "New City").
Other recurring Punic names on the site include 𐤇𐤍𐤁𐤏𐤋
(*Ḥannibaʿl*, "grace of Baʿal"), 𐤏𐤁𐤃𐤌𐤋𐤒𐤓𐤕 (*ʿAbd-Melqart*,
"servant of Melqart," rendered Hamilcar in Greek and Latin),
and 𐤁𐤓𐤒 (*Brq*, "lightning," the Barca family epithet).

The script's broader historical significance is large.
Phoenician was adapted by the Greeks (probably in the 9th or 8th
century BCE) into the Greek alphabet, which added vowel
characters and was subsequently adapted into Latin, Cyrillic,
and the broader family of European alphabetic writing systems.
The alphabetic principle that distinguishes these scripts from
the syllabic and logographic writing systems they replaced (or
coexisted with) traces directly to the Phoenician inheritance.

## The language

Punic is the western, post-colonial development of the
Phoenician language, itself a member of the Northwest Semitic
language family alongside Hebrew, Aramaic, and Ugaritic. The
language was spoken at Carthage from the late 8th century BCE
through the city's destruction in 146 BCE and continued in use
in North Africa long after, with the late form sometimes called
*Neo-Punic* attested in inscriptions through approximately the
3rd century CE. Augustine, writing as a North African in the
late 4th and early 5th century CE, describes Punic as still
spoken in his day in rural Numidia.

Punic shows the standard Northwest Semitic morphological
features: tri-consonantal roots, prefix and suffix conjugations
of the verb, construct chains, definite-article prefixation. Its
vocabulary is largely shared with Phoenician proper but with
local lexical developments. Loanwords from Berber and Greek are
attested, particularly in the post-classical Neo-Punic stage.

## The corpus

Approximately 6,000 Phoenician and Punic inscriptions are
preserved across the Mediterranean basin. The principal
Carthaginian corpus comes from:

- **The Tophet stelae.** Several thousand votive stelae from the
  precinct in southern Carthage, mostly brief dedicatory
  inscriptions in formulaic language. The single largest
  Punic-language corpus from any site.

- **Tomb inscriptions.** Funerary inscriptions from the Douïmès
  and other Carthaginian cemeteries, sometimes brief (just a
  name) and sometimes extended.

- **Civic and architectural inscriptions.** Building dedications,
  cultic dedications, occasional lists of magistrates and
  priests.

- **Ostraca and graffiti.** Brief everyday inscriptions on
  potsherds and walls.

The major reference editions are the *Corpus Inscriptionum
Semiticarum* (CIS, the principal 19th and early-20th-century
edition) and Donner and Röllig's *Kanaanäische und aramäische
Inschriften* (KAI, the standard modern reference).

## What the inscriptions tell us

The corpus is large but linguistically narrow. Most surviving
inscriptions are formulaic dedications or brief tomb texts. The
language as preserved is therefore better-attested in its
formulaic registers than in its everyday or literary use. We
have very little Punic narrative prose, no Punic literature in
the sense of preserved-on-its-own-terms texts (the only major
exception is the *Periplus of Hanno*, preserved through Greek
translation), and no Punic philosophical, scientific, or
religious writing of the kind the comparable Greek and Hebrew
records preserve.

The result is that we know Punic well enough to read what
survives but cannot reconstruct the full range of the language's
expressive use. What Carthaginian aristocrats said to each other
in Punic, what was sung at festivals, what was written on
perishable material such as papyrus and leather: none of this is
recoverable. The 6,000 inscriptions are the upper bound of what
the language's preserved record will allow.

## Reading Punic on the site

Where Phoenician script is included on entity pages (people,
places, deities, institutions), the script is the actual
attested form where preserved or a careful reconstruction
following standard transliteration practice where not. The
transliteration system used follows the conventions of modern
Phoenician-Punic philological scholarship: ʾ, ʿ, ḥ, ṭ, ṣ, š, etc.
for the Semitic consonants that lack direct English equivalents.

Reader unfamiliarity with the script is expected and is part of
why the site uses Punic script alongside Latinized name forms
rather than as a primary display. The Phoenician script in name
fields is for orientation and respect for the original; the
Latinized name (Hannibal, Carthage, Hamilcar) is the primary
display the encyclopedia uses for navigation.

## Material evidence

The script's history and decipherment depend on a handful of
foundational inscriptions. The Ahiram Sarcophagus, dated to
approximately 1000 BCE, bears the earliest substantial fully-developed
Phoenician-alphabet text anywhere — the foundational physical
document of the script Carthage inherited from its Tyrian
metropolitan origin. The Nora Stone from southern Sardinia,
inscribed in the late 9th or early 8th century BCE, is the earliest
Phoenician inscription anywhere outside the Levantine homeland —
direct evidence of the script's early western-Mediterranean reach
that predates Carthage's conventional foundation date.

Bilingual inscriptions anchor the modern decipherment apparatus.
The Cippi of Melqart, recovered from Malta and entered into European
scholarship in the 18th century, supplied Jean-Jacques Barthélemy
with the parallel Punic-Greek text he used to break the Punic
alphabet open in the 1750s. The Karatepe Bilingual provides the
Phoenician-Luwian-hieroglyph parallel that allowed decipherment of
the Anatolian hieroglyphic script. The Idalion Bilingual gave
George Smith the Phoenician-Cypriot-syllabary parallel that broke
the Cypriot syllabary in 1871. The Libyco-Punic Mausoleum of Dougga
preserves the Libyan-Punic bilingual that enabled the 19th-century
decipherment of the Libyan (Berber) alphabet.

The Tabnit Sarcophagus and the Eshmunazar II Sarcophagus, both
late-6th-century BCE Sidonian royal texts, are among the longest
continuous Phoenician inscriptions; their curse-formula and royal-
titulary vocabulary established conventions that subsequent
Phoenician-Punic inscriptions inherit.

Of the lost Punic-language literature, the Hanno's Periplus
Inscription is the principal partial survival — the Punic
original is lost, but the Greek translation preserved through the
medieval manuscript tradition stands as the closest thing we have
to a substantive specimen of Punic narrative literature, and as a
methodological exemplar for how Punic writing reaches modern
scholarship at all.

## Sources

The principal modern reference works on Punic language are
Krahmalkov's *A Phoenician-Punic Grammar* (Brill, 2001) and
Friedrich and Röllig's earlier *Phönizisch-Punische Grammatik*
(3rd ed., 1999). Sznycer's chapter in Nicolet 1978 provides a
useful French-language synthesis. For the inscriptional corpus,
CIS and KAI are the standard editions.
