Carthage ran a working diplomatic apparatus that produced
multiple treaties across the city's history. The Greco-Roman
tradition preserves the Roman-Carthaginian treaties in
substance; the agreements with other polities (the Numidian
kingdoms, the Greek Sicilian cities, the Etruscan powers, the
Hellenistic eastern states) survive only in fragments. The
Roman-Carthaginian sequence is therefore the documented
backbone, with the Roman-perspective bias the surviving
sources inherit.

## The eight Roman-Carthaginian treaties

The relationship with Rome is documented through eight
treaties across the fifth through second centuries BCE.
Polybius (3.22–27) preserves the pre-264 BCE treaties; Livy
and others document the later ones.

509 BCE: First Treaty. The
[first Roman-Carthaginian treaty](/events/first-treaty-with-rome)
set up the basic commercial-jurisdictional framework: Rome's
commercial activity restricted to coastal Italy, Carthage's
restricted to Africa, Sicily, and Sardinia, with specific
provisions on which harbors each side could use. The date is
contested in some modern readings, covered under
[the open question on pre-264 treaties](/openQuestions/fpw-pre-264-treaties).

348 BCE: Second Treaty. The
[second treaty](/events/second-treaty-with-rome) renewed and
extended the 509 framework with tighter commercial
restrictions. Polybius preserves the content; the dating is
generally accepted.

306 BCE: Third Treaty (Philinus). Philinus of Akragas, a
third-century BCE pro-Carthaginian historian whose work
survives only through Polybius's polemical engagement with
it, refers to a third treaty that supposedly restricted Roman
operations in Sicily. If genuine, the 264 BCE intervention at
Messana would have been a treaty violation. Polybius rejects
the treaty's existence. The question is treated under the
[Philinus treaty controversy](/events/philinus-treaty-controversy)
and the
[first-treaty-trust-Polybius editorial take](/editorialTakes/first-treaty-trust-polybius).

279 BCE: Pyrrhic War Treaty. The
[Pyrrhic War treaty](/events/pyrrhic-war-treaty) set up
Roman-Carthaginian cooperation during Pyrrhus's western
campaign. The cooperation was naval: Carthaginian fleet
support for Roman operations in Italy in exchange for Roman
fleet support for Carthaginian operations in Sicily. The
treaty was the high point of pre-FPW Roman-Carthaginian
cooperative diplomacy.

241 BCE: Treaty of Lutatius. The
[Treaty of Lutatius](/events/treaty-of-lutatius) ended the
First Punic War. Carthage gave up Sicily and the surrounding
islands and accepted an indemnity. Polybius preserves the
terms in detail. The post-treaty Roman seizure of Sardinia
in 238 BCE went well beyond what the original agreement had
required, and the violation is one of the long-running
grievances behind Barcid orientation toward Rome.

226 BCE: Ebro Treaty. The
[Ebro Treaty](/events/ebro-treaty) between Rome and the
Carthaginian Iberian state, under
[Hasdrubal the Fair](/people/hasdrubal-the-fair), set the
Ebro river as the boundary of Carthaginian operations in
Iberia. How it relates to the subsequent Saguntum crisis is
one of the central questions about the SPW outbreak.
Saguntum can be read as a Carthaginian violation, as a Roman
provocation through extension of *socii* protection, or as
some combination of the two.

218 BCE: Declaration of war. The
[218 BCE Roman declaration of war](/events/roman-declaration-of-war-218)
was delivered by the Roman embassy at Carthage in the famous
"toga episode": the spokesman folded his toga and offered
war or peace, and the Carthaginian senate replied, "shake
out whichever pleases you." That ended the diplomatic phase
of the SPW outbreak. The pre-war sequence (Saguntum, the
Roman embassies, the Carthaginian responses) is treated
under the
[Hannibal-as-integrated-Punic-Hellenistic-figure framing](/editorialTakes/carthage-as-cultural-integrator).

201 BCE: Treaty of 201. The
[Treaty of 201](/events/treaty-of-201-bce) ended the Second
Punic War. The terms (a 10,000-talent indemnity over fifty
years, the navy reduced to ten ships, no warfare without
Roman authorization, recognition of Numidian claims under
Masinissa) constrained Carthage for the rest of its
existence. The treaty's significance is treated under
[period 07](/periods/07-the-long-peace) and the
[destruction-not-weak-enough editorial take](/editorialTakes/destruction-not-weak-enough).

## Diplomacy outside the Roman relationship

Carthage maintained treaty relations with multiple non-Roman
polities, though the record is much patchier than for Rome.

Syracuse and the Sicilian Greek cities. The principal
non-Roman polity Carthage dealt with diplomatically across
the Sicilian Wars (fifth through third centuries BCE). The
treaty-and-warfare cycle with Syracuse under
[Dionysius I](/people/dionysius-i),
[Agathocles](/people/agathocles), and
[Hieron II](/people/hieron-ii) produced multiple agreements,
though the specific texts do not survive.

Numidian kingdoms. The relationships with the
[Massylii](/groups/massylii) and
[Masaesyli](/groups/masaesyli) ran on dynastic marriage and
on tribute-and-cavalry arrangements that are documented at
specific moments. The
[Numidian-Punic interface theme](/themes/punic-numidian-relations)
covers the broader framing.

Hellenistic east. Carthage maintained working diplomatic
relations with Macedon (the
[treaty with Philip V](/events/treaty-with-philip-v) of 215
BCE during the SPW), with the Seleucid empire (Hannibal's
exile contacts after 195 BCE), and with Ptolemaic Egypt
(commercial and political). The eastern relationships
appear in Greek-source references rather than as preserved
treaty texts.

Etruscan powers. The Carthaginian-Etruscan alliance produced
the victory over the Phocaean Greeks at
[Alalia](/events/battle-of-alalia) around 535 BCE. The
[Pyrgi Tablets](/artifacts/pyrgi-tablets), bilingual
Etruscan-Punic inscriptions, survive as direct material
evidence of the relationship, even though the broader
treaty framework does not.

## How Carthage actually negotiated

The Carthaginian apparatus worked through formal senatorial
embassies appointed by the
[Council of 104](/institutions/council-of-104) and the
broader senate, with substantive authority to negotiate and
to ratify. The institutional capacity to produce coherent
diplomatic positions, negotiate them through formal
embassies, and ratify outcomes through senate process is
part of what Aristotle praised in *Politics* II.11.

After 201, the diplomatic pattern shifted to repeated
Carthaginian appeals to Rome for arbitration of Numidian
encroachments. The dynamic is treated under the
[Masinissa-as-strategic-opportunist editorial take](/editorialTakes/masinissa-strategic-opportunist).
The pattern constrained Carthage's foreign-policy
independence and helped produce the configuration that led
to the Third Punic War.

## Sources

The principal sources are Polybius (especially Book 3 for
the Roman-Carthaginian treaties) and Livy (for the SPW
diplomatic sequence). Modern treatments include
[Hoyos's *The Carthaginians* (2010)](/sources/hoyos-carthaginians-2010)
for the diplomatic apparatus generally, and Sanmartín's
specialist work on Carthaginian treaty practice. The
[treaty-system reading thread](/threads/treaty-system) walks
the Roman-Carthaginian sequence in chronological order.
