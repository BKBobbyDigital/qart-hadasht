The Carthaginian state operated a sophisticated diplomatic
apparatus that produced multiple documented treaty arrangements
across the city's history. The Greco-Roman tradition preserves
substantively only the Roman-Carthaginian treaties — the
treaties with other polities (Numidian kingdoms, Greek
Sicilian cities, Etruscan powers, Hellenistic eastern states)
exist only fragmentarily in the surviving record. The Roman-
Carthaginian treaty sequence is therefore the principal
documented evidence base, with the bias toward Roman-perspective
framing the surviving evidence inherits.

## The eight Roman-Carthaginian treaties

The Roman-Carthaginian relationship is documented through eight
distinct treaties across the 5th through the 2nd centuries BCE.
Polybius (3.22-27) preserves the substantive content of the
pre-264 BCE treaties; Livy and other sources document the
later treaties.

**509 BCE — First Treaty.** The
[first Roman-Carthaginian treaty](/events/first-treaty-with-rome)
established the basic commercial-jurisdictional framework: a
zone of Roman commercial activity restricted to coastal Italy
and a zone of Carthaginian commercial activity restricted to
Africa, Sicily, and Sardinia, with specific provisions for
mutual recognition of certain harbors. The treaty's date is
contested in some modern readings — covered under
[the open question on FPW pre-264 treaties](/openQuestions/fpw-pre-264-treaties).

**348 BCE — Second Treaty.** The
[second treaty](/events/second-treaty-with-rome) renewed and
extended the 509 framework, with substantively tightened
commercial-restriction provisions. Polybius preserves the
treaty's content; the dating is generally accepted.

**306 BCE — Third Treaty.** The
third (Philinus) treaty is referenced by
Philinus of Akragas (a 3rd-century BCE pro-Carthaginian
historian whose account of the FPW outbreak is partially
preserved through Polybius's polemical engagement with it). The
treaty allegedly contained a provision restricting Roman
operations in Sicily, which would have made the 264 BCE
intervention at Messana a treaty violation. Polybius rejects
the existence of this treaty; the question is treated under
the [Philinus treaty controversy](/events/philinus-treaty-controversy)
event and the
[first-treaty-trust-Polybius editorial take](/editorialTakes/first-treaty-trust-polybius).

**279 BCE — Pyrrhic War Treaty.** The
[Pyrrhic War treaty](/events/pyrrhic-war-treaty) established
specific Roman-Carthaginian cooperation during Pyrrhus of
Epirus's western Mediterranean campaign. The cooperation was
substantively naval — Carthaginian fleet support for Roman
operations in Italy in exchange for Roman fleet support for
Carthaginian operations in Sicily. The treaty represents the
high-water mark of Roman-Carthaginian cooperative diplomacy
before the FPW.

**241 BCE — Treaty of Lutatius.** The
[Treaty of Lutatius](/events/treaty-of-lutatius) ended the
First Punic War, with substantial Carthaginian territorial
concessions (loss of Sicily and the surrounding islands) and
indemnity payments. The treaty's terms are preserved in
Polybius with substantive detail; the post-treaty modifications
(Roman seizure of Sardinia in 238 BCE) substantially worsened
the Carthaginian position beyond what the original treaty
required.

**226 BCE — Ebro Treaty.** The
[Ebro Treaty](/events/ebro-treaty) between Rome and the
Carthaginian Iberian state (under
[Hasdrubal the Fair](/people/hasdrubal-the-fair)) established
the Ebro river as the boundary of Carthaginian operations in
Iberia. The treaty's relationship to the subsequent Saguntum
crisis is one of the load-bearing questions of the SPW outbreak
— the Saguntum crisis is interpretable as Carthaginian treaty
violation, Roman provocation through socii-protection
extension, or strategic ambivalence between the two readings.

**218 BCE — Declaration of war.** The
[218 BCE Roman declaration of war](/events/roman-declaration-of-war-218),
delivered by the Roman embassy at Carthage with the famous
"toga episode" (the embassy's spokesman folding his toga to
offer war or peace, with the Carthaginian senate's response of
"shake out whichever pleases you"), formally ended the
diplomatic phase of the SPW outbreak. The pre-war diplomatic
sequence — Saguntum crisis, Roman embassies, Carthaginian
responses — is treated under the
[Hannibal as integrated Punic-Hellenistic figure framing](/editorialTakes/carthage-as-cultural-integrator).

**201 BCE — Treaty of 201.** The
[Treaty of 201](/events/treaty-of-201-bce) ended the Second
Punic War. The terms — 10,000-talent indemnity over 50 years,
naval-fleet reduction to 10 warships, prohibition on
Carthaginian warfare without Roman authorization,
recognition of Numidian territorial claims under
Masinissa — substantively constrained the Carthaginian state
for the remainder of its existence. The treaty's structural
significance is treated under
[period 07](/periods/07-the-long-peace) and the broader
[destruction-not-weak-enough editorial take](/editorialTakes/destruction-not-weak-enough).

## Diplomatic practice beyond the Roman relationship

Carthage maintained diplomatic-treaty relations with multiple
non-Roman polities across its history, though the documentary
record is substantially less complete than for the Roman
relationship.

**Syracuse and the Sicilian Greek cities** were the principal
non-Roman polity Carthage operated with diplomatically across
the Sicilian Wars period (5th–3rd centuries BCE). The
treaty-warfare cycle with Syracuse under
[Dionysius I](/people/dionysius-i),
[Agathocles](/people/agathocles), and
[Hieron II](/people/hieron-ii) produced multiple documented
treaty arrangements, though the specific texts are not
preserved.

**Numidian kingdoms** — the relationships with the
[Massylii](/groups/massylii) and
[Masaesyli](/groups/masaesyli) Numidian confederations — were
substantively diplomatic-political across the Punic Wars
period, with dynastic-marriage networks and tribute-and-cavalry
arrangements documented through specific moments. The
[Numidian-Punic interface theme](/themes/punic-numidian-relations)
covers the broader framing.

**Hellenistic eastern Mediterranean states.** Carthage
maintained substantive diplomatic relationships with the
Hellenistic kingdoms — Macedon (the
[treaty with Philip V](/events/treaty-with-philip-v) of 215
BCE during the SPW), the Seleucid empire (Hannibal's exile
relationships post-195 BCE), and Egypt (Ptolemaic-Carthaginian
commercial-political contacts). The eastern relationships are
documented through Greek-source references rather than through
preserved treaty texts.

**Etruscan powers** — Carthaginian-Etruscan alliance produced
the famous victory over the Phocaean Greeks at
[Alalia](/events/battle-of-alalia) c. 535 BCE. The
Carthaginian-Etruscan relationship is documented through the
Alalia event and the famous
[Pyrgi Tablets](/artifacts/pyrgi-tablets) bilingual Etruscan-
Punic inscriptions, but the broader treaty-diplomatic
framework is not directly preserved.

## Diplomatic style and institutional apparatus

The Carthaginian diplomatic apparatus operated through formal
senatorial sub-delegations — embassies appointed by the
[Council of 104](/institutions/council-of-104) and broader
senate, with substantive negotiating authority and treaty-
ratification capacity. The Carthaginian state's diplomatic
flexibility is one of the substantive features of the
political tradition Aristotle praised in the *Politics*: the
institutional capacity to produce coherent diplomatic positions,
negotiate them through formal embassies, and ratify outcomes
through senatorial process.

The post-201 BCE diplomatic-arbitration pattern — Carthaginian
appeals to Rome for arbitration of Numidian encroachments,
treated under the [Masinissa as strategic opportunist editorial take](/editorialTakes/masinissa-strategic-opportunist) —
substantively constrained Carthaginian foreign-policy
independence and produced the strategic configuration that
generated the Third Punic War.

## Sources

The principal sources for Carthaginian diplomacy are Polybius
(particularly Book 3 for the Roman-Carthaginian treaties) and
Livy (for the SPW diplomatic sequence). Modern treatments
include [Hoyos's *The Carthaginians* (2010)](/sources/hoyos-carthaginians-2010)
for the broader diplomatic apparatus and
Sanmartín's specialist work on Carthaginian treaty practice.
The [treaty-system reading thread](/threads/treaty-system) walks
the Roman-Carthaginian treaty sequence in chronological order
as a curated reading path.
