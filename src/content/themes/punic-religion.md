## The pantheon

Carthaginian religion descended from the Phoenician tradition of Tyre. Its
gods were continuous with those of the Levantine Phoenician cities, but the
relative weight of individual deities at Carthage diverged from the Tyrian
pattern. Four gods dominate the surviving Carthaginian record; many more are
attested in inscriptions and material culture. Each deity below has a
structured entry under [Deities](/deities) with full *interpretatio*,
attestation, etymology, and source citations.

**Baal Hammon** (𐤁𐤏𐤋 𐤇𐤌𐤍, *Baʿal Ḥamon*) was the chief male deity of
Carthage from at least the 7th century BCE. The name combines *Baʿal*
("lord") with an element variously read as "the brazier," "the hot one," or
toponymic. Greco-Roman sources identify him with Kronos and Saturn, an
identification with consequences for how the Tophet evidence has been read.
His title and image appear on stelae from the Tophet precinct in greater
numbers than any other deity. Whether he ranked above or alongside the other
principal Carthaginian deities is debated; the consensus is that he was
preeminent in the early Carthaginian period and shared that status with
Tanit from approximately the 5th century BCE onward.

**Tanit** (𐤕𐤍𐤕, *Tinnit* in Punic), styled "face of Baal" (*Pene Baʿal*) in
inscriptions, rose to formal prominence in the Carthaginian pantheon during
the 5th century BCE and from then on appears in dedications above Baal
Hammon as often as below him. Her iconographic sign, the so-called "sign of
Tanit," a stylized human figure of triangle, horizontal bar, and disc, is
the most widely recognized Carthaginian religious symbol and appears on
stelae, mosaics, coins, and personal ornaments. Greco-Roman sources
identify her with Astarte, Hera, or Juno Caelestis. The relative ascent of
Tanit at Carthage is one of the few features of Punic religion clearly
visible in the inscriptional record over time.

**Melqart** (𐤌𐤋𐤒𐤓𐤕, "king of the city"), patron deity of Tyre, retained a
lasting place in Carthaginian religious life as the link to the Phoenician
homeland. Annual delegations from Carthage to Tyre, attested across
centuries, made offerings at the Melqart sanctuary there. Greco-Roman
sources identify him with Heracles, an identification that placed him in
the religious imagination of the wider Mediterranean. Hannibal's pre-war
oath, sworn at his father's altar, is generally understood to have been
sworn at an altar of Melqart.

**Eshmun** (𐤀𐤔𐤌𐤍), a healing deity of Sidonian origin identified by the
Greeks with Asklepios, was honored at Carthage with a major sanctuary on
the Byrsa hill and (in the surviving Greek tradition) treated as
particularly important to the city's medical and ritual culture. Less
prominent in dedications than Baal Hammon and Tanit but securely attested
across the Carthaginian period.

Beyond these four, the Carthaginian pantheon included Reshef, Astarte (in
older inscriptions, before her partial assimilation to Tanit), Sid, and a
range of more localized deities visible in inscriptions but rarely in the
literary record.

## What the inscriptions tell us

The single richest source for Carthaginian religion is the corpus of
several thousand votive stelae from the Tophet precinct, dating from the
late 8th century BCE through 146 BCE. The stelae are dedicatory: they
record offerings, name the dedicator and (often) a parent or grandparent,
and identify the deity to whom the offering was made. The formulae are
formulaic, but the cumulative evidence has allowed scholars to trace
changes over time in the relative prominence of individual deities, in the
proportion of offerings made jointly to Baal Hammon and Tanit (rising over
time), and in the social composition of the dedicating families.

Beyond the Tophet, dedicatory inscriptions on temple precincts, cippi, and
altars give us additional priestly titles, festival names, and ritual
vocabulary. The corpus is fragmentary but is the closest thing we have to
direct Carthaginian testimony about Carthaginian religious practice.

What the inscriptions do not give us is theology. Carthaginian religion
left no surviving prayer book, no narrative of the gods, no equivalent of
the Greek mythological tradition. We have ritual practice and personal
piety; we do not have systematic religious thought.

## What the literary record tells us, and how to read it

Greco-Roman sources discuss Carthaginian religion at length and almost
always polemically. Diodorus's account of the Carthaginian sacrificial
panic during the Agathocles invasion (Diodorus 20.14), Plutarch's
discussion of child sacrifice in *De Superstitione*, and Tertullian's
later Christian polemic all preserve information about Carthaginian
ritual through hostile framing. The information is real; the moral
coloring is the writer's.

The most contested and consequential of these reports concerns the
practice known to the Greeks as *molk* (𐤌𐤋𐤊, *mlk*) sacrifice and
specifically the question of whether children were sacrificed at the
Tophet. That question is treated in its own right under
[The Tophet question](/themes/the-tophet-question).

## What survives, and what does not

We have:

- The pantheon, in broad outline, attested across inscriptions and
  literary sources.
- The Tophet precinct itself, excavated and stratigraphically dated.
- The ritual vocabulary of dedications: *mlk*, *neder* (vow), *bʿl ḥrd*
  (priestly title).
- Iconography: the sign of Tanit, the symbols of Baal Hammon, the
  cippus form, the betyl.
- The annual delegation to Tyre and the persistence of Melqart's
  importance.

We do not have, and largely cannot recover:

- A Carthaginian theology, in the sense of systematic doctrine.
- The actual content of prayers, hymns, and ritual recitation.
- The internal religious experience of Carthaginian worshippers.
- The full priestly hierarchy, in detail.
- The relationship between civic religion and private religious
  practice, which the inscriptions hint at but do not describe.

The asymmetry is structural. What survives is what was inscribed in
stone or rendered in ritual archaeology. What does not survive is what
was spoken, written on perishable material, or transmitted through
practice. The Carthaginian religious imagination is irrecoverable in
ways the religious imagination of literate-text-preserving cultures is
not.

## Material evidence

The artifact corpus underlying this theme is substantial. The Tophet
Stelae are the largest single body of Punic religious-textual evidence
anywhere — thousands of inscribed votive stones from the Carthage
Tophet, with dedications to Baal Hammon and Tanit, six centuries of
continuous use, and the iconographic and inscriptional vocabulary that
defines Punic state religion. The Sign of Tanit appears on the stelae,
on votive iconography across the diaspora, on Carthaginian state
coinage (the Carthage Tetradrachm of the 4th century BCE renders
Tanit's head as the obverse type), and in domestic-religious contexts
preserved at Kerkouane. Beyond the Carthage corpus, the El Hofra Stele
Cache from Cirta in the Numidian interior documents the persistence
of Punic religious practice across the post-146 BCE collapse; the
Tharros Tophet Stele represents the Sardinian diaspora's distinct
iconographic conventions.

For the metropolitan Phoenician background, the Eshmun Sanctuary
Votive Statue from Sidon attests to the healing-deity child-dedication
tradition that the Carthage Eshmun cult on Byrsa hill descended from.
The Cippi of Melqart, the 2nd-century BCE Punic-Greek bilinguals from
Malta, record private dedications to the Tyrian patron god whose
Carthaginian importance the Melqart-Heracles identification preserves.

Apotropaic and personal-religious practice surfaces in the
Phoenician/Punic Grinning Mask tradition and the Punic Glass Face
Bead corpus — small protective objects deposited in tombs that
extend Carthaginian religion beyond the state-sanctuary and Tophet
contexts into the domestic register.

## Sources

The principal modern syntheses are Lancel 1995, MacDonald 2015, and the
relevant chapters of Hoyos's *The Carthaginians* (2010). Krings's
*La civilisation phénicienne et punique* handbook provides the deepest
specialist coverage; Sznycer's chapter in Nicolet 1978 is essential for
the inscriptions. The major epigraphic corpora are the *CIS* (*Corpus
Inscriptionum Semiticarum*) and Donner & Röllig's *KAI*. For the wider
Phoenician background, Aubet 2001 is the standard reference.
