The standard Greco-Roman picture of Carthage's military, most
influentially in Polybius 6.52, is of a mercenary apparatus
inferior to Rome's citizen army. The picture is partly correct
(Carthage's army did rely heavily on hired and allied troops)
and partly misleading. It tells us as much about Polybius's
argument for Rome as it does about how Carthage's military
actually worked. The picture that emerges from the surviving
evidence is of a flexible, multi-component force that operated
effectively at scale across simultaneous theaters and that
produced the most successful single commander of the ancient
Mediterranean (Hannibal) before the war's fiscal and manpower
limits caught up with the system.

## Citizen, mercenary, and allied components

Three recruitment categories together produced Carthage's
field armies in the Punic Wars period.

Citizens. The citizen body in arms was small: perhaps four to
six thousand men at full mobilization during the Second Punic
War, drawn from the Carthaginian aristocratic and military
classes. The [Sacred Band](/groups/sacred-band), the elite
Carthaginian citizen infantry attested through the Sicilian
Wars and the Mercenary War, was the most visible expression
of the tradition. Officers across the broader army came
substantially from the citizen body. The aristocratic command
tradition that runs through Hamilcar, Hannibal, Hasdrubal,
Mago, and the wider Barcid command circle is a citizen-officer
class operating over a mercenary-and-allied troop base.

Mercenaries. The mercenary component was the main source of
infantry across Carthage's military history. The recruitment
pools included [Libya](/groups/libyans), the largest single
source and the supplier of the army's heavy infantry;
[Iberia](/places/iberia), supplying Celtiberian and Iberian
infantry plus the
[*gladius hispaniensis*](/groups/celtiberians); the
[Balearic Islands](/places/ebusus), supplying the famous
[slingers](/groups/balearic-slingers); and Gaul, especially
the [Cisalpine Gauls](/groups/cisalpine-gauls) Hannibal picked
up during the Italian campaign. Recruitment worked through
long-running contractual relationships with specific networks,
not through ad-hoc hiring. The
[Mercenary War of 241–238 BCE](/events/mercenary-war) is the
documented case of those contractual relationships breaking
down.

Allies. Allied contingents came from polities under
Carthaginian hegemony or in treaty with Carthage.
[Numidian](/groups/massylii) cavalry was the most important
of these, supplied through the long-term political and
marriage relationships the
[Numidian-Punic interface](/themes/punic-numidian-relations)
treats. Sicilian Punic-cultural cities supplied infantry and
naval contingents during the Sicilian Wars; the
[Tripolitanian emporia](/places/sabratha) supplied tribute and
troops.

## The cavalry tradition

Carthage's cavalry was the best in the ancient Mediterranean
during the Punic Wars: it carried the field at
[Trebia](/events/battle-of-trebia), at
[Cannae](/events/battle-of-cannae), and across the rest of
Hannibal's Italian campaign. The system rested on Numidian
recruitment networks. Numidian cavalry was the principal
Carthaginian field arm, attested across the whole Punic Wars
period in multiple theaters, and its effectiveness rested on
long-running political relationships rather than tactical
recruitment.

The cavalry's defection under
[Masinissa](/people/masinissa) before
[Zama](/events/battle-of-zama) in 202 BCE is what turned
Roman tactical competence into battlefield victory. The same
arm that had given Hannibal his decisive advantage at Cannae
gave Scipio his at Zama. The whole strategic configuration
of the war shifted on the change of side.

## War elephants

The war elephants are one of the more recognizable features
of Carthaginian armies in the popular imagination. The
reality is less central. Elephants were a tactical
experiment rather than a core institution, and their
effectiveness in the documented engagements was mixed.

The species was almost certainly the now-extinct North
African elephant (*Loxodonta africana pharaohensis*), much
smaller than the Indian elephants the Hellenistic kingdoms
used. The Carthaginian elephant corps operated across the
Sicilian Wars, the First Punic War, the Mercenary War, the
[Barcid Iberian campaigns](/narratives/the-barcid-iberian-state),
and Hannibal's Italian campaign (the Alps-crossing
elephants). Documented deployments include Bagradas (255
BCE, where the elephants broke Regulus's line), Trebia (218,
mixed results), and Zama (202, where Scipio's open-lane
formation effectively neutralized the charge).

Specific engagements are treated on their own event pages.
The pattern across the whole period is one of tactical
experiment that produced occasional decisive results but
never became a reliable institutional arm the way the
cavalry did.

## The navy

Carthage's navy was the most developed in the ancient
Mediterranean. The
[Carthaginian sea power as system narrative](/narratives/carthaginian-sea-power-as-system)
treats it as a working state institution in its own right.
The core features: the [cothon](/places/cothon) at Carthage
(paired commercial and military basins), standardized
shipbuilding documented through the
[Marsala Punic Warship](/artifacts/marsala-punic-warship)
and the [Egadi Islands bronze warship rams](/artifacts/egadi-bronze-ram),
the capacity to mobilize hundreds of quinqueremes (350 at
Cape Ecnomus in 256 BCE), and a centralized command structure
that operated independently of the army's recruitment system.

The navy was run as a state apparatus rather than as a
mercenary-and-allied institution. The contrast with the
army's recruitment model matters. The Carthaginian naval
defeats in the First Punic War reflect the difficulty of
rebuilding fleets after catastrophic storm and battle losses
rather than any systemic inferiority. [Adherbal's](/people/adherbal)
victory at [Drepana](/events/battle-of-drepana) is the
counter-evidence to the standard "Carthage couldn't fight at
sea" framing.

## The fiscal limits of the system

The system's long-run weakness was that it was expensive to
maintain. Mercenary armies require steady state revenue;
recruitment networks require continuous political tending.
The [10,000-talent indemnity](/events/treaty-of-201-bce) of
the Treaty of 201 BCE and the Carthaginian recovery across
the long peace are treated under
[period 07](/periods/07-the-long-peace). The structural point
is that the apparatus was substantially more expensive to
sustain than the Roman alternative, and the fiscal pressure
of two long wars contributed to the eventual outcome.

The Roman citizen-army model required different fiscal
infrastructure. Citizen-soldiers serve more cheaply than
mercenaries, and Rome could replace battlefield losses
through citizen levies faster than Carthage could
re-stabilize contract networks. The asymmetry between the
two models in fiscal cost and recovery speed was the Roman
advantage. The Carthaginian army was not inferior in tactical
or operational terms. It was simply more expensive to keep in
the field.

## Sources

The principal modern reference is
[Hoyos's *The Carthaginians* (2010)](/sources/hoyos-carthaginians-2010),
plus Hoyos's *Hannibal's Dynasty* (2003) for the Barcid
command tradition. The Hoyos-edited
[*Companion to the Punic Wars* (2011)](/sources/hoyos-companion-punic-wars-2011)
is the standard single-volume survey of where current
specialist debate stands on the wars and the military
institutions that fought them. On the elephants, Charles's
*Hannibal's Elephant Army* (2008) is the standard treatment.
On the navy, the
[Carthaginian sea power narrative](/narratives/carthaginian-sea-power-as-system)
treats the institution in detail. The
[Krings *Manuel de recherche*](/sources/krings-manuel-1995)
chapters on Carthaginian military institutions remain the
deepest specialist reference.
