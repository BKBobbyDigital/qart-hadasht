The standard Greco-Roman characterization of the Carthaginian
military — captured most influentially in Polybius (6.52) —
treats it as a fundamentally mercenary apparatus, structurally
inferior to the citizen-army Roman model. The characterization
is partly accurate (the Carthaginian army did substantially
depend on hired and allied troops) and partly misleading: the
characterization tells us about Polybius's Roman-perspective
framework more than about how the Carthaginian system actually
operated as a state-military institution. The honest picture
that emerges from the surviving evidence is of a flexible,
multi-component military apparatus that operated effectively at
scale across multiple simultaneous theaters and that produced
the most successful single military commander of the ancient
Mediterranean (Hannibal Barca) before the structural fiscal
and manpower limits of the system caught up with it.

## Citizen, mercenary, and allied components

The Carthaginian military operated through three distinct
recruitment categories that together produced the field armies
of the Punic Wars period.

**The citizen component** was small relative to the
population — perhaps 4,000–6,000 men at full mobilization in
the Second Punic War period, drawn from the Carthaginian
aristocratic and military-citizen strata. The
[Sacred Band](/groups/sacred-band) (the elite Carthaginian
citizen-infantry formation, attested through the Sicilian Wars
and the Mercenary War) was the principal documented
manifestation of citizen-soldiering in the Carthaginian
tradition. Officers across the broader army were drawn
substantially from the citizen body — the Carthaginian
aristocratic command tradition (Hamilcar, Hannibal, Hasdrubal,
Mago, the broader Barcid command-circle) is a citizen-officer
tradition operating over a mercenary-and-allied troop base.

**The mercenary component** was the principal infantry source
across the Carthaginian military's operational history.
Mercenaries were recruited from
[Libya](/groups/libyans) (the largest single source — Libyan
heavy infantry was the Carthaginian army's principal infantry
arm), [Iberia](/places/iberia) (Celtiberian and Iberian
infantry plus the famous *[gladius hispaniensis](/groups/celtiberians)*),
the [Balearic Islands](/places/ebusus) (the famous
[Balearic slingers](/groups/balearic-slingers)), Gaul
(Celtic infantry, particularly the
[Cisalpine Gauls](/groups/cisalpine-gauls) during the Italian
campaign), and other Mediterranean recruitment zones. The
mercenary apparatus operated through long-term contractual
relationships with specific recruitment networks, not through
ad-hoc hiring; the
[Mercenary War of 241–238 BCE](/events/mercenary-war) was the
principal documented case of the contractual structure breaking
down.

**The allied component** was provided by polities under
Carthaginian hegemony or in treaty relationship with Carthage.
[Numidian](/groups/massylii) cavalry was the most consequential
allied component, supplied through the political-marriage and
treaty relationships the
[Numidian-Punic interface](/themes/punic-numidian-relations)
documented in detail; Sicilian Punic-cultural cities supplied
infantry and naval contingents during the Sicilian Wars; the
[Tripolitanian Emporia](/places/sabratha) supplied tribute and
contingents.

## The cavalry tradition

The Carthaginian cavalry was the best in the
ancient Mediterranean during the Punic Wars period —
documented through the Carthaginian cavalry victories at
[Trebia](/events/battle-of-trebia),
[Cannae](/events/battle-of-cannae), and the broader Italian
campaign. The system depended on Numidian recruitment networks
— Numidian cavalry was the principal Carthaginian field arm,
attested across the entire Punic Wars period in multiple
theaters. The cavalry's effectiveness reflected long-term
political-cultural relationships with the Numidian populations,
not ad-hoc tactical recruitment; the
[Punic-Numidian relations theme](/themes/punic-numidian-relations)
treats the broader institutional framework.

The cavalry's defection to Roman alignment under
[Masinissa](/people/masinissa) before
[Zama](/events/battle-of-zama) in 202 BCE was the structural
factor that converted Roman tactical-strategic competence into
Carthaginian battlefield defeat. The Numidian cavalry
component had been the Carthaginian tactical advantage at
Cannae; under Masinissa's command at Zama it became the Roman
tactical advantage, and the entire Punic War's strategic
configuration shifted accordingly.

## War elephants

Carthaginian war elephants are one of the more recognizable
features of Carthaginian military organization in the popular
imagination. The historical reality is more complex: war
elephants were a tactical-experimental component rather than a
core institutional element, and their actual effectiveness in
the documented Carthaginian engagements was mixed.

The species was probably the now-extinct North African elephant
(*Loxodonta africana pharaohensis*), substantially smaller than
the Indian war elephants used by Hellenistic eastern
Mediterranean armies. The Carthaginian elephant corps operated
across the Sicilian Wars, the First Punic War, the Mercenary
War, the
[Barcid Iberian campaigns](/narratives/the-barcid-iberian-state),
and Hannibal's Italian campaign (the famous Alps-crossing
elephants). Documented Carthaginian elephant deployments include
the Battle of Bagradas (255 BCE, where the elephants decisively
broke the Roman line under Regulus), Trebia (218 BCE, mixed
results), and Zama (202 BCE, where Scipio's open-lane formation
substantively neutralized the elephant charge).

The elephant component is treated under specific event pages
for each engagement; the broader pattern is one of
tactical-experimental use that produced occasional decisive
results but never became a reliable institutional component
the way the cavalry tradition did.

## The naval institution

The Carthaginian navy was the most developed
naval institutional system in the ancient Mediterranean — the
[Carthaginian sea power as institutional system narrative](/narratives/carthaginian-sea-power-as-system)
treats the naval institution in its own right. The principal
features: the
[cothon](/places/cothon) infrastructure (paired commercial and
military basins at Carthage), standardized shipbuilding
documented through the
[Marsala Punic Warship](/artifacts/marsala-punic-warship) and
the [Egadi Islands bronze warship rams](/artifacts/egadi-bronze-ram),
mass-mobilization capacity (350 quinqueremes at Cape Ecnomus in
256 BCE), and centralized command structures that operated
independently of the army's recruitment apparatus.

The naval institution was treated as a state apparatus rather
than a mercenary-allied component — the contrast with the
army's recruitment model is structurally significant. The
First Punic War's Carthaginian naval defeats reflect the
structural difficulty of rebuilding fleets after catastrophic
storm and battle losses rather than systemic Carthaginian naval
inferiority; the [Adherbal](/people/adherbal) victory at
[Drepana](/events/battle-of-drepana) is the counter-evidence to
the standard "Carthage couldn't naval" framing.

## The fiscal-manpower limits

The Carthaginian military system's structural vulnerability was
fiscal-manpower-dependent: maintaining mercenary armies
required substantial state revenue, and the recruitment networks
required maintained political relationships. The
[10,000-talent indemnity](/events/treaty-of-201-bce) of the
Treaty of 201 BCE and the substantive Carthaginian fiscal
recovery across the long peace are treated under
[period 07](/periods/07-the-long-peace); the structural point is
that the Carthaginian military apparatus was substantively
expensive to maintain at full mobilization, and the war-cost
fiscal pressure across both Punic Wars contributed to the
strategic outcomes.

The Roman state-citizen-army model required substantially
different fiscal infrastructure (citizen-soldiers serve at lower
cost than mercenaries; Roman political organization recovered
from defeat through citizen-replacement faster than Carthaginian
political organization could recover from contract-network
breakdown). The structural Roman advantage in the Punic Wars
was the fiscal-manpower asymmetry the two
military models produced; the Carthaginian military was not
inferior in tactical-operational terms, it was structurally
more expensive to sustain.

## Sources

The principal modern reference for Carthaginian military
organization is [Hoyos's *The Carthaginians* (2010)](/sources/hoyos-carthaginians-2010)
and the broader Hoyos *Hannibal's Dynasty* (2003) for the
Barcid command tradition. For elephants specifically,
Charles's *Hannibal's Elephant Army* (2008) is the standard
modern treatment; for the navy, the
[Carthaginian sea power narrative](/narratives/carthaginian-sea-power-as-system)
treats the institutional structure. The
[Krings-edited handbook](/sources/krings-manuel-1995) chapters
on Carthaginian military institutions remain the deepest
specialist reference.
