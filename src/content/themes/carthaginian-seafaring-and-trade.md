## The Phoenician inheritance

Carthage's commercial reach was not a Carthaginian invention.
The Phoenician homeland cities (Tyre, Sidon, Byblos, Arwad) had
established a Mediterranean trading network in the early Iron
Age, with seasonal and permanent installations along the coasts
of North Africa, Sicily, Sardinia, the Balearics, southern
Iberia, and (probably) the Atlantic coast as far as Britain by
the 9th or 8th century BCE. The Phoenician trade economy was
built on three principal exports: silver from Iberia (the famous
mines of Tartessos near modern Huelva), tin from the Atlantic
(probably Cornwall via Iberian intermediaries), and the
purple-dye industry of the Levantine coast itself.

When Tyre declined as a metropolitan center in the 7th and 6th
centuries BCE, the network it had created did not collapse. It
was inherited, reorganized, and substantially expanded by
Carthage. The Carthaginian commercial empire of the 5th to 3rd
centuries BCE was the structural successor to the Tyrian
network, with the western settlements increasingly oriented
toward Carthage rather than toward the Levantine homeland.

## What Carthage traded

The principal Carthaginian commercial commodities are partly
attested in literary sources and partly inferred from
archaeology. The export side of the network appears to have
included:

- **Silver from Iberia.** The single most important commercial
  commodity, exported from the Carthaginian-controlled mines of
  southern Iberia (especially around modern Cartagena, the
  *Carthago Nova* founded by Hasdrubal the Fair in the late 3rd
  century BCE).
- **Tin.** Less directly attested but probably traded through
  Carthaginian intermediaries at Gades and the Atlantic
  Phoenician colonies.
- **North African agricultural produce.** Grain from the
  Carthaginian hinterland; olive oil and wine from Carthaginian
  estates in modern Tunisia and Algeria.
- **Manufactured goods.** Punic ceramics, metalwork, and dyed
  textiles, with the Tyrian purple-dye industry continued at
  Carthage.

The import side included:

- **Greek and Etruscan luxury goods.** Painted ceramics, fine
  metalwork, and other manufactured items found in
  Carthaginian-area tombs and settlements.
- **Egyptian items.** Faience, scarabs, and other small
  manufactured goods, sometimes imitated locally in Punic
  workshops.
- **Slaves.** Imported from various sources for use in
  agriculture, mining, and domestic service.

The aggregate volume of this trade is difficult to estimate
from the surviving evidence but was large enough to fund
Carthage's military and political role across centuries.

## The geography of the network

The Carthaginian network's geographic reach is partly attested
in literary sources, partly inferred from Punic material
culture, and partly preserved in unique documents like the
*Periplus of Hanno*. The principal nodes:

- **The Carthaginian capital and its African hinterland.**
  Modern Tunisia and northeastern Algeria.
- **The western Sicilian network.** Motya, Panormus, Lilybaeum,
  Solus, and other Carthaginian-controlled or aligned cities,
  contested with Greek Sicily through the Sicilian Wars and
  ultimately lost in the First Punic War.
- **Sardinia and Corsica.** Major Carthaginian holdings,
  including Tharros, Sulcis, Caralis, and Nora, lost to Rome
  in the 238 BCE seizure.
- **The Balearic Islands.** Ibiza in particular, a major
  Punic settlement.
- **Southern Iberia.** Gades, Malaga, and especially the
  Cartagena region after the late-3rd-century BCE Barcid
  reorganization.
- **The Atlantic.** Lixus on the Moroccan coast and the
  network of installations described in Hanno's Periplus along
  the West African Atlantic.
- **The eastern Mediterranean.** Less central but attested:
  Carthaginian ceremonial connections with Tyre, commercial
  contacts with the Aegean and Egypt.

## What the trade enabled

Carthaginian wealth from this network funded the city's
military mobilizations in the Sicilian Wars, the Punic Wars,
and the Iberian project. The 10,000-talent indemnity imposed by
the Treaty of 201 BCE, paid down ahead of schedule, is the kind
of fiscal capacity only a major commercial power could sustain.
The Roman concern that produced Cato's advocacy for Carthage's
destruction in the 150s BCE was concern about Carthaginian
economic recovery, which by the 160s BCE had restored the city
to wealth comparable to its pre-Zama position.

The network's structural feature was that it was cellular and
flexible rather than centralized. Carthaginian commercial
practice (as far as we can tell from the limited surviving
evidence) operated through partnerships, family firms, and
ad-hoc joint ventures rather than through a centralized state
trading apparatus. The flexibility was the network's strength
in normal operation; it was a vulnerability when Roman pressure
disrupted specific nodes (Sicily, Sardinia, southern Iberia)
without immediately disrupting the rest.

## What we don't know

Much of the practical detail of how Carthaginian trade
operated is poorly preserved. We do not have the kinds of
commercial records that survive in the cuneiform archives of
Mesopotamia or the papyrus archives of Egypt. We know who was
trading and roughly what was being traded; we do not know how
contracts were structured, how credit was extended, how
disputes were resolved, how risks were distributed across
voyages. The institutional infrastructure of Carthaginian
commerce is largely reconstructed from comparative evidence
and from scattered references in Greek and Roman sources.

## Material evidence

The physical record of Carthaginian seafaring is uneven but
exceptional in specific cases. The Marsala Punic Warship is the
single surviving Punic-period warship hull anywhere, recovered
off Lilybaeum in 1971 and probably a casualty of the Battle of
the Aegates Islands or its surrounding First Punic War
operations. The hull's mortise-and-tenon joinery, the inscribed
Punic shipwright's marks on the timbers, and the construction
evidence the find preserves constitute the principal documentation
for Punic naval architecture.

The Egadi Islands Bronze Warship Rams — twenty-seven recovered
since 2010 from the Battle of the Aegates Islands seabed — extend
the documentation dramatically. Some rams carry Punic inscriptions
invoking Baal; others bear Latin inscriptions naming Roman
construction officials. V-shaped impact scratches across many
striking surfaces preserve direct physical evidence of the
tactical patterns Polybius described.

Harbor architecture survives in the Carthage Cothon, the paired
circular military and rectangular commercial basins still visible
as topographic features in the modern archaeological park. The
Motya Kothon, predating the Carthage cothon by approximately two
centuries, is the earliest substantial Phoenician-colonial harbor
(or sacred-pool, per recent reinterpretation) installation in the
western Mediterranean.

For Phoenician metropolitan-period commercial production, the
Murex Shells and Tyrian Purple industry is the foundational
Phoenician commercial activity — the practice that gave
"Phoenician" its etymology and underwrote the Levantine
commercial reach the Carthaginian network inherited and
extended. The Mahdia Shipwreck (a Roman-period cargo of Greek
art lost off Tunisia c. 80-60 BCE) extends the maritime
narrative into the post-Carthaginian period and documents the
late-Hellenistic trade routes that ran through what had been
Carthage's commercial sphere.

## Sources

The principal modern treatment of the Phoenician colonial
network is Aubet's *The Phoenicians and the West* (2nd ed.
2001). For Carthaginian commercial operations specifically,
Hoyos's *The Carthaginians* (2010) provides a useful chapter
synthesis. The *Periplus of Hanno* (treated separately as a
source on the site) is the unique surviving Carthaginian
commercial-exploration document. The Krings *La civilisation
phénicienne et punique* handbook (1995) is the deepest
specialist reference for the broader civilizational context.
