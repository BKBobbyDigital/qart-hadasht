## What we know about named women

The surviving record names Carthaginian women only at the
highest aristocratic level and only in moments of political-
historical drama:

**Sophonisba.** Daughter of Hasdrubal Gisco, married first to
Masinissa of Numidia (a politically motivated alliance during
the Second Punic War), then to the Numidian king Syphax (a
Carthaginian-driven re-marriage to keep him aligned with
Carthage). After Syphax's defeat by Scipio in 203 BCE, she
took poison rather than be paraded in the Roman triumph.

**Hasdrubal's wife.** Unnamed in the surviving record. Wife of
Hasdrubal the Boetharch, Carthaginian commander during the
final Roman siege. Appeared on a balcony of the temple of
Eshmun on the Byrsa during the final assault, denounced her
husband as a coward in a speech preserved by Appian, killed
her two children, and threw herself into the burning temple.

**Hannibal's wife (Imilce).** The Iberian noblewoman Hannibal
married before crossing the Alps in 218 BCE. Named only in
some later traditions; her actual fate is not preserved.

**The Barcid daughters.** The daughter of Hamilcar Barca who
married Naravas the Numidian to seal the alliance during the
Mercenary War. The daughter of Hasdrubal the Fair who married
into the Iberian aristocracy. None named in the surviving
sources.

The pattern is structural: the surviving record names women
when their marriages, deaths, or political acts intersect
directly with major military-political events that the Greek
and Roman tradition cared about. Women whose lives did not
intersect with these events are unnamed and largely invisible.

## What inscriptions tell us

The Tophet stelae and other Carthaginian inscriptions provide
the broader window onto women's lives that the literary
tradition does not. The inscriptional patterns:

**Female dedicators.** Women appear as dedicators of votive
offerings in their own right, sometimes alongside husbands or
fathers and sometimes independently. The pattern indicates
that Carthaginian women had at minimum the religious-legal
capacity to make formal dedications.

**Matrilineal naming.** A minority of Carthaginian inscriptions
include matronymics (mother's name) alongside the more usual
patronymics. The practice suggests that maternal lineage was
sometimes a meaningful identification, even in a strongly
patrilineal society.

**Priestess offices.** Women appear in dedicatory and votive
inscriptions in priestess roles (*kohenet*, parallel to the
masculine *kohen*), particularly in relation to Tanit's cult.
Female religious office is firmly attested.

**Independent property.** Some Carthaginian funerary
inscriptions for women indicate independent property
ownership and the capacity to make funerary arrangements,
suggesting that Carthaginian women had at least limited
property rights.

The aggregate inscriptional picture suggests that
Carthaginian women had a more substantial public role,
particularly in religious and economic spheres, than the
literary tradition would lead us to expect. This is a common
pattern in ancient Mediterranean societies and is not unique
to Carthage; the inscriptional evidence routinely qualifies
the literary picture.

## Family structure

Carthaginian family organization, on the available evidence,
followed the broader Phoenician-Punic pattern:

**Patrilineal descent.** The standard organizational principle,
attested in essentially every patronymic chain in the
Tophet stelae and other inscriptions.

**Multi-generational households.** Wealthy Carthaginian houses
appear to have included extended family alongside slave staff,
in the standard Mediterranean elite pattern.

**Marriage patterns.** Aristocratic marriage was substantially
political (the Barcid dynastic marriages, the Sophonisba
remarriage). Ordinary Carthaginian marriage practice is less
attested.

**Divorce.** Attested in Phoenician-Punic legal contexts more
broadly; specific Carthaginian practice is imperfectly known.

**Children and child-rearing.** The Tophet has produced the
famous and contested evidence about infant mortality and
ritual response (treated separately under the Tophet question
theme). The broader pattern of how Carthaginian families
raised their children is largely unrecoverable.

## Religion and women

The Tanit cult is the principal documented sphere of
Carthaginian women's religious participation. As Tanit's
prominence in the pantheon rose from approximately the 5th
century BCE onward, female religious office and female
participation in dedication appear to have rose with it. The
sign of Tanit appears on stelae dedicated by women in
substantial numbers.

Whether Tanit's prominence reflected the broader social
position of women, or whether the social position of women
reflected the religious-cultic structure, is a question that
the surviving evidence does not answer. The two correlate,
but the causal direction is not recoverable.

## What we cannot recover

The texture of Carthaginian women's lives is largely lost.
The legal framework governing women's status, the practical
division of labor inside households, women's role in
commerce beyond the elite level, the educational and
religious socialization of girls: all of these are mostly
unknown. We have flashes (Sophonisba's political career,
Hasdrubal's wife's defiance, the inscriptional dedications
of named female priestesses) and the structural inference
that Carthaginian women were more substantively present in
public life than the literary tradition preserves. We do not
have the texture.

This pattern is not unique to Carthage; it is the pattern of
ancient Mediterranean source-survival. What is distinctive
about the Carthaginian case is that the inscriptional record,
particularly from the Tophet, gives us better quantitative
data on women's religious participation than is available
for many comparable ancient cities.

## Sources

The principal modern treatments are in MacDonald's *Carthage:
A New History* (2015) and in Krings's *La civilisation
phénicienne et punique* handbook (1995). Specialist work on
Phoenician-Punic gender and family includes Bonnet's various
studies on Phoenician religion (which engages substantially
with women's religious roles) and the broader comparative
work on Mediterranean ancient societies. The Tophet
inscriptional corpus is the principal primary-evidence base.
