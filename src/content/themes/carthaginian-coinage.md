## The late adoption

The first fact about Carthaginian coinage is how late it
begins. Greek cities had been striking coins since the 6th
century BCE; Carthage, by then already one of the wealthiest
states in the Mediterranean, struck nothing until roughly the
end of the 5th. This was not backwardness. The Phoenician
commercial tradition Carthage inherited ran on weighed silver
and bullion, a system that handles long-distance trade
perfectly well, and Tyre itself adopted coinage only in the
mid-5th century. A state coins money when something it needs
to do requires coins.

For Carthage that something was war. The earliest
Carthaginian coins are tetradrachms struck in Sicily from
about 410 BCE, in the years of the great invasions under
Hannibal son of Gisco and Himilco, and their purpose is
written on them: alongside issues marked *qrtḥdšt*
("Carthage"), the commonest legends are *mḥnt* ("the camp")
and variants naming the army itself as the issuing authority.
Greek and Sicilian mercenaries expected pay in coin, so the
Carthaginian expeditionary command became a mint. Coinage
entered Carthaginian practice as a military-fiscal instrument,
not a commercial habit, and it kept that character to the end:
the major expansions of output track the Sicilian wars, the
First Punic War, and the Barcid build-up in Iberia.

## The Sicilian beginnings

The Siculo-Punic series is visually Greek. The early issues
borrow types directly from Syracusan models (the quadriga, the
head of a goddess in the style of the Syracusan Arethusa), and
the die-engraving is of Greek quality, in some issues almost
certainly by Greek hands. What makes them Carthaginian is the
Punic legend and the issuing authority. This is the same
pattern of deliberate selection the site describes in the
[cultural integrator
take](/editorialTakes/carthage-as-cultural-integrator):
Carthage adopted the Greek instrument, hired or trained the
best available engravers, and put it under Punic authority for
Punic purposes.

## The metropolitan series: Tanit and the horse

From the mid-4th century BCE Carthage itself became a major
mint, and the metropolitan coinage settled into the
iconographic pair that defined it for over a century: the head
of [Tanit](/deities/tanit) wreathed in grain on the obverse, a
standing or rearing horse, often with a palm tree, on the
reverse. The [Carthage
tetradrachm](/artifacts/carthage-tetradrachm) is the canonical
example.

Both types reward and resist reading. The Tanit head tracks
the goddess's rise to the head of the civic pantheon in
exactly the period the [votive
record](/narratives/the-fifth-century-religious-transformation)
shows the same thing, and the grain wreath connects her to the
agricultural prosperity of the African hinterland; the
engraving itself is adapted from Greek Kore/Persephone models,
possibly reinforced by the state adoption of the Demeter and
Kore cult in 396 BCE. The horse and palm are less settled.
Ancient foundation legend (preserved in Justin and Virgil)
has a horse's head dug up at the founding of the city as an
omen of its warlike future, and the palm (Greek *phoinix*) may
pun on "Phoenician"; both readings are attractive, neither is
attested from the Carthaginian side. The types were official
choices and meant something; what they meant is modern
inference, and the site labels it as such.

## The metal as a fiscal record

Because coins can be assayed, the precious-metal series
doubles as the only year-by-year financial record of the
Carthaginian state that survives. The standard catalogue of
the gold and electrum, [Jenkins and
Lewis](/sources/jenkins-lewis-carthaginian-gold-1963),
established the sequence: high-purity gold staters in the
prosperous mid-4th century, progressively alloyed electrum in
the early 3rd, and heavy debasement across the First Punic
War, when the indemnity after defeat and twenty-three years of
fleet-building drained the treasury. By the war's end Carthage
was striking heavily debased electrum and base-metal issues,
and the fiscal incapacity the coins document is the same
incapacity that produced the pay dispute behind the
[Mercenary War](/events/mercenary-war).

The Mercenary War left its own numismatic trace, and it is one
of the most remarkable items of evidence in the whole record:
the rebels struck coins. Overstruck on Carthaginian issues,
with Greek legends including ΛΙΒΥΩΝ ("of the Libyans"), the
rebel coinage shows a mercenary-Libyan coalition organized
enough to operate a mint and assert monetary authority — the
material counterpart to the site's reading of the revolt as a
[coordinated political
undertaking](/editorialTakes/mercenary-war-atrocity-structural)
rather than a spasm of indiscipline.

## The Barcid Iberian coinage

The final major chapter is Iberian. From 237 BCE the Barcid
command struck silver at a new standard, the shekel rather
than the Greek tetradrachm, from the mining wealth described
in the [Iberian silver
narrative](/narratives/iberian-silver-and-the-barcid-economy).
The [coinage itself](/artifacts/barcid-iberian-coinage) is
among the finest of the pre-Roman west: a male head usually
identified as Melqart with the club of Heracles, war
elephants, ship prows. Whether the Melqart heads carry the
features of Hamilcar, Hasdrubal, or Hannibal, in the manner of
Hellenistic ruler portraiture, has been argued since the 19th
century and remains unresolved; if true it would be the only
surviving likeness of any Carthaginian, which is precisely why
the question attracts more confidence than the evidence
supports. The shekel standard, the new mint at [New
Carthage](/places/carthago-nova), and the army-pay function
together make the Barcid coinage the clearest single
expression of the [Barcid Iberian
state's](/editorialTakes/barcid-iberian-state-as-state)
character: Punic in standard and authority, Hellenistic in
idiom, military in purpose.

## What coins are evidence for

Numismatic evidence does work for this site in four
directions. It dates: hoard sequences and overstrikes anchor
chronology where the literary record is vague. It counts:
metal fineness and output scale are the nearest thing to
surviving Carthaginian state accounts. It shows: coin types
are official self-presentation, the only mass-produced
Carthaginian images that survive as the state issued them. And
it warns: every interpretive reading of a type, from the palm
pun to the Barcid portraits, is modern inference from an
image, and the confidence vocabulary applies to coins exactly
as it does to texts.
