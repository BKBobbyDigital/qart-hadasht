The literary record of Carthage is overwhelmingly
Greco-Roman and overwhelmingly hostile. The inscriptional
record is Carthaginian and overwhelmingly mundane. The
contrast is the central methodological fact of Punic
studies, and it is why the inscriptional corpus matters as
much as it does: where the literary sources tell us how
Greeks and Romans wanted to remember Carthage, the
inscriptions tell us what Carthaginians themselves wrote
about their own gods, their own families, their own
offices, their own dead. Roughly six thousand Phoenician
and Punic inscriptions survive across the Mediterranean,
and the working reconstruction of Carthaginian religion,
social structure, naming practice, and civic administration
rests substantially on this corpus rather than on the
literary tradition.

This page treats the inscriptional record as a category of
evidence in its own right: what survives, what genres are
represented, what the formulaic conventions look like, what
questions the corpus can answer and what it cannot.

## What survives

Surviving Phoenician and Punic inscriptions cluster into a
few principal genres, distributed unevenly by site and
period.

Votive stelae are by far the largest category. The Tophet
precincts at Carthage, Motya, Sulci, Tharros, Hadrumetum,
and elsewhere have produced thousands of small inscribed
stones, the bulk of them dedications to Baal Hammon — and,
from the fifth century BCE forward at Carthage, to
*Tnt Pn Bʿl*, "Tanit Face of Baal." The standard formula
is highly conservative: a brief opening naming the deities
("To Lady Tanit Face of Baal and to Lord Baal Hammon"), a
dedication verb (*ndr*, "vowed"), the dedicator's name with
two or three generations of patronymic, and a request for
the deity's hearing and blessing (*kšmʿ qlʾ ybrk*, "because
he heard his voice, may he bless him"). The formula's
stability across six centuries is itself one of the most
striking features of Punic religious practice in the
surviving record.

Funerary epitaphs are the second-largest category, attested
across the Phoenician homeland (the Eshmunazar II
Sarcophagus and the Tabnit Sarcophagus from Sidon are the
principal examples) and across the western diaspora.
Carthaginian elite tomb inscriptions are rarer than one
might expect — the city's necropoleis were extensively
reused and disturbed in the Roman period — but substantial
corpora survive from Punic Sicily, Sardinia, and Spain.

Civic and bilingual inscriptions include the
Phoenician-Aramaic bilingual at Karatepe (a Cilician text
but written in Phoenician), the Phoenician-Etruscan
trilingual gold tablets from Pyrgi, the Phoenician-Greek
bilingual on the Cippi of Melqart from Malta, and the
Phoenician-Greek bilingual from Idalion on Cyprus. These
inscriptions are the philological anchor for the modern
reconstruction of Punic vocabulary and syntax — without
bilingual texts, the script's consonantal structure would
leave too many words ambiguous.

The two surviving "tariff" inscriptions — the Marseille
Tariff (KAI 69) and the fragmentary Carthage Tariff (KAI
74) — are the single most informative texts for
reconstructing actual Punic sacrificial practice. Both list
prices for different sacrificial animals (ox, ram, ewe,
kid, lamb, bird) and specify the cuts that go to the
officiating priests and those that return to the
dedicators. They are the closest the surviving record
comes to a priest-side ritual handbook.

Civic-administrative inscriptions are rare but include the
*šft* (suffete) office attested across Carthage and the
colonial network, magistracy dedications, and the few
surviving treaty and boundary texts.

Commercial and economic inscriptions survive in small
numbers, mostly as inscribed amphora handles, ostraca, and
brief weight or container labels.

## What the corpus tells us that the literary record does not

Several question domains in Punic studies depend principally
on the inscriptional corpus because the Greco-Roman
literary record either ignores them, distorts them, or
filters them through hostile framing.

The actual pantheon, in proportion. Diodorus and Plutarch
tell us about Baal Hammon (under the *interpretatio* of
Kronos) because Greek readers wanted to know about child
sacrifice; the inscriptions tell us that Baal Hammon was
indeed the pre-eminent recipient of Tophet dedications,
that Tanit emerged from the fifth century BCE forward to
share or supersede the position, that Astarte declined
across the same period, that Eshmun and Melqart held
substantial but distinct cults, and that Bes was massively
important in personal and household practice while almost
invisible in civic religion. The inscriptional record
corrects the distorted weighting of the literary tradition.

Female religious agency. Greco-Roman literary sources for
Carthage almost never mention named Punic women. The
inscriptional record names hundreds — donors, priestesses,
dedicators on behalf of family members, makers of votive
offerings. The Tophet stelae include explicit female
dedications (the formula *bʿlt ndr*, "mistress of the
vow"). Priestess titles (*khnt*) are attested across
multiple sites. Women appear in commercial and funerary
inscriptions as economic actors. Reconstructing Punic
women's status from the literary record alone produces a
near-blank; the inscriptional record produces a partial
but substantive picture.

Theophoric naming as a window into popular religion. The
formulaic Punic name is constructed as *ʿbd* + deity
("servant of X"), *ger* + deity ("client of X"), *bn* +
deity ("son of X"), or similar. Counting theophoric
elements across the inscriptional corpus produces a
quantitative picture of which deities were active in
popular naming at each period. The decline of *ʿštrt*
(Astarte) elements from the fifth century BCE forward, the
rise of *tnt* (Tanit) elements, the persistence of *mlqrt*
(Melqart) elements as a marker of metropolitan-Tyrian
connection, the regional variation in *bʿl* compounds —
all of this is inscriptionally derived.

The suffete office across the colonial network. *Šft* is
attested epigraphically not just at Carthage but at Lepcis
Magna, at Punic Sardinian sites, and across the colonial
diaspora — suggesting the office was a shared Phoenician
civic institution rather than a uniquely Carthaginian one.
Aristotle's description of Carthaginian governance can be
checked against the inscriptional attestation of the
magistracies.

Punic continuity into Roman North Africa. Neo-Punic
inscriptions — the late Punic script that continued in use
through the Roman imperial period — are attested into the
second and third centuries CE in some North African
contexts. The inscriptional record is the principal
evidence for Punic linguistic and religious persistence
after 146 BCE, a topic the Latin literary record handles
only glancingly.

## What the corpus does not preserve

The inscriptional record is not a substitute for the lost
Carthaginian literary tradition. It does not preserve
narrative history, biographical writing, political
crisis-by-crisis accounts, philosophical or theological
treatises, or the everyday correspondence that would tell
us how Carthaginians explained themselves to themselves at
length. The formulaic conservatism of votive inscriptions
that makes the corpus comparable across centuries is the
same conservatism that limits what each individual
inscription can tell us. A six-line Tophet stele tells us
a great deal about the formula and not much about the
dedicator.

The gap in the literary tradition — what Carthaginians
wrote about themselves in extended prose — remains a gap.
The inscriptional record is the largest, most central
piece of the surviving record, but it is not the missing
literature. The two have to be read together, with the
literary tradition's distortions read through the
inscriptional corrective and the inscriptional
fragmentariness supplemented by what the literary
tradition preserves.

## How the corpus is cited

When the encyclopedia cites a specific Phoenician-Punic
inscription, it uses the standard reference apparatus. The
two principal modern corpora are the
[Corpus Inscriptionum Semiticarum](/sources/cis) (CIS,
Paris 1881–) and Donner and Röllig's
[Kanaanäische und Aramäische Inschriften](/sources/kai)
(KAI, Wiesbaden 1962–2002). A citation like "KAI 14"
refers to the Eshmunazar II Sarcophagus as numbered in
KAI; "CIS I 5510" refers to a specific Carthaginian Tophet
stele as numbered in CIS Part I. For inscriptions
discovered after the relevant corpus's publication, or for
site-specific collections, the standard citation is to the
individual site corpus (the Maktar steles, the Constantine
*Hofra* cache, the Sulcis material).

Individual inscriptions with their own analytical weight
have their own artifact pages on the site: the
[Marseille Tariff](/artifacts/marseille-tariff) and
[Carthage Tariff](/artifacts/carthage-tariff) for
sacrificial practice; the
[Eshmunazar II Sarcophagus](/artifacts/eshmunazar-ii-sarcophagus)
and [Tabnit Sarcophagus](/artifacts/tabnit-sarcophagus) for
Sidonian royal funerary epigraphy; the
[Pyrgi Tablets](/artifacts/pyrgi-tablets), the
[Karatepe Bilingual](/artifacts/karatepe-bilingual), the
[Idalion Bilingual](/artifacts/idalion-bilingual), and the
[Cippi of Melqart](/artifacts/cippi-of-melqart) for the
principal bilingual anchors; the
[Nora Stone](/artifacts/nora-stone) and
[Hanno's Periplus Inscription](/artifacts/hannos-periplus-inscription)
for early-colonial and exploration texts; the
[El Hofra Stele Cache](/artifacts/hofra-stele-cache) for
the post-146 Numidian continuation; and the
[Carthage Votive Inscription Slab](/artifacts/carthage-votive-inscription),
[Tharros Tophet Stele](/artifacts/tharros-tophet-stele),
and the broader
[Tophet Stelae of Carthage](/artifacts/tophet-stelae-carthage)
for the votive tradition.

For the corpus as a whole — the body of evidence that
makes the modern reconstruction of Carthaginian religion,
society, and identity possible at all — the CIS and KAI
source entries are the top-level reference, and this theme
is the analytical hub through which the site treats
inscriptional evidence as a primary category rather than
as a supplement.
