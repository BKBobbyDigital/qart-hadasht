## The strategic decision

By the spring of 218 BCE Hannibal had inherited from Hasdrubal
the Fair a Carthaginian Iberian position substantial enough to
fund and support a major military project against Rome. The
Saguntum siege of 219 BCE had committed Carthage to war. The
Roman declaration that followed in early 218 BCE meant the
question was no longer whether to fight but where.

The strategic options were three. Hannibal could meet a Roman
expedition in Iberia (the choice the Roman senate expected him
to make, given Roman intentions to invade Iberia under the
elder Scipio). He could meet a Roman expedition in Africa
(the choice the Roman senate had also planned for, with
Sempronius Longus's force initially destined for Sicily and
North Africa). Or he could move first, and force the
engagement on his own ground.

Hannibal chose the third option. The decision is preserved as
his alone in the surviving Roman tradition, made against the
counsel of cautious Carthaginian opinion at home. The
Carthaginian senate's authorization may have been broader than
the surviving Roman framing suggests; the Barcid faction's
political authority in 218 BCE was sufficient to commit the
state to a strategic gamble of this scale without senatorial
opposition that left visible traces.

## The departure and the march

Hannibal departed Carthago Nova in late spring 218 BCE. Polybius
gives about 90,000 infantry and 12,000 cavalry at the start
(3.35), and about 50,000 infantry and 9,000 cavalry after the
Pyrenees, once garrisons had been left and some Iberian troops
sent home; 37 elephants crossed the Rhone (3.42). For the army's
arrival in Italy he drew on the bronze tablet Hannibal set up at
the Lacinian promontory, which makes those figures among the
better-attested numbers of the Punic Wars, though they are
Hannibal's own.

The march through northeastern Iberia and southern Gaul
consumed the early summer. The Carthaginian army crossed the
Pyrenees in midsummer, marched through what is now southern
France, and reached the Rhone river near modern Avignon in
August. The Rhone crossing, contested by the Volcae tribe on
the eastern bank, was forced through a combination of
diversionary cavalry sent upriver to the rear of the Volcae
position and the main crossing in inflated leather rafts and
boats. Polybius's account of how the elephants were crossed
(on rafts disguised as sections of riverbank to overcome the
animals' fear of water) is preserved with characteristic
detail.

Roman intelligence about Hannibal's movement was good. The
elder Scipio had landed at Massalia (modern Marseille) with
the Iberian-bound expeditionary force shortly before Hannibal's
Rhone crossing; Scipio's reconnaissance located Hannibal's
position; the two armies missed each other by a few days
because Hannibal had moved upriver to the crossing point
faster than Scipio's intelligence predicted. By the time
Scipio's main force reached the eastern bank, Hannibal was
already moving north into the Alpine foothills.

The Roman strategic response was significant. Scipio sent his
brother Gnaeus on with the expeditionary force to Iberia
(opening the long Iberian campaign that would produce Scipio
Africanus's victory at Ilipa twelve years later) and returned
himself to northern Italy to meet Hannibal where the Alpine
descent would deliver him.

## The crossing

The Alpine ascent began in October or early November 218 BCE.
The specific pass Hannibal used is contested; Polybius's
account suggests one set of geographic features (most
consistent with the Col du Clapier or the Col de la Traversette
on the modern French-Italian border), while Livy's account
seems to imply a different southerly crossing. The 2010s
discovery of horse-manure deposits at the Col de la Traversette
dated to the Hannibalic period has shifted modern scholarship
toward the Traversette identification, though without
consensus.

The crossing took approximately fifteen days. Conditions were
extreme: cold beyond the army's preparation, snow obstructing
the passes, hostile Allobroges and other Alpine peoples
attacking the column from defensive positions on slopes above
the path, narrow trails that required laborious individual
movement of pack animals and elephants, water sources frozen
or contaminated. In the descent a landslide had carried away
the path, and Polybius (3.55) describes the army cutting a new
one along the cliff: a day's work for the horses and pack
animals, three days for the elephants, with Numidians working
in relays. The better-known version, in which the rock is split
with fire and vinegar, is Livy's (21.37) and does not appear in
Polybius; the two accounts, and the ancient quarrel over whether
the Alps were impassable or ordinary, are set side by side in
the [source comparison](/sourceComparisons/alps-crossing).

Casualties through the crossing were substantial. Hannibal
descended into the Po Valley with 12,000 African and 8,000
Iberian infantry and no more than 6,000 cavalry (Polybius
3.56), against about 50,000 infantry and 9,000 cavalry after
the Pyrenees. How that loss divides between the march through
Gaul and the mountains cannot be recovered, and Livy (21.38)
shows that the Roman tradition had no agreed figures at all.
The elephants came through the crossing and fought at the
Trebia; nearly all of them died in the cold that followed
(Polybius 3.74).

## The arrival

The Carthaginian army that descended into the Po Valley in late
November 218 BCE was reduced but operational. Hannibal moved
quickly to recruit Gallic auxiliaries from the Insubres and
other tribes hostile to the Roman colonial presence in northern
Italy; within weeks the army's effective strength had recovered
to approximately 40,000 with the Gallic additions. The
preliminary cavalry engagement at the Ticinus in late November
218 BCE, where Hannibal's Numidians defeated the elder Scipio's
cavalry and wounded the consul (the rescue of his father by
the future Africanus is preserved in this engagement), confirmed
to the Romans that the Carthaginian field army was a serious
operational threat. The Battle of the Trebia followed in
December, and the Italian campaign of the next fifteen years
opened.

## What the crossing meant

Three observations.

The operational achievement was real. Moving an army of
approximately 50,000 across the Pyrenees, the Rhone, and the
Alps in a single campaigning season was unprecedented in scale
for the period. The losses were severe but not catastrophic,
and the army that arrived in Italy was operationally capable.
The crossing's reputation as one of the great strategic moves
of antiquity is earned.

The strategic surprise was decisive. Roman strategic
preparation had assumed Carthaginian operations in Iberia and
North Africa, with Italy itself outside the operational space.
Hannibal's arrival in northern Italy reshaped the war's
geography in a single move. The elder Scipio's rapid return
to Italy, the Roman scrambling to meet Hannibal in the Po
Valley, and the political pressure that produced the
Trebia engagement were all consequences of Roman strategic
unpreparedness for what Hannibal had done.

The longer-term consequence was the campaign that followed.
The crossing produced a Carthaginian field army inside Italy
with no readily available Roman counter-force in adequate
strength. The fifteen years of Italian campaign that followed
(Trebia, Trasimene, Cannae, the long stalemate, the Metaurus,
the eventual recall to Africa for Zama) all rest on the
operational fact the crossing delivered. The crossing was not
itself the war; but the war's specific shape was produced by
the crossing.

## What we don't know

The route through the Alps, despite recent archaeological
work, is not securely identified. Several details of the
crossing (the exact moment of the elephants' crossing of the
Rhone, the specific location of the broken path in the descent,
the names of the Alpine peoples Hannibal fought) are
preserved imperfectly. The Carthaginian inscription at
Lacinium that Polybius cites for the troop figures has not
survived, so the specific numbers depend on Polybius's
reading of a document we cannot independently verify.

What survives is enough to reconstruct the operation in broad
outline and to understand its strategic significance. The
specific geography of the route, and the specific causes of
the substantial losses, are at the limit of what the surviving
record will allow.
