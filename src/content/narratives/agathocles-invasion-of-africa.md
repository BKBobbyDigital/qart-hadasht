## A war he was losing

Agathocles came to power in Syracuse in 317 with Carthaginian help: the
Carthaginian general [Hamilcar](/people/hamilcar-ally-of-agathocles),
Justin says, lent him 5,000 African
troops and took his oath of loyalty, and the peace Hamilcar brokered in
314 left the Greek cities under Syracusan leadership (see
[Agathocles' life](/narratives/agathocles-life)). Carthage repudiated
that arrangement. In 311 it sent a new army under
[Hamilcar son of Gisco](/people/hamilcar-son-of-gisco): 130 warships,
2,000 citizen soldiers including many nobles, 10,000 Libyans and
mercenaries from Etruria and the Balearics. A storm sank sixty of the
warships and two hundred transports, and Carthage mourned the drowned
nobles in its customary way, by hanging the city walls with black
sackcloth (Diodorus 19.106). Hamilcar still beat Agathocles at the
Himeras river below the hill of Ecnomus, won over most of the Greek
cities, and shut him up in Syracuse (19.107–110).

## The crossing

Agathocles told the Syracusans only that he had found the road to
victory. He raised money by confiscation, freed slaves of military age,
manned sixty ships, and slipped out of the harbor in 310 when grain
ships approaching Syracuse drew the blockading fleet after them
(Diodorus 20.4–5; Justin 22.4). On the next day the fleet saw an eclipse so
complete that stars appeared, which Agathocles explained away as an omen
against the Carthaginians (20.5). Modern calculation dates it to 15
August 310 BCE, one of the firmest dates in the period; the astronomer
P. H. Cowell used it in 1906 to reconstruct the fleet's course
([Cowell 1906](/sources/cowell-agathocles-eclipse-1906)).

He landed at a place called the Quarries, near Cape Bon, pursued to the
beach by the Carthaginian fleet, and there burned his ships. Diodorus
presents it as a vow to Demeter and Kore, the goddesses of Sicily, made
during the pursuit; Justin as a way of telling the army it must win or
die (Diodorus 20.6–7; Justin 22.6). The goddesses were the same pair
Carthage had adopted in 396 (see [Demeter and Kore](/deities/demeter-kore)).

## The first victory

The army marched through a country of gardens, irrigated estates and
country houses, took a town Diodorus calls Megalepolis and then White
Tunis, and plundered both (20.8). Carthage, whose field army was in
Sicily, raised a citizen army that Diodorus puts at 40,000 foot, 1,000
horse and 2,000 chariots, under two generals chosen because they had an
inherited feud, so that neither could use the command against the city
(20.10). One of them, Bomilcar, had long wanted to be tyrant, and
Diodorus stops here to explain why Carthaginian generals were tempted:
the city advanced its leading men to command in war and then, in peace,
harried them with lawsuits and false charges, so that some deserted
their posts and others tried to make themselves tyrants (20.10.3–4).

Agathocles had about 13,500 men, and gave his unarmed rowers shield
covers stretched on sticks to look like armor; he loosed owls, Athena's
birds, among the ranks to hearten them (20.11). Hanno died fighting with
the Sacred Band, Bomilcar withdrew, and the Carthaginians fled to the
city. Diodorus found the Carthaginian losses given as anything from
under 1,000 to over 6,000, and reports 20,000 pairs of manacles in their
camp (20.12–13). The claim on the battle sets out the figures
([claim](/claims/agathocles-white-tunis-victory)).

Carthage turned to its gods. Believing that Heracles (Melqart) of the
mother city was angry, it sent money, costly offerings and even golden
shrines from its temples to Tyre, where it had once sent a tenth of its
public revenue and lately very little; and, Diodorus says, sacrificed two hundred children of the leading families
to Cronus, usually identified with Baal Hammon, with three hundred more
volunteering (20.14). It also sent to Hamilcar in Sicily, asking him to
send help (20.15).

## Sicily and Africa

Hamilcar was not recalled. He tried to storm Syracuse on the strength of
false news that Agathocles' army had been destroyed, failed, and sent
5,000 men to Carthage (20.16). In 309 he led his whole army against
Syracuse by night; the column fell into confusion on the narrow roads,
the Syracusans holding Euryalus charged it, and Hamilcar was captured
and tortured to death (20.29–30). His head was carried to Africa, where
Agathocles rode up to the Carthaginian camp and showed it to them
(20.33). Justin credits the victory to Agathocles' brother Antander
(22.7). The invasion had drained Carthaginian strength from Sicily; the
decisive defeat there came from a Carthaginian attack that failed. The
site formerly said Hamilcar was recalled; see the
[claim](/claims/agathocles-hamilcar-recalled).

In Africa Agathocles took towns along the coast, including Neapolis and
Hadrumetum, won over Aelymas, a Libyan king, and plundered the
countryside, while his army came close to mutiny when his son
Archagathus killed an officer who had insulted him (20.17–18, 33–34).

## Ophellas

His most useful ally was Ophellas, a former companion of Alexander who
ruled Cyrene and wanted Libya for himself. Agathocles offered him the
bargain: Africa for Ophellas, Sicily for Agathocles, once Carthage fell.
Ophellas marched more than 10,000 foot, 600 horse and 100 chariots,
followed by as many noncombatants and colonists, some recruited from
Athens, for more than two months across the desert of the Syrtis
(Diodorus 20.40–42; Justin 22.7).

A few days after they arrived, Agathocles accused Ophellas of plotting
against him and attacked his camp while many of his men were out
foraging. Ophellas died fighting, and Agathocles took over his army
(Diodorus 20.42). Justin has the two dining together often and Ophellas
adopting Agathocles' son before being killed off his guard (22.7). The
popular story of a murder at a banquet merges the two
([claim](/claims/agathocles-ophellas-murder)).

## Bomilcar

In the same year Bomilcar made his attempt. At a review in the New City
he dismissed all but his confederates, some 500 citizens and 1,000
mercenaries, declared himself tyrant and cut his way into the agora. The
young men of the city formed companies, the citizens fought him from the
tall buildings around the agora, and his men were driven back into the
New City. The elders offered amnesty; the rest were spared, but Bomilcar
was tortured and put to death in spite of the oaths, and Diodorus says
the Carthaginians "preserved the constitution of their fathers"
(20.43–44). Justin has him crucified in the middle of the forum, and adds that from the
cross he accused the Carthaginians
of their crimes against their own generals: Hanno falsely charged with
aiming at kingship, Gisco exiled, and the secret ballots against his
uncle Hamilcar, the general who had preferred to make Agathocles an ally
(22.7). Bomilcar's attempt and its failure are the clearest evidence of
the tension, which Diodorus had just described, between Carthage's
councils and its generals ([claim](/claims/agathocles-bomilcar-coup)).

## The collapse

After taking Utica, where he hung captured citizens on his siege engine
to stop the defenders shooting, and Hippou Acra, Agathocles went back to
Sicily in 307 to deal with the war there, leaving Archagathus in command
(20.54–55). In his absence the Carthaginian senate sent out three armies, to the
coast, the midlands and the interior; Archagathus split his own forces
to meet them, and two of his divisions were destroyed (20.59–61). Agathocles
returned, lost a battle, and on the following night both camps
panicked: the Carthaginians, sacrificing their finest prisoners as
thank-offerings, set their own camp on fire, and Agathocles' army, taking
the noise for an attack, fled in the dark and lost thousands (20.64–67).

His Libyan allies deserted him, and he could not ship his army home. He
planned to slip away with his younger son, Heracleides, leaving
Archagathus, whom he suspected of an affair with his stepmother;
Archagathus told the officers, and the soldiers put Agathocles in chains.
In a night panic his guards brought him out, the soldiers took pity and
freed him, and he sailed secretly for Sicily. When the army learned he
had gone it killed both his sons, chose its own generals, and made terms
with Carthage: the cities handed back, 300 talents paid, service in the
Carthaginian army for those who wanted it and a town in Sicily for the
rest. Those who held out were besieged, their leaders crucified and the
rest set to restore the farmland they had laid waste (Diodorus 20.68–69;
Justin 22.8).

## The peace

The peace of 306 gave Carthage back all the cities it had held in Sicily
before the war, and Carthage paid Agathocles gold to the value of 300
talents of silver (150, according to Timaeus) and 200,000 measures of
grain (Diodorus 20.79). Carthage had survived an invasion of its
homeland and its general's attempted coup, and still paid to be rid of
the man who had caused both. Agathocles took the title of king soon
after the Successors did, reigned until 289, and was preparing a new war
on Carthage when he died (Diodorus 21.16).

## What the campaign showed

It showed that a hostile army could land in Africa, live off the
country, and draw Libyan towns and Numidian princes away from Carthage;
the same weakness that the Mercenary War would expose from inside. It
also showed where Carthage was strong: its walls, its control of the sea,
which kept Agathocles from ever bringing his army home by ship, and its
ability to raise money and men while he could not. Rome's landing under
Regulus in 256 followed the same plan and failed in a similar way (see
[the African expedition and Regulus](/narratives/the-african-expedition-and-regulus)).
When Scipio argued in 205 for invading Africa, Livy has him answer Fabius
by pointing to Agathocles, who turned the war back to the country it
had come from (Livy 28.43). The speech is Livy's, but it shows the
precedent was part of the Roman argument.

Whether the invasion was a masterstroke or a gamble is weighed in the
[editorial take](/editorialTakes/agathocles-masterstroke-or-gamble).

## A note on sources

The narrative rests on Diodorus 19–20, with Justin 22 for a shorter
parallel that sometimes differs (on Ophellas, on Hamilcar's death) and
adds the Carthaginian politics behind Bomilcar. Diodorus says he could
not accept Timaeus's books on Agathocles because of their malice, and
calls Callias, the other main historian of the reign, a paid flatterer
(21.17); Polybius attacks Timaeus on the same score (12.15). Modern
accounts often call Diodorus's narrative Timaean all the same, and its
moral tone is hostile. The military narrative is the firmer part; the
characterizations of Agathocles, and of Carthage, need the usual
caution ([claim](/claims/agathocles-diodorus-bias)).
