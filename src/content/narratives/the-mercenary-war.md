## How it began

In the late summer and autumn of 241 BCE, the mercenary contingents
that had served the Carthaginian war effort in Sicily were
transferred back to Africa. Approximately 20,000 men of varied
origin arrived at Sicca (modern El Kef in Tunisia), an inland
Carthaginian-controlled town to which the contingents had been sent
to await pay and discharge. They included Iberians, Gauls,
Ligurians, Balearic islanders, Greeks, Italians (including Spendius,
an escaped Roman slave of Campanian origin), and Libyan auxiliaries.
The Carthaginian government's plan, normal in the period, was to
settle accumulated back pay and dismiss the contingents to their
home territories.

The plan failed. The Lutatius indemnity, especially as stiffened on
Roman ratification, had absorbed the cash flow that immediate full
settlement required. The Carthaginian senate sent Hanno the Great to
negotiate a reduced payment with the men at Sicca. The negotiations
produced exactly the wrong outcome: Hanno's offer was rejected, the
contingents marched on Tunis, and the dispute escalated into a
demand not just for full back pay but for additional concessions and
broader recognition of the men's grievances.

By the early winter of 241–240 BCE the rebellion had taken on a
character no Carthaginian commander had anticipated. Libyan
communities across the African hinterland joined the revolt, drawn
by long-running grievances over Carthaginian tribute, military
levies, and the use of Libyan agriculture to support the city's
imperial expenses. Polybius reports that women contributed jewelry
and personal savings to the rebel cause in numbers he treats as
remarkable. By 240 BCE the Carthaginian state faced not just a
mercenary mutiny but a substantive multi-ethnic revolt of its own
African hinterland, with the city's land communications cut and
several of its most important coastal positions, including Utica,
besieged.

## How it was fought

The early military campaigns under Hanno the Great produced minimal
Carthaginian progress. Hanno was a senator and political figure
rather than an experienced field commander, and his operations
were hampered by indecision and by his rivalry with other
Carthaginian factions. The senate transferred command to Hamilcar
Barca in 240 BCE.

Hamilcar's appointment is the structural turning point of the war.
He had spent the closing years of the First Punic War in Sicily as
the most successful Carthaginian field commander of that conflict,
and he brought the same operational style (rapid maneuver, deep
intelligence about local geography, willingness to engage on
unexpected terms) to the African theater. His first major
engagement, at the Bagradas River in 240 BCE, broke the rebel
forces in the field. His subsequent alliance with Naravas, the
Numidian Massylii prince who joined him with approximately 2,000
cavalry in 239 BCE (sealed by the marriage of one of Hamilcar's
daughters to Naravas), gave him the cavalry capability the rebels
could not match. Through 239 and 238 BCE he gradually contained,
then encircled, the principal rebel concentrations.

The war's climax came at the engagement Polybius calls "the Saw"
or the Defile, an encirclement-and-starvation operation in which
Hamilcar trapped approximately 40,000 rebel troops in a narrow
canyon and starved them until they surrendered or were destroyed.
The operation is one of the most ruthless in any Hamilcar source
and was executed against the leadership of Spendius, who was
captured. Spendius was crucified outside the walls of Tunis along
with the other captured rebel leaders.

The remaining rebellion under Mathos held out into 237 BCE around
Tunis and Utica, but with the principal rebel field force
destroyed at the Saw, the outcome was no longer in doubt. Mathos
was eventually captured and paraded through Carthage in a
ritualized public execution that Polybius preserves with
characteristic detail. The war ended in the spring of 237 BCE.

## Why it was savage

Polybius treats the war's brutality at length. Both sides used
torture, mutilation, and reciprocal atrocity beyond the standards
of contemporary Greek and Roman warfare. The pattern was
escalating: an early Carthaginian execution of captured rebels was
matched by rebel mutilation of Carthaginian prisoners, which was
matched in turn by a Carthaginian policy of crucifixion, which was
matched by further atrocities on the rebel side. By the end of
239 BCE the war's character had become genuinely "truceless" in the
sense Polybius's term suggests: no negotiated end was available
because the accumulated atrocities had foreclosed it.

Why this happened is contested. Polybius's account emphasizes the
leadership: Spendius's escaped-slave status (which gave him personal
reason to oppose any settlement that might return him to Roman
jurisdiction) and Mathos's particular hatred of Carthaginian rule.
Modern scholarship, particularly Hoyos (2007), is more willing to
read the brutality structurally: a multi-ethnic mercenary force
with no shared political identity plus an indigenous Libyan revolt
drawing on long-running colonial grievance plus a Carthaginian state
whose existence was at stake produced the conditions in which
atrocity escalated faster than political restraint could rebuild.

## What it meant

Three structural consequences shaped Carthaginian history for the
rest of the third century BCE.

Hamilcar's reputation. The Mercenary War made Hamilcar Barca's
military authority. The political capital his suppression of the
revolt produced was what authorized, and shortly thereafter
funded, the Iberian project he opened in 237 BCE. The Barcid
dominance of Carthaginian military and political life from 237 BCE
to Zama begins here.

Hanno's eclipse. The political rivalry between the Barcid
family and the senatorial faction around Hanno the Great, which
the Mercenary War inflamed, became one of the defining axes of
Carthaginian internal politics. Hanno's repeated subsequent
opposition to the Barcid Iberian project, and to Hannibal's
campaigns in Italy, was a structural consequence of the political
realignment the Mercenary War produced.

The Sardinian seizure. While Carthage was distracted, the
Sardinian mercenary garrison also revolted, and Rome took
advantage of the situation to seize Sardinia in 238 BCE on a
manufactured pretext. The act, technically a violation of the
Lutatius treaty's allied-protection clause, was something
Carthage was militarily incapable of resisting. Polybius (3.10,
3.27-28) treats the Sardinian seizure as the deepest cause of the
Second Punic War, and the seizure is properly read as a
consequence of the Mercenary War: without Carthaginian distraction
the Roman opportunity would not have arisen. The Mercenary War
ended in 237 BCE; the Iberian project began in the same year; the
arc that ends at Zama begins here.

## A note on the war's name

The Romans called it *bellum sine fide* ("the war without good
faith"), itself a translation of Polybius's Greek *aspondos
polemos* ("truceless war"). The latter term entered the modern
English-language tradition through translations of Polybius and
gives the war its standard alternative name. "Mercenary War" is
the more conventional modern label. Both reflect features of the
conflict the surviving sources emphasize: the mercenary
composition of one side, and the absence of negotiated settlement
that the war's brutality produced.
