## The least foregrounded brother

Mago is the third son of [Hamilcar Barca](/people/hamilcar-barca),
the youngest of the three Barcid brothers, and the figure
who appears in the surviving record mainly through moments
anchored by someone else. He commanded the African heavy
infantry that closed the trap at
[Cannae](/events/battle-of-cannae), but the battle belongs
to Hannibal in every account. He carried the news of the
victory back to Carthage and poured out a bushel of
equestrian rings at the senate floor, but the scene is the
senate debate between Hanno the Great and the Barcid
faction. He was one of three Carthaginian commanders at the
[Upper Baetis](/events/battle-of-the-upper-baetis) in 211
that destroyed the elder Scipios, but the engagement is
remembered as the prelude to
[Scipio Africanus's](/people/scipio-africanus) Iberian
command. He was at [Ilipa](/events/battle-of-ilipa) in 206,
but Ilipa is Scipio's tactical signature. He landed at Genoa
in 205 and operated in the Po valley for two years, but the
campaign never produced a battle the tradition cared to
remember. He died at sea in 203, but Zama the following year
swallowed the memory.

What the literary tradition does not foreground is that Mago
was the Barcid commander who held two theaters for Carthage
across the long stretch when no other Barcid field authority
was available. From Hasdrubal Barca's death at the
[Metaurus](/events/battle-of-metaurus) in 207 through to his
own death in 203, Mago was the only Barcid in the field
outside Hannibal's own southern Italian position. Those five
years are when Carthage's extra-peninsular military capacity
rested on him.

## Cannae and the senate mission (216 BCE)

At [Cannae](/events/battle-of-cannae), Mago commanded a wing
of the African heavy infantry — the formation that, after
the Roman center had pushed forward into the deliberately
yielding Spanish-Celtic line, pivoted inward against the
Roman flanks and closed the encirclement. The conventional
modern reading treats the African infantry as a held-back
reserve committed at the decisive moment. Lazenby and Daly
read it more as wings deployed alongside the center that
turned in as the Roman advance created the opening. Either
way, the timing of that movement is what determined whether
the deliberate yield in the Carthaginian center produced a
controlled encirclement or a collapse. Polybius 3.114 names
Mago among the commanders; Livy 22.46 gives a parallel
account. The encirclement that followed produced something
like 50,000 Roman dead and the most complete tactical
envelopment in the surviving record of ancient warfare.

Hannibal sent Mago back to Carthage with the news. The
mission included the famous gesture of pouring out a bushel
of equestrian rings, taken from the Roman dead, at the senate
floor (Livy 23.11–13). The senate debate that followed,
between Hanno the Great and the Barcid faction, is the
best-documented moment of Carthaginian wartime politics that
survives. Reinforcements were granted, but smaller than what
Hannibal had asked for. Mago acted in this mission as a
political figure as well as a field commander; the
[Barcid Iberian state narrative](/narratives/the-barcid-iberian-state)
treats this fusion of command and senate-faction
representation as a recurring feature of how the family
operated.

## The Iberian command (215–206 BCE)

After the mission, Mago joined
[Hasdrubal Barca](/people/hasdrubal-barca) and
[Hasdrubal Gisco](/people/hasdrubal-gisco) in Iberia. The
three commanders divided the theater by region: Hasdrubal
Barca held the northeast against Roman operations along the
Ebro, Hasdrubal Gisco the south, Mago the center and the
southern interior. The arrangement worked for several years.
In 211 they destroyed the elder Scipios at the
[Upper Baetis](/events/battle-of-the-upper-baetis), the
highest point of Carthaginian fortunes in Iberia and one of
Mago's named engagements. Then Scipio Africanus took over
the Roman command in 210, and the Carthaginian position
began coming apart.

The defeats at [Baecula](/events/battle-of-baecula) in 208
and [Ilipa](/events/battle-of-ilipa) in 206 mark the
deterioration. Baecula loosed Hasdrubal Barca for the
Italian crossing that ended at the Metaurus. Ilipa was the
end of the Iberian theater as a viable Carthaginian
position. Mago was at both. After Ilipa, with what was left
of his force, he withdrew to the Balearic Islands and
wintered there through 205, preparing the next move. The
Balearic interval matters: with the Iberian command
structure gone and senate guidance reaching him slowly, the
decision to open a northern Italian front with the residual
force was substantially Mago's own.

## The northern Italian campaign (205–203 BCE)

Mago's last command was the most ambitious Carthaginian
initiative of the war's closing years: a third front in
northern Italy, opened by landing at Genoa and rallying the
Ligurian and Cisalpine Gallic populations against Rome. The
idea was to compel Rome to pull forces from Africa, where
Scipio was by then operating, and to ease the pressure on
Hannibal still in the south. Mago landed in 205, took Genoa,
and operated in the Po valley for nearly two years,
gathering Ligurian and Gallic auxiliaries and engaging Roman
forces in smaller actions without forcing a major battle.

Whether the campaign was a useful strategic move or a waste
of Carthage's last field force is genuinely contested in the
record. It did keep some Roman forces tied down in the
north. It did not bring relief to Hannibal, and it did not
divert Rome from Africa. The sources (Livy 28–30, Appian
*Hannibalica* 49–54) do not let the question be settled.
What is clear is that it cost Carthage a substantial
fighting force at a moment when the senate had no settled
plan for what was needed in Africa.

The campaign ended at the
[Battle of the Insubres](/events/battle-of-the-insubres-203)
in the late summer of 203, where the consul Quinctilius
Varus and the praetor Cornelius Cethegus defeated Mago in a
pitched battle. Mago was wounded — the sources do not
specify the wound but call it serious. By then the
Carthaginian senate had already recalled him to defend
Africa against Scipio. He embarked from the Ligurian coast
with what he had left and died at sea on the voyage south,
never reaching Carthage.

## Wider operational context

Mago's Iberian command coincided with other extra-peninsular
moves the Carthaginian senate authorized. Livy 23.40–41
records a 215 BCE expedition to Sardinia under Hasdrubal
"the Bald" (a different Hasdrubal from the Barcid), meant to
exploit Sardinian discontent with Roman rule and recover the
island Carthage had lost in the 238 post-Mercenary-War
crisis. The Roman praetor Titus Manlius Torquatus defeated
it. Mago had no attested role. The episode is worth flagging
only because it shows what the Carthaginian senate was
willing to try while Mago was in Iberia, and because the
Sardinian seizure of 238 itself, treated under the
[Mercenary War narrative](/narratives/the-mercenary-war), is
one of the long-running grievances shaping Barcid
orientation toward Rome.

His death in 203, combined with Hasdrubal Barca's death at
the Metaurus four years earlier, left Hannibal without
Barcid field-command support during the African endgame.
Hannibal at [Zama](/events/battle-of-zama) was the last
Barcid in the field. The narrowing of the family's command
structure to a single point of failure is part of the
condition under which the African defeat happened.
