## What survives

The text of the treaty survives only in Polybius, *Histories* 3.22,
in a Greek translation he prepared from what he describes as the
archaic Latin original preserved in the Roman archives. Polybius
includes the treaty in a methodologically careful excursus on the
pre-264 Roman-Carthaginian relationship, partly to address the
historiographic disputes that Philinus of Akragas's lost
pro-Carthaginian history had raised about Roman conduct in the
First Punic War (a topic treated separately on this site at the
First Punic War outbreak event).

The text is short. Its principal provisions:

- Roman ships are not to sail beyond a navigational landmark Polybius
  calls the Fair Promontory (Καλοῦ Ἀκρωτηρίου), with exceptions for
  storm or pursuit by enemies.
- Roman traders may visit Carthage and Carthaginian-controlled
  territories in Africa and Sicily, but must conduct transactions
  through prescribed officials and may not buy land or settle.
- The Carthaginians undertake not to harm the Latin allies of Rome,
  particularly the named cities of Latium that Polybius lists.
- Both sides promise not to recruit troops in the other's territory
  and not to attack ports or strongholds belonging to the other.

What is striking about these terms is the asymmetry. The substantive
restrictions fall on Rome: navigational, territorial, commercial.
The Carthaginian commitments are limited and largely passive (not
to harm Latin allies, not to attack Roman strongholds). The treaty
is the kind of document a major regional power would impose on a
smaller city emerging into independent foreign relations.

## What this implies

By the conventional 509 BCE dating, this is the year of Rome's
transition from monarchy to republic. Roman external relations were
being renegotiated in the wake of the expulsion of the kings.
Carthage by contrast was already a major Phoenician-colonial power,
the principal western successor to Tyre's commercial network, with
trading relationships across the central and western Mediterranean
basins. The 509 treaty captures the relationship at a moment of
maximum Carthaginian-over-Roman asymmetry.

Three centuries of subsequent development would close the gap. The
348 BCE renewal preserves the same general framework with
elaborated terms; the 279/278 Pyrrhic-War-period treaty represents
both states cooperating against a common threat as approximate
equals; the 264 BCE Mamertine crisis breaks the framework entirely
when Rome crosses the Strait of Messina with a field army. Each
treaty in the sequence is closer to parity than the last. The 509
document is the starting point of that long convergence.

## What the dating dispute is about

Theodor Mommsen, in the 19th century, advanced what is sometimes
called the doublet hypothesis: that Polybius's "first" treaty is
actually a duplicate of the 348 BCE treaty, with the 509 dating
either an error of Polybius or a Roman archival fiction. The
hypothesis rests on the substantial overlap in provisions between
the 509 and 348 documents (Polybius preserves both), Polybius's
contested archival access, and what Mommsen treated as the
implausibility of the political geography for the late 6th
century BCE.

The hypothesis is defensible but not generally accepted today.
Polybius dates the treaty by reference to the consuls of the year
(Brutus and Horatius, recorded in the Roman consular fasti) and
cross-references the chronology to Xerxes' crossing into Greece
(480 BCE), a fixed Greek-historiographic marker. The treaty
overlap with the 348 document is consistent with renewal of an
earlier framework rather than with duplication. The
political-geographic objection has lost force as the broader
chronology of Phoenician western expansion has clarified.

Modern scholarship generally accepts Polybius's chronology while
acknowledging that the dating is not independently verifiable. The
site adopts this position: the 509 date is the best-supported
reading of the available evidence, and its acceptance is the
working assumption, but the alternative is not refuted.

## What we do not know

A number of basic questions about the treaty remain unanswered.
The location of the Fair Promontory is contested between several
candidate identifications (Cape Bon being the most widely accepted,
but not securely established). The specific procedural arrangements
for Roman traders at Carthage are not preserved in the surviving
text. The Carthaginian and Roman officials who actually negotiated
the document are not named in any source. The conditions under
which the treaty was renewed in 348 BCE, and how those renewal
discussions related to its earlier text, are imperfectly known.

What remains is the document itself, in the form Polybius preserved
it, as the earliest substantial evidence of the Roman-Carthaginian
relationship that would, two and a half centuries later, transform
into the longest and most decisive war the western Mediterranean
had yet seen.

## A note on sources

The treaty's content depends entirely on Polybius. No parallel
preservation of the text survives in Diodorus, Livy, or any other
ancient source. Modern scholarship on the document is therefore
substantially the modern scholarship on Polybius's archival access,
his translation choices, and his chronological reasoning. The
principal modern treatments are in Hoyos's *Unplanned Wars* (1998),
Aubet's *The Phoenicians and the West* (2nd ed. 2001), and the
relevant chapters of Hoyos's *The Carthaginians* (2010).
