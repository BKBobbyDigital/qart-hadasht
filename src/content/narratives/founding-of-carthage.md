## The literary tradition

The fullest preserved version of the foundation story comes from
Justin's epitome of Pompeius Trogus, Book 18, written probably
in the 2nd or 3rd century CE on the basis of Trogus's
1st-century-BCE history (which itself drew on earlier
Greek-historiographic sources, ultimately including Timaeus of
Tauromenium in the early 3rd century BCE). The narrative as
Justin preserves it runs as follows.

In Tyre, the death of King Mattan left the kingdom to be ruled
jointly by his son Pygmalion and his daughter Elissa (Dido was
her Latin-Greek name; Elissa, Punic ʾLŠT, the underlying
Phoenician form). Pygmalion married Elissa to Sychaeus, a wealthy
priest of Melqart. Coveting Sychaeus's treasure, Pygmalion had
him murdered. Elissa, learning of the murder through a dream,
loaded the treasure secretly onto ships and fled Tyre with a
band of followers. They stopped briefly in Cyprus, where they
took aboard a number of women to provide brides for the
expedition. They then sailed west to North Africa.

At a hilltop site on the African coast (the future location of
Carthage), Elissa negotiated with the local Libyan inhabitants
for land. The Libyans offered her as much land as could be
covered by an ox-hide. Elissa accepted, then cut the hide into
thin strips and stretched them around a substantial area, the
hill that became the citadel of Carthage and was thereafter known
as the Byrsa, from the Greek for "ox-hide." On this hill the
city was founded.

Justin's account ends with Elissa's death. The local Libyan king
Iarbas demanded marriage; Elissa, having sworn fidelity to the
murdered Sychaeus, threw herself onto a pyre rather than accept.
The traditional foundation date is 814 BCE, given by Timaeus
and preserved in this calendrical form through Justin and
Velleius Paterculus.

Virgil's *Aeneid*, written in Augustan Rome eight centuries after
the events, replaced Iarbas with Aeneas and produced the
romantic-tragic version of Dido's death that has dominated
European literary reception since. The Virgilian version is
unambiguously a literary development for Augustan purposes;
Aeneas (a figure of the Trojan War tradition, four hundred years
earlier) and Dido (a figure of the foundation tradition) cannot
have met. Virgil compresses the chronology for the symbolic
purpose of staging Roman-Carthaginian conflict's origins as a
spurned-lover narrative. The earlier Iarbas tradition is the
older and historically more relevant version.

## The Punic-script question

In 1981 the Phoenician linguist Charles Krahmalkov published an
article in the *Journal of Semitic Studies* arguing that an
inscribed gold pendant from the Douïmès cemetery at Carthage
provided Punic-script evidence consistent with the traditional
814 BCE foundation date. The pendant had been recovered in late
19th-century French excavations and bears a brief, partly damaged
inscription. Krahmalkov's reading proposed a personal name and a
relative date that, given the pendant's archaeological context,
would push the cemetery's early phase into the early 9th century
BCE.

The reading is contested. Subsequent philological work has
proposed alternative readings of the relevant characters, and the
archaeological context (a 19th-century excavation of variable
stratigraphic precision) is hard to verify against later
evidence. Most modern scholarship treats the Krahmalkov reading
as a serious minority position rather than an established
consensus. It is nevertheless the most substantial attempt to
bring Punic-script evidence to bear on the foundation chronology
and the closest thing we have to a contemporary Carthaginian
attestation of an early date.

## What archaeology says

The earliest material-culture horizon recoverable at Carthage
dates to approximately 750-720 BCE. The evidence is broadly
consistent across the modern excavation campaigns of the late
20th and early 21st centuries: ceramic finds, structural remains,
and tomb contexts indicate continuous Phoenician occupation from
the late 8th century BCE onward.

This is approximately a century later than the traditional 814
BCE foundation date. The gap is the central fact about which the
modern chronological dispute organizes itself. Three readings
are defensible.

**The literary date is approximately correct.** Carthage was
founded around 814 BCE; the archaeological gap reflects the
limits of what can be recovered from a site continuously occupied
for 2,800 years and substantially destroyed-and-rebuilt in 146
BCE. Krahmalkov's reading, if accepted, supports this view.

**The literary date is symbolic.** Timaeus calculated backward
from later evidence (perhaps from a known Tyrian regnal
sequence) to produce a foundation date that gave Carthage
suitably ancient origins. The actual continuous urban
foundation occurred in the late 8th century BCE and corresponds
to the recovered material-culture horizon.

**The tradition compresses an extended foundation process.**
Carthage as a continuous urban settlement dates to the late 8th
century, but earlier Phoenician seasonal or trading installations
may have existed at the site for decades or longer before being
consolidated into a permanent foundation. The 814 date marks the
symbolic beginning of the longer process, the late 8th-century
archaeology its consolidation.

The site treats the question as genuinely open. None of the three
readings is foreclosed by the available evidence.

## The Tyrian connection

What is not seriously contested is the Tyrian-colonial frame.
Carthage was founded by Phoenicians from Tyre in the broad
chronological window of the late 9th to late 8th century BCE.
This frame is firmly attested in the literary tradition, in the
broader archaeology of Phoenician western expansion, and in the
sustained Carthaginian-Tyrian relationship that persisted across
centuries. Carthaginian state delegations made annual offerings
at the Tyrian sanctuary of Melqart well into the Punic Wars
period. The Carthaginian-Tyrian connection was treated by both
cities as one of religious and ancestral continuity, not mere
historical origin.

Carthage was also not the only such foundation. The Tyrian
western expansion of the 9th and 8th centuries BCE was a
substantively integrated network enterprise — Gadir on the
Atlantic, Lixus on the Moroccan coast, Motya on Sicily, Sulci
and Tharros on Sardinia, the broader Tunisian coastal foundations,
others — operating with shared religious-cultic infrastructure
(principally the Melqart cult and the annual Tyrian
delegations), shared commercial conventions, and shared
linguistic-inscriptional practice. The
[Phoenician colonial network theme](/themes/phoenician-colonial-network)
treats this network as the structural context Carthage emerged
within. Across the early centuries Carthage was one node among
many; through demographic accumulation, geographic position,
and the homeland's Assyrian-period constraint of the 8th-7th
centuries BCE, Carthage grew across the 6th-5th centuries BCE
into the network's metropolitan center. The foundation was the
beginning of a long network-and-emergence arc rather than the
isolated colonial-founding moment the legendary tradition
foregrounds.

This is the foundation tradition's secure historical core. The
specific date is debated; the figure of Dido is legendary; the
narrative details are stylized. The Tyrian colonial event itself
is not in dispute.

## How to read it

The standard either-or framing (was it history or legend?) is the
wrong question. The Carthaginian foundation tradition is *both*.
It preserves a real historical event in a literary form that has
been shaped by centuries of retransmission. The Tyrian colonial
foundation is the historical core; the 814 BCE date is the
memorialized symbolic chronology; the Dido figure is the
legendary focal point around which the foundation memory
organized itself; the Virgilian elaboration is the literary
afterlife in which the tradition continued to develop.

What is genuinely informative about the tradition is not whether
its specific elements are true but what its existence and
transmission tell us. That Carthage preserved a foundation
narrative centered on a Tyrian princess for centuries, that
Carthaginian state ceremony reaffirmed the Tyrian connection
annually, that the *byrsa* etymology persisted whether or not it
was the actual origin of the place-name: these are facts about
how Carthage understood itself as a colonial city of Phoenician
origin. The foundation tradition is evidence about Carthaginian
self-conception. Whether Dido literally existed is a different
and probably less important question than what the tradition
that crystallized around her preserves.

## A note on sources

The principal preserved literary sources are Justin's epitome of
Trogus (the fullest preserved narrative), Diodorus Siculus
(fragmentary), and Virgil's Aeneid (literary elaboration, not
historical evidence). The traditional 814 BCE date traces back
through Velleius Paterculus to Timaeus of Tauromenium. The
modern archaeological synthesis is in Aubet 2001, Lancel 1995,
and MacDonald 2015; the Punic-epigraphic argument for the early
date is in Krahmalkov 1981. The reception history of the Dido
tradition is large enough to be a separate field; the present
narrative covers only the foundation tradition as it relates to
the foundation event itself.
