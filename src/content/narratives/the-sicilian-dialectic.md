The history of Carthaginian Sicily between Himera and Agathocles
is usually told in pieces. The Magonid disaster of 480, the
revenge campaign of 410–405, Dionysius the Elder's wars, the
plague at the siege of Syracuse, Timoleon, the Krimisos, then
Agathocles. Treated as a continuous arc the seven decades of war
have a recognizable internal shape: two well-matched powers
testing the limits of what either could impose on the other, and
producing in the end a partition neither side had set out to
create. The Halycus boundary that defined western Punic Sicily
from the early fourth century onward was a negotiated result of
operational stalemate, and the equilibrium it expressed was the
political ground on which the Pyrrhic and First Punic Wars
eventually broke.

## The seventy-year silence

The defeat at [Himera](/events/battle-of-himera) in 480 BCE was
catastrophic but not strategically terminal. Carthage held its
western Sicilian footing (Panormus, [Motya](/places/motya), Solus,
the Elymian inland cities at Eryx and Segesta), held Sardinia,
and held the African heartland. What it did not do, for the next
seventy years, was return to offensive operations in Sicily. The
sources for the period are thin, but the broad picture is clear
enough: a generation of internal consolidation, the development
of the city's African and Sardinian agricultural and tributary
base, and the absence of any major Carthaginian field army
crossing the Sicilian channel.

The Greek-Sicilian side of the silence is more legible. Gelon's
Syracuse, then Hieron's, then the post-tyrannical Syracusan
republic, then the long fifth-century rivalry with Akragas and
the southern Greek cities, kept the Greek-Sicilian political world
absorbed in its own internal contests. The Athenian expedition
of 415–413 BCE intervened and was destroyed. By the time
Carthaginian operations resumed in 410, the Greek-Sicilian
political landscape had reshaped substantially without Carthage
having played any direct role in the reshaping.

## The revenge campaigns, 410–405

The Carthaginian return was triggered by an appeal from Segesta,
the Elymian city in western Sicily, against its long-standing
neighbor Selinus. Diodorus (13.43) is the principal source and
makes the dynastic logic explicit: the commander Carthage
appointed, [Hannibal son of Gisco](/people/hannibal-son-of-gisco),
was the grandson of the Hamilcar killed at Himera, and the
campaign was framed inside Carthage as a vindication of the
family's earlier loss. Whether the framing reflects the actual
political logic at Carthage or is a Greek narrative imposition is
not recoverable; either way, the campaign was massive.

The 409 expedition took
[Selinus](/events/sack-of-selinus-409) and
[Himera](/events/sack-of-himera-409) in the same campaigning
season. Both cities were sacked thoroughly, the populations
killed or enslaved, the sites largely abandoned. The destruction
of Himera was symbolically pointed (the city where the Magonid
defeat had occurred was now itself destroyed by the same
family's command), and Diodorus records that Hannibal son of
Gisco performed sacrifices at the site of his grandfather's death.

The 406 expedition under
[Himilco](/people/himilco) and Hannibal son of Gisco moved
against [Akragas](/events/siege-of-akragas-406), the wealthiest
Greek city in Sicily after Syracuse, and took it after a long
siege in which Hannibal son of Gisco died of plague in the
Carthaginian camp. The capture of Akragas opened the southern
coast and brought the Carthaginian army within striking distance
of Syracuse itself. The Carthaginian campaign continued through
405, taking Gela and Camarina, before plague in the besieging
army (a recurrence of the disease that had killed Hannibal son of
Gisco) forced Himilco to accept a negotiated settlement with the
newly-installed Syracusan tyrant [Dionysius I](/people/dionysius-i).

The 405 settlement is the first treaty whose terms the surviving
sources let us reconstruct in any detail. Carthage gained
recognition of its sovereignty over Selinus, Akragas, Gela, and
Camarina (the cities it had taken in 409–405, all of which were
to be tributary to Carthage and unfortified). The Elymian and
Sican cities retained their autonomy under Carthaginian
oversight. Syracuse was confirmed as the dominant Greek-Sicilian
power, with Dionysius as tyrant. The fourth-century pattern of
Carthaginian western and southern Sicilian dominance, with
Syracuse confronting it across an uncertain boundary, was set
in this settlement.

## Dionysius and the long contest

Dionysius spent the next thirty-eight years (405–367) testing
whether the 405 settlement could be reversed. The first
counterattack, the
[Second Sicilian War](/events/second-sicilian-war) of 398–392
BCE, opened with the
[siege of Motya](/events/siege-of-motya-397) in 398–397, the
first time Dionysius's siegecraft (which he had specifically
developed for this purpose, with attested innovations in
torsion artillery and siege towers) was brought to bear on the
Carthaginian Sicilian network. Motya fell after a long siege and
was sacked. Himilco's relief expedition the following year
recovered the western Sicilian position and pressed eastward,
reaching the walls of Syracuse before plague again ravaged the
Carthaginian besieging army. Himilco negotiated his own withdrawal
and the disgrace of the failure reportedly drove him to suicide
on his return to Carthage (Diodorus 14.76).

The pattern repeated in the Third (383–376 BCE) and Fourth
(368–367 BCE) Sicilian Wars. Each was a Dionysian initiative to
push the Carthaginian zone back; each produced sharp tactical
reversals on both sides; each ended in negotiated settlement
without strategic resolution. The Halycus river, running south
from the north coast to the south coast roughly through the
center of the island, emerged across these wars as the
operational boundary between the Carthaginian and Syracusan
zones. Dionysius died in 367 with the partition essentially as
it had been left in 405, displaced and recovered repeatedly but
not fundamentally shifted.

The fact of the partition is the load-bearing thing. Neither
power could push the other off the island; both retained the
capacity to inflict severe damage; the boundary moved with
operational fortunes but recurred to roughly the same line
across multiple wars. The structural condition was a balance
that the surviving Greco-Roman literary tradition tends to
under-read, because the Greek sources are organized around
Dionysius's offensive and the Carthaginian sources are lost.
Read against the actual outcomes, the four wars under Dionysius
look less like a Greek attempt at liberation that failed
narrowly and more like a sustained test of a partition that the
operational facts kept enforcing.

## Timoleon and the Krimisos

The 340s brought a different kind of pressure. The Syracusan
political crisis under Dionysius II had produced a chaotic
succession of tyrants and counter-tyrants across central and
eastern Sicily, and the Corinthian general
[Timoleon](/people/timoleon), invited by a Syracusan opposition
faction in 344, set about restoring Greek political order across
the island. The Carthaginian response, a major expedition in
341 under a commander named Hasdrubal (the patronymic is not
preserved), met Timoleon at the
[Krimisos](/events/battle-of-crimisus) and was heavily defeated.

The Krimisos is reported (Plutarch *Timoleon* 25–29, Diodorus
16.79–80) as a Greek victory on the Himera scale, with very
heavy Carthaginian losses and the capture of substantial
treasure that Timoleon dedicated at Corinth. The narrative is
shaped by Plutarch's biographical interest in Timoleon as a
moral exemplar and by the Sicilian-Greek tradition's interest in
remembering a final Greek victory in the long contest. Read
against the actual outcome, however, the Krimisos did not
fundamentally shift the partition either. The peace settlement
that followed (around 339 or 338 BCE) confirmed the Halycus
boundary essentially as it had been: Carthage retained the
western Sicilian zone, Timoleon's restored Syracuse and the
allied Greek cities held the central and eastern zones. The
victory was real and the Carthaginian losses were heavy; the
partition held.

## What the seven decades produced

By the time Agathocles seized the Syracusan tyranny in 317 BCE
and the war that opened in 311 began, the Carthaginian-Syracusan
relationship had developed an institutional pattern that
neither side regarded as anomalous. Carthage held the western
third of Sicily through a network of fortified cities
([Lilybaeum](/places/lilybaeum), founded after Motya's
destruction in 397, was the principal new establishment;
Panormus, Drepana, Eryx, Solus, Heraclea Minoa held the rest of
the western frontier). The Halycus boundary was an established
geographical reference point. The Greek-Sicilian political world
operated through a coalition logic in which Syracuse was the
hegemon and the smaller cities aligned with or against the
hegemon according to circumstance. Carthaginian diplomacy
operated through the same coalition logic from the other side,
playing the smaller Greek cities against Syracuse when the
opportunity arose.

The structural fact of the partition is what the Agathocles
war (311–306) and the
[invasion of Africa](/narratives/agathocles-invasion-of-africa)
ultimately tested and failed to overturn. The 306 settlement
confirmed the partition again. The pattern broke not because one
of the two Sicilian powers eventually prevailed over the other
but because a third power, Rome, intervened in 264 in the
Mamertine affair at Messana and opened a war neither Carthage
nor Syracuse had wanted with each other.

Treating the seven decades as a single arc clarifies what the
First Punic War actually disrupted. It disrupted a Sicilian
political order that had developed across three generations and
that, from the inside, looked stable. The Roman intervention
read this stability as a Carthaginian power-projection problem
that needed to be solved; the surviving Greco-Roman literary
tradition has largely accepted the Roman framing. The Sicilian
Greek and Carthaginian record, read on its own terms, suggests
something different: a long partition that produced its own
political and cultural institutions and that was destabilized
from outside, not from within.

## Sources and the framing problem

The principal narrative source for the whole period is Diodorus
Siculus, writing in the late first century BCE and drawing
substantially on the fourth-century Sicilian historians Philistus
(a contemporary of Dionysius) and Timaeus (writing in exile from
Sicily in the early third century). Plutarch's *Timoleon* covers
the 340s. Herodotus 7.165–167 is the principal source for the
Magonid expedition; Thucydides has scattered references in his
Sicilian books. Aristotle's *Politics* II.11 treats the
Carthaginian constitution as comparable to the best Greek
constitutions, which suggests something about how the fourth-century
Greek political world thought about Carthage as a peer.

The framing problem the sources collectively present is that
they are Greek (and, in Diodorus's case, Greek-via-Roman) and
they organize the narrative around Greek protagonists. The
Carthaginian commanders enter as antagonists; their internal
political and military logic is reconstructed at second hand.
What the Carthaginian record of the same events would have
looked like is irrecoverable. The shape this site gives to the
seven decades attempts to read against the Greek framing where
the framing's distortions are visible (the systematic
exaggeration of Carthaginian numbers, the moralizing of
Carthaginian generals, the treatment of stalemate as Greek
near-victory). It does not pretend to recover the Carthaginian
side of the record. That side is lost.

What it does try to recover is the structural shape of the
period: a stable two-power partition that the Greek sources
present as a long series of Greek near-misses and that, read
against the operational facts, looks rather more like an
equilibrium neither side managed to break.

Modern reference treatment of the Greek military side of this
dialectic is Rawlings's
[*Ancient Greeks at War*](/sources/rawlings-ancient-greeks-at-war-2007),
a survey of Greek warfare from the Mycenaean period through
Alexander. The chapters on mercenary recruitment, fourth-century
professionalization, and siegecraft are particularly relevant
to the Sicilian theater Carthage operated in: the Greek
mercenary economy of the late classical period is the
recruitment context behind both Carthaginian and Sicilian-Greek
operations across the seven decades, and the fourth-century
Hellenistic military transformation that Rawlings traces on the
Greek side is the structural background to the Carthaginian
army of the same period. Rawlings also pushes back against the
once-standard reading that distinguished "citizen" Greek
warfare sharply from "mercenary" Hellenistic warfare — a
comparative-anachronism that has done equivalent damage on the
Carthaginian side of the analysis, where Polybius's later
"mercenary inferiority" framing has obscured the actual
institutional shape of Carthaginian field armies.
