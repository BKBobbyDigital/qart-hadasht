This page is about Rome, on a site about Carthage, and it is worth saying
at the outset why it is here. The reason is not balance. It is that the
surviving accounts of the Second Punic War were written to explain a
Roman victory, and an account built to explain a victory is not the same
thing as an account of what the victory cost. Polybius stops his
narrative in Book 6 to set out the Roman constitution as the reason Rome
survived Cannae. Livy reads the refusal to ransom the prisoners as proof
of the character that won the war. Both are making an argument, and
both are good sources precisely because the argument is visible. Neither
is trying to add up the damage, and the habit of reading them for the
story they wanted to tell has left the impression that the winner walked
away intact.

## The count

The scale of the Roman loss is the part the sources do record, because
the disasters were the drama. They disagree on the figures in the way
they usually do. Polybius puts the Roman dead at Cannae at around
seventy thousand; Livy gives a little over forty-eight thousand killed
with a further large number captured. The site treats the totals as
[contested and the order of magnitude as secure](/claims/cannae-casualties):
tens of thousands of men in a single afternoon, from a citizen body that
the census of 233 counted at roughly two hundred and seventy thousand
adult males.

Cannae was one day of a seventeen-year war that also included Trebia,
Trasimene, and the destruction of both consular armies at Cannae's scale
in Iberia in 211. By the census taken as the war closed, the recorded
figure had fallen to around two hundred and fourteen thousand. Census
returns are difficult evidence, since what the Republic was counting and
how completely it managed to count it during a war are both disputed, and
[Brunt's reconstruction](/sources/brunt-italian-manpower-1971) remains the
standard attempt to work out what the numbers will bear. Read
conservatively they still describe a state that lost something close to a
fifth of its citizen manpower and kept fighting anyway. The proportion of
Roman adult males under arms in the worst years has no parallel in the
pre-modern record.

## The land

The other cost is one the Roman sources mention constantly without ever
totalling, because it was too ordinary to itemise. Hannibal spent fifteen
years in Italy. He did not spend them besieging Rome; he spent them
moving through the Italian countryside, living off it, and fighting for
the allegiance of the communities in it. Bruttium, Lucania, Apulia and
Campania absorbed most of that, year after year, from both armies at
once, because a Roman force shadowing Hannibal fed itself the same way he
did.

Then there was what Rome did to the communities that changed sides.
Capua went over after Cannae and was retaken in 211. Its leading men were
executed, its self-government was abolished, and its territory was taken
into public ownership. Tarentum was stormed in 209 and its population
sold. These were not incidental reprisals but a deliberate policy of
making defection unsurvivable, and the confiscated land is the piece with
the longest tail: a great deal of southern Italy entered the Roman public
domain in these years, and the question of who was farming it became the
central political quarrel of the following century.

## Paying for it

Rome ran out of money. The war tax was doubled in 215. By 210 the
treasury was borrowing from private citizens, and the repayment, when it
finally came, was made in instalments, the last of which was settled
partly in public land rather than cash. The bronze coinage was reduced in
weight repeatedly as the emergency wore on, and the currency system was
rebuilt around a new silver coin, the denarius, introduced during the war
itself. A state does not redesign its money in the middle of a war for
any reason other than that the old arrangement has stopped working.

The indemnity Carthage paid after 201 has a section on this site of its
own, and the [terms of the settlement](/claims/treaty-201-terms) are
usually read, correctly, as a burden imposed on the defeated. It is worth
noticing that Rome needed it.

## What the emergency taught the Republic

The most consequential damage was to the way the Republic worked, and it
did not look like damage at the time. Fighting in Italy, Sicily, Iberia,
and eventually Africa at once required commanders in the field for years
on end, which meant extending commands far beyond the annual term the
system was built around. Prorogation stopped being exceptional. In 210
the command in Iberia went to a man of about twenty-five who had held no
qualifying office, on the strength of his name and the fact that nobody
else wanted the job. Scipio justified the choice completely. He also
established that the Republic would, under enough pressure, hand
extraordinary power to an individual because he was the right individual,
which is a precedent that outlived the emergency that produced it.

The aristocracy that came out of the war had also learned that foreign
command was the fastest route to standing at home, and that conquest paid.
The anxiety this produced is visible in the decades after: the Senate's
suppression of the Bacchanalia in 186, Cato's censorship in 184 and the
moralising campaign that went with it, the recurring complaint that
wealth from abroad was corrupting Roman life. It is easy to read that
literature as conventional grumbling. It is at least as plausible to read
it as a governing class noticing that its own society had changed
underneath it and not being able to say why.

## The argument about the aftermath

Where this stops being description and becomes a real dispute is the
question of what all that added up to. The strong version belongs to
[Toynbee](/sources/toynbee-hannibals-legacy-1965), who argued that the
war ruined the Italian smallholding peasantry, that slave-worked estates
filled the vacuum, and that the land crisis the Gracchi failed to solve
two generations later is Hannibal's doing at one remove. It is a clean
mechanism and it held the field for a long time.

[Rosenstein](/sources/rosenstein-rome-at-war-2004) has made the most
serious case against it, working from demography rather than from the
literary tradition. His argument is that Rome conscripted mainly young
unmarried men, whose absence a farm household could absorb, that the
rural population recovered rather than collapsed, and that the later
agrarian crisis owes more to the long overseas deployments that came
after the war than to the devastation during it.

Neither position denies the cost. They disagree about the causation, and
the disagreement is unresolved. The site treats it as an
[open question](/openQuestions/hannibalic-war-cost-to-rome) rather than
picking a winner, because the evidence genuinely does not settle it.

## Why this belongs here

The Rome that voted to destroy Carthage in 146 was not the Rome that
Hannibal invaded in 218. It had acquired a large public domain in
southern Italy and an unresolved argument about it, a political class
whose careers ran through foreign commands, and a demonstrated
willingness to end a city's existence as a community when that city had
proved it could not be trusted. Capua was the rehearsal.

This site argues elsewhere that Carthage was destroyed
[because it had not become weak enough](/editorialTakes/destruction-not-weak-enough),
and that reading stands. But it describes one side of a relationship. The
other side is a state that had been hurt badly enough, half a century
earlier, to have lost its appetite for arrangements that depended on a
rival's continued good behaviour. The virtue narrative in Polybius and
Livy explains why Rome survived. It does not explain what surviving did
to Rome, and the answer to that is part of why Carthage did not.
