A general on campaign does not usually bring historians. Hannibal
brought at least two, and the fact is easy to pass over because so
little came of it. Cornelius Nepos names them: Sosylus of Sparta and
Silenus of Caleacte, who wrote up Hannibal's deeds and stayed with him,
in the phrase that survives, as long as fortune allowed. Polybius adds
a third name, Chaereas, about whom nothing else is known at all.

What these men produced was not a Carthaginian literature of the war.
It was a Greek one, written by Greeks, for readers who read Greek. But
it was written from inside the tent, by people who had access to the
commander and an interest in his account of things, and that makes it
the closest approach to the Carthaginian side that the ancient world
ever produced. Its near-total disappearance is the single largest gap
in the evidence for the war.

## The circle

[Sosylus](/sources/sosylus-sparta) is remembered as the man who taught
Hannibal Greek, and wrote seven books on his deeds. That detail about
the language lessons deserves more attention than it usually gets. A
Carthaginian aristocrat learning Greek from a Spartan, and then keeping
that Spartan with him through fifteen years in Italy, is not a man
picking up a useful skill. It is consistent with the reading the site
sets out under
[Carthage as cultural integrator](/editorialTakes/carthage-as-cultural-integrator):
Hellenistic forms adopted deliberately and on Punic terms, by people
who knew exactly what they were adopting.

[Silenus](/sources/silenus-caleacte) came from Kale Akte on the north
coast of Sicily, which is to say from the contested zone where Punic
and Greek Sicily met, and he is the more consequential of the two
because of what became of his work.

**Chaereas** is a name and nothing more. Polybius pairs him with
Sosylus in order to dismiss them together, and no fragment, subject or
date attaches to him. He is included here because the bare fact that a
third such writer existed is itself evidence: this was not an
eccentricity of one commander's household but something closer to an
establishment.

## What one papyrus is worth

Of all of it, one substantial fragment survives: a scrap of papyrus
found at Würzburg, carrying part of what is generally taken to be
Sosylus's account of a naval engagement, most probably the battle off
the mouth of the Ebro in 217. It turns on a Massaliot tactical
maneuver, described by someone who understood what he was describing.

The fragment matters far beyond its length, because it is the only
place where a modern reader can look directly at Hannibal-aligned
narrative rather than at someone's report of it. And what it shows is a
competent military writer. That is worth holding onto for the next
section.

## Polybius clears the ground

[Polybius](/sources/polybius-histories) mentions Sosylus and Chaereas
once, at 3.20, and only to sweep them aside: their writing, he says,
has the authority of the common talk of a barber's shop. It is the
harshest thing he says about anyone in this part of his work.

Two observations. The first is that Polybius made a professional habit
of clearing rivals out of his path, and did the same to
[Timaeus](/sources/timaeus) at much greater length and to
[Philinus](/sources/philinus-akragas) and
[Fabius Pictor](/sources/fabius-pictor) more briefly. A dismissal from
Polybius is evidence that a work existed and circulated widely enough
to be worth dismissing. He does not waste contempt on writers nobody
was reading.

The second is that the one fragment we can check does not read like
barber-shop gossip. It reads like a naval narrative by someone who
could follow a maneuver. That does not make Sosylus reliable across
seven books, and a sample of one proves very little. But it does mean
the standard verdict on him descends from a hostile competitor whose
judgment the surviving evidence does not corroborate, and the site
treats it accordingly.

## The chain that got Silenus into Livy

Silenus survived by a different route, and the route is worth tracing
because it is the mechanism by which a Carthaginian-aligned account
entered the Roman tradition at all.

[Coelius Antipater](/sources/coelius-antipater), writing the first
Latin monograph on the war in the later second century, took Silenus as
a principal authority. [Livy](/sources/livy-auc), a century after
Coelius, drew on Coelius. So material composed inside Hannibal's camp
reached the standard Roman narrative of the war after passing through
a Latin intermediary with a taste for rhetorical heightening, and then
through a historian writing for Augustan Rome.

What arrives at the end of that chain is not Silenus. It is Silenus
filtered twice by writers with their own purposes, and there is usually
no way to separate the layers. Where Livy carries a vivid Hannibalic
scene that Polybius lacks, Coelius is the likely conduit and Silenus
the possible origin, and "likely" and "possible" are as far as the
evidence goes.

The best-preserved single piece comes through a different channel
again. [Cicero](/sources/cicero-de-divinatione), collecting examples of
significant dreams, reports on Silenus's authority that Hannibal
dreamed at the outset of the invasion of a youth of divine aspect who
led him toward Italy, with a vast serpent laying the country waste
behind, and was told not to look back. The site does not read this as
something Hannibal experienced. It reads it as something Hannibal's
circle circulated, which is a different and more useful kind of
evidence: it shows the invasion being narrated, while it was happening,
as a thing with divine sanction. The same impulse is visible in
Hannibal's cultivation of a bond with Hercules, the Greek face of
[Melqart](/deities/melqart), treated on this site as
[deliberate self-fashioning](/claims/melqart-heracles-identification)
rather than as piety or legend.

Cicero adds a judgment that is worth as much as the dream. He calls
Silenus the writer who pursued Hannibal's affairs most diligently. A
Roman consular rating a Hannibal-aligned Greek as the careful authority
tells us the tradition was taken seriously in Rome even by people with
no reason to flatter it.

## The one document in Hannibal's own voice

There is exactly one text in this story that Hannibal commissioned
himself. During his years in Bruttium he set up a bronze tablet at the
sanctuary of Hera Lacinia at Croton, recording his achievements and the
forces he had brought into Italy, inscribed in Punic and in Greek.
Polybius says he consulted it, and rests his figures for Hannibal's
army on it.

The [Lacinian inscription](/sources/lacinian-inscription) does not
survive, and its authority is more complicated than Polybius allows. He
trusts it because it is an inscription, which is a sound instinct in
general: bronze does not suffer the corruptions of literary
transmission. But this particular bronze was cut more than a decade
after the crossing it records, by the commander whose numbers were in
question, in the theater to which he had by then been reduced, and in
Greek as well as Punic. It is a publication, not a return. What it
attests securely is what Hannibal wanted recorded about his own army.

The detail that sharpens this: Livy knows the monument exists, and
discusses the dispute about Hannibal's troop numbers, and does not use
the inscription to settle it. The silence admits several explanations
and settles none of them, but it is not the behavior of a historian who
regarded the tablet as decisive.

There is one more thing worth saying about the tablet, which is about
audience rather than accuracy. Hannibal put his account of himself at a
Greek sanctuary, in Greek, in southern Italy, at the point in the war
when the allegiance of the Italian Greeks was the thing he most needed
and was steadily losing. Whatever else it was, it was addressed to
somebody.

## What the pattern shows

Set the pieces beside each other and a shape emerges that is not really
about Hannibal.

The Carthaginian side of this war had its writers. They were
professionals, they were present, and at least one of them could handle
a technical military subject. Their work circulated well enough that
Polybius troubled to insult it and Cicero to praise it. And it is gone,
while the accounts written by the winning side survive in volume.

That is not entirely an accident of manuscript survival, though it is
partly that. It is also that the transmitting culture was Roman, and
what a culture copies is what it has use for. Silenus survives to the
degree he was useful to Coelius; Coelius survives to the degree he was
useful to Livy. The same mechanism is visible in the one Carthaginian
book Rome deliberately preserved, treated on this site under
[Mago of Carthage](/narratives/mago-of-carthage): an agricultural
manual, kept because Roman landowners wanted it, while the libraries
that held everything else were handed to the Numidian kings and lost.

And there is the further turn, which is the one that complicates any
simple story about silenced voices. The Carthaginian account of the
Carthaginian war was written in Greek, by Greeks, for a Greek-reading
public, at the commission of a Carthaginian general who had learned
Greek from one of them and who published his own numbers bilingually at
a Greek shrine. The losing side does not reach us in its own language
because it did not entirely choose to speak in its own language. That
is a fact about how the Hellenistic Mediterranean worked, and it is
also, read carefully, evidence of exactly the cultural fluency this
site argues the Carthaginian elite possessed.
