The Phoenician homeland from 539 BCE through 332 BCE, from
Cyrus the Great's conquest of the Levant to Alexander's
destruction of [Tyre](/places/tyre), operated under Persian
suzerainty as part of the satrapy of Across-the-River. The
period is rarely treated in popular Carthage accounts, but it
is the structural condition within which Carthage transitioned
from being a Tyrian colony to being the metropolis of the
western Phoenician sphere. The Carthaginian internal
developments of the fifth century, the
[constitutional arc](/narratives/the-fifth-century-constitutional-arc)
and the
[religious transformation](/narratives/the-fifth-century-religious-transformation), happened against the background of a homeland that had been
politically subordinated to Persia and could no longer
project the metropolitan-political authority earlier centuries
had given it.

This narrative covers what the surviving evidence supports
about the Persian-period homeland, the specific moments of
attested Persian-Carthage interaction, the continuing cultic
ties that bridged political separation, and the broader
rebalancing of the western Phoenician sphere that produced
Carthage as its new center.

## The setup before Persian rule

The Phoenician city-states had operated as independent maritime-
commercial powers along the Levantine coast from approximately
the early first millennium BCE. Tyre in particular had been the
metropolis of Carthage and most of the western Phoenician
colonies, projecting political-religious authority westward
through ritual delegations, dynastic ties, and the broader
network the
[colonial-network theme](/themes/phoenician-colonial-network)
treats. The homeland's political vulnerability was constant, sandwiched between the great Near Eastern powers, but the
Phoenician cities had maintained substantial autonomy across
the Assyrian period (paying tribute while keeping their
political institutions intact) and survived the Babylonian
trauma. Nebuchadnezzar besieged Tyre for thirteen years
(586–573 BCE) without fully capturing the island-city; the
siege is preserved in apocalyptic terms in Ezekiel 26–28.
Tyre eventually accepted Babylonian suzerainty but the
political institutions continued and the colonial enterprise
went on.

By the time Cyrus the Great defeated the Babylonians in 539
BCE, Carthage was already substantial. The conventional
foundation date of 814 BCE places the city well into its
third century of existence; the archaeological evidence places
the foundation slightly later (late ninth century BCE) but
the same general framework holds. Mago I's military
reorganization at Carthage, the
[Magonid expansion](/periods/02-magonid-expansion) that would
define the next century and a half, is conventionally dated
to c. 550 BCE, roughly contemporary with the Persian conquest
that was about to transform the homeland.

## The Persian conquest and the new arrangement

The Phoenician cities submitted to Cyrus's Persia in 539 BCE
without significant resistance. The arrangement that followed
was, on balance, favorable to the Phoenician cities. They were
incorporated into the satrapy of Eber-Nari (Across-the-River,
the Trans-Euphrates region), kept their local kings, retained
substantial commercial autonomy, and became the principal
naval contingent of the Persian Empire. Persia needed
Phoenician ships and sailors for its imperial ambitions, particularly for the planned western operations against Egypt
and eventually against Greece, and treated the Phoenician
cities accordingly.

Phoenician fleets fought as the spearhead of Persian naval
operations across the period. The squadrons Themistocles
destroyed at Salamis in 480 BCE were predominantly Phoenician;
the same Phoenician naval-military capacity that had projected
the western colonial network in earlier centuries was now
deployed in service of Persian imperial campaigns. The
arrangement was paradoxical: the homeland prospered
commercially under Persian rule, but its political-military
energy was channeled into Persian imperial purposes rather
than into the metropolitan relationship with the western
colonies.

This is the structural fact that matters for Carthage. With
Tyre and Sidon operating as Persian naval bases, the older
metropolitan-political relationship with the western colonies
became practically impossible. Tyre could not direct
Carthaginian operations in Sicily or Sardinia while its own
fleet was committed to Persian wars in Greece and Egypt. The
western Phoenician sphere (Carthage, the Sicilian colonies,
Sardinia, the Iberian peninsula, the North African coast) needed a new political center, and Carthage was the only
candidate of sufficient scale.

## The Cambyses episode

The single best-attested direct Persian-Carthage interaction
in the surviving record is Herodotus 3.17–19: Cambyses, after
conquering Egypt in 525 BCE, planned to extend the Persian
campaign west against Carthage. He intended to use his
newly-acquired Phoenician fleet for the operation. The
Phoenician sailors refused. They told Cambyses, per
Herodotus, that they were bound by solemn oaths to Carthage as
their daughter-city and could not attack it in good faith.
Cambyses, who had ruled Egypt by force but did not have the
same compulsion over the Phoenician contingent, dropped the
project.

The episode is historically interesting for several
reasons. First, it is direct attestation that Carthage was
already substantial enough by c. 525 BCE to be a Persian
strategic concern; the Persian state had identified the
western Phoenician sphere as a potential target less than
fifteen years after its own conquest of the homeland. Second,
it documents the metropolis-colony cultic bond surviving
into the Persian period in a form strong enough to override
direct Persian imperial command. The Tyrian sailors' refusal
was not a tactical objection — they were not arguing that the
attack was militarily unwise. They were arguing on religious
and dynastic grounds that the attack was impermissible. The
Cambyses episode is the single most concrete piece of evidence
we have for the Tyre-Carthage cultic bond surviving the
Persian conquest intact.

Third, the episode previews a recurring Persian-period pattern.
Persia would treat the Phoenician cities as commercial-naval
assets while accepting that the cities had their own
political-religious commitments that limited what the empire
could ask them to do. The Phoenician homeland prospered
commercially under Persian rule precisely because the
arrangement was not full subordination but a working
accommodation that preserved Phoenician cultural and religious
autonomy. The colonial relationship with Carthage was a piece
of that preserved autonomy.

## Sidon's prominence and the Sidonian royal sarcophagi

Across the Persian period Sidon eclipsed Tyre as the principal
Phoenician city. The Sidonian-Persian relationship was
particularly close — Sidonian kings participated in Persian
imperial councils, Sidonian ships led the naval contingent at
several major operations, and Sidonian commercial reach
expanded substantially across the period. The material
evidence the homeland preserves from the Persian period is
overwhelmingly Sidonian.

The [Eshmunazar II Sarcophagus](/artifacts/eshmunazar-ii-sarcophagus)
(c. 5th c. BCE) is the principal surviving Phoenician royal
funerary artifact and one of the longest Phoenician
inscriptions to survive in any context. The sarcophagus, re-used Egyptian basalt with a long Phoenician inscription
covering the lid and front, documents the Sidonian king's
self-presentation in Persian-period terms: his piety toward
Eshmun, his royal accomplishments under his patron deity, and
his curse on any future violator of the tomb. The inscription
preserves Persian-era Phoenician royal language in a form
useful for comparative reading against Carthaginian
inscriptional practice. The Eshmunazar inscription does not
mention Carthage, but it documents what Persian-period royal
Phoenician practice looked like across the same period
Carthage was transitioning to its metropolitan role.

The [Tabnit Sarcophagus](/artifacts/tabnit-sarcophagus), of
Eshmunazar II's father, provides the comparable material from
a slightly earlier generation. Together the two Sidonian
sarcophagi are the best-preserved Phoenician-homeland royal
material from the Persian period and the principal evidence
for what Carthage's continuing cultic-cultural relationship
with the homeland was relating to.

The Tyrian-side material from the same period is comparatively
thin. Tyre's island-city setting and the destruction by
Alexander in 332 BCE left less recoverable material than
Sidon's mainland setting preserved. The Tyrian role in
Carthaginian cultic life (the annual Melqart tribute, the
priestly delegations, the metropolis-cult continuity) operated through institutional practices rather than through
material monuments that survived for the modern record.

## The 480 BCE coordination question

The Greek narrative tradition preserves a claim about
Persian-Carthage coordination that has been contested in modern
scholarship since its first careful examination. Herodotus
7.165–167 reports that the Carthaginian invasion of Sicily in
480 BCE, the catastrophic
[Battle of Himera](/events/battle-of-himera), was undertaken
in coordination with the Persian invasion of Greece, and that
the two operations were synchronized to overwhelm the Greek
world from both ends simultaneously. Diodorus 11.20 amplifies
the tradition, placing the Carthaginian defeat at Himera on
the same day as the Greek naval victory at Salamis.

The historicity is genuinely uncertain. The simultaneity, if
accurate, is remarkable and would suggest substantial Persian-
Carthaginian diplomatic coordination of a kind not otherwise
attested. The simultaneity, if invented, would be a Greek
narrative construction — the pan-Hellenic tradition imposing a
satisfying double-victory shape on what was actually two
unconnected operations. Modern scholarship has produced
defenders of both reads:

Those who treat the coordination as historical point to the
timing (the two operations were undeniably close in date even
if not the same day), to the Persian-Carthaginian commercial
ties of the period, and to the strategic logic of a
two-front Mediterranean offensive against the rising Greek
power. Those who treat it as Greek narrative construction
point to the absence of any non-Greek attestation, to the
suspicious neatness of the synchrony, to the Greek
historiographical tradition's habit of imposing such
patterns, and to the lack of any specific Persian-Carthaginian
diplomatic record that would document the coordination.

The site's working position is that the question is unresolved
on the surviving evidence. The Cambyses episode demonstrates
that Persian-Carthaginian diplomatic-strategic contact was
possible in the Persian period; it does not demonstrate that
the 480 BCE operations were coordinated specifically. The
Greek narrative might be preserving a real coordination it
witnessed, or it might be constructing a coordination that
fit its argumentative needs. Both readings are defensible.

What is not in doubt is the Carthaginian disaster at Himera
itself, that part of the tradition is independently
attested, and the parallel-with-Salamis observation, whether
coincidence or coordination, captures something real about the
Greek-perceived shape of the period. The Greek world
experienced 480 BCE as a Mediterranean-wide defense against
two simultaneous Persian-aligned offensives, and the
Carthaginian and Persian roles in that experience were not
fully separable in Greek memory.

## Continuing cultic ties

Despite the political separation that Persian rule imposed,
the cultic relationship between Carthage and Tyre continued
throughout the Persian period and well into the Hellenistic
period after. The
[annual delegation to Tyre](/events/annual-tyre-delegation), the ritual offering Carthage sent to the Temple of Melqart at
Tyre every year, continued without interruption across the
entire Persian period. The practice is attested by Polybius
(31.12) as late as the third century BCE, which puts its
continuation across both the Persian period and the
Hellenistic period through to the Punic Wars. Hannibal's
documented offerings at the Heracleion of Gades before the
Italian campaign, treated by
[Rawlings on Hannibal and Hercules](/sources/rawlings-hannibal-and-hercules-2005), operate within the same Melqart-cult continuity.

[Melqart](/deities/melqart) himself retained his
metropolitan-anchor position in the Carthaginian pantheon
across the Persian period. The
[fifth-century religious transformation](/narratives/the-fifth-century-religious-transformation)
that elevated Tanit and stabilized the broader pantheon did
not displace Melqart from his Tyrian-anchor position. The
naming patterns at Carthage continued to use Melqart-elements
in personal names as markers of metropolitan-Tyrian
connection; the priestly office *mqm ʾlm* ("raiser of the
deity"), tied specifically to the Tyrian Melqart cult,
continued at Carthage across the period; the religious
calendar continued to mark Tyrian festivals. Cultural and
religious continuity ran in parallel with political
independence.

This is the substantive bridge the Persian period preserved.
Carthage was no longer politically subordinate to Tyre (it
could not be, given Tyre's Persian subordination) but it
remained culturally and religiously the daughter-city of
Tyre. The relationship that the Cambyses Phoenician sailors
invoked when refusing to attack Carthage was the cultic
relationship, not the political one. The political
relationship had already weakened by 525 BCE; the cultic one
held strong for centuries beyond.

## Aristotle's Persian-period treatment of Carthage

By the late fourth century, when [Aristotle](/sources/aristotle-politics)
wrote *Politics* II.11 (c. 340 BCE), Phoenicia was still under
Persian rule. The Aristotelian treatment of Carthaginian
governance (its praise of the Carthaginian constitution as a
peer of the best Greek ones, its description of the
suffeteship, the senate, the popular assembly, the Hundred and
Four court) is the principal Persian-period Greek source on
Carthage's internal political organization. The treatment
appears within two years of Alexander's destruction of Tyre
(332 BCE) and represents the closing moment of the Persian
period in the Greek historiographical record.

Aristotle does not connect the Carthaginian constitution to
the Persian-period homeland situation, but the timing is
worth noting. The Carthaginian polity Aristotle treats as a
peer of the Spartan constitution is the polity that emerged
across the Persian-period rebalancing — the
[Magonid-to-republic transition](/openQuestions/fifth-century-constitutional-development)
the fifth-century constitutional arc traces. The constitution
Aristotle praised was the institutional product of Carthage's
emergence as the western Phoenician sphere's new center,
which was itself the product of Tyre's loss of
metropolitan-political capacity under Persian rule.

## Alexander's siege of Tyre and the Persian period's end

The Persian-period arrangement ended with Alexander's siege
of Tyre in 332 BCE. The seven-month siege (Alexander
constructing a causeway from the mainland to take the
island-city, the eventual breach, the massacre and
enslavement of the population) is one of the major
campaigns of the broader Alexandrian conquest. Tyre's
resistance was the longest of any city Alexander attacked
on the Levantine coast; the destruction was correspondingly
total.

The Carthaginian response is preserved in the literary
tradition with characteristic ambiguity. Tradition reports
that a Carthaginian delegation was at Tyre when Alexander
arrived and was caught in the siege; some reports say
Alexander allowed them safe passage, others say he held them
through the siege. The Carthaginian state did not intervene
militarily on Tyre's behalf, the operational distance and
the strategic situation made intervention impractical, but
the cultic relationship continued past the destruction.
Refugee Tyrian populations went to Sidon, to Cyprus, and to
Carthage; the eventual restoration of Tyre under Hellenistic
rule continued the older cultic institutions in modified
form.

For Carthage, Alexander's destruction of Tyre marked the
formal end of the Persian-period arrangement and the
beginning of the Hellenistic period proper. The
metropolis-colony relationship that had survived for two
centuries under Persian rule continued through the
Hellenistic period (the annual Melqart tribute persisted),
but Carthage's position as the unambiguously dominant
Phoenician-cultural power was now structurally fixed. There
was no longer any Tyrian political center capable of
contesting Carthage's metropolitan position even in
principle.

## What the period leaves behind

The Persian-rule period over Phoenicia produced two
parallel developments that the rest of the site's
chronological coverage has to understand. The Phoenician
homeland prospered commercially and culturally under
Persian arrangements while losing the capacity for
metropolitan-political projection over the western
colonies. Carthage emerged across the same period as the
western Phoenician sphere's new center, in continuing
cultic communion with the homeland but in political and
military independence. The combination, homeland
commercial-cultural vitality plus Carthaginian political
emergence, is the structural shape of the Persian period
for Phoenician civilization.

The Persian-side sources are silent on Carthage; the
Persian royal inscriptions do not list it among satrapies
or tributary peoples; the Persian administrative
apparatus apparently did not treat Carthage as worth
specific documentation. What the surviving evidence
gives us is the Greek-side coverage (Herodotus on
Cambyses and on Himera, Aristotle on the constitution),
the Phoenician-homeland material (the Sidonian royal
sarcophagi as Persian-period royal practice), and the
continuing cultic-institutional ties (the annual Melqart
tribute, the priestly offices, the naming patterns) that
documented Carthage's surviving daughter-city
relationship to the homeland.

The third leg of the fifth-century arc, alongside the
constitutional and religious transformations inside
Carthage, is the external Persian-period context within
which those internal transformations unfolded. The site
treats the three together as the period the modern reader
has to recover to understand what the Carthage of the
Punic-Wars period actually was — a city whose mature
political constitution, religious pantheon, and
metropolitan-Mediterranean position were all products of
the seventy years between Himera and the late fifth-century
Sicilian revival, all developing within the structural
condition Persian rule over the homeland created.
