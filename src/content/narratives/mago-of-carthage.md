When the Roman commission appointed to administer the conquered
Carthaginian territory in 146 BCE turned to the question of what
to do with the city's libraries, the answer they gave is among
the most revealing single pieces of surviving evidence about how
the conquerors of Carthage understood Carthaginian intellectual
culture. They gave the libraries to the Numidian allies — the
contents not catalogued, not preserved, not even kept in Roman
hands. From the entire Carthaginian written tradition, they held
back exactly one work: the twenty-eight-book agricultural
treatise of [Mago of Carthage](/people/mago-agronomist), which
the senate specifically ordered translated into Latin. The
preservation order is preserved in Pliny the Elder's
[*Natural History*](/sources/pliny-natural-history) 18.5; the
translation was entrusted to Decimus Junius Silanus, a senator
who had the requisite Punic competence. Pliny treats the decree
as routine. It was anything but.

The treatise that resulted from that decree — circulated in
Latin, abridged into Greek, cited by Varro and Columella and
Pliny himself, transmitted through Palladius into the late Roman
Empire and through the Byzantine *Geoponica* into the
Greek-Eastern Mediterranean, picked up by Ibn al-Awwam in
12th-century al-Andalus and carried forward through the medieval
Arabic agronomic literature, returning to Latin Europe through
the Renaissance — is the only Carthaginian intellectual product
that made it into the Western canon and stayed there. For two
thousand years, Mago has been the single Carthaginian voice the
Mediterranean world has read. Pliny calls him
*[parens rusticationis](/sources/mago-treatise)*, "the father of
country science." It is the most extraordinary surviving Roman
acknowledgment of a Carthaginian intellectual.

This narrative treats Mago as a figure in his own right, the
treatise as the Carthaginian primary source we actually have,
the transmission chain from 146 BCE forward, and the question of
what the survival reveals about the wider Carthaginian
intellectual culture of which Mago is the only sustained
surviving piece.

## The man, as far as we know

Almost nothing about Mago's life is recoverable. The surviving
sources name him only as Mago of Carthage or "the Carthaginian
Mago," and "Mago" was one of the most common aristocratic
Carthaginian names — held by the founder of the Magonid dynasty,
by [Hannibal's younger brother Mago Barca](/people/mago-barca),
and by uncounted other Carthaginians across six centuries.
Distinguishing the agronomist from the other Magos is a modern
editorial convention; the ancient sources rely on context.

The treatise's dating is similarly inferential. Modern
scholarship places composition between the late 4th and the
mid-3rd century BCE on the basis of the institutional context
the work presupposes: a Carthaginian agricultural economy
operating across a settled African hinterland with substantial
estate-scale production, sophisticated specialization in
Mediterranean tree crops, and a developed Punic technical
vocabulary that Mago can deploy without translation. The earlier
boundary is set by when this economic configuration plausibly
existed at scale; the later boundary by Mago's preceding the
3rd-century-BCE military disruptions enough to write
substantially in a settled-agriculture mode. Late 4th or early
3rd c. BCE is the modern best estimate.

One contested detail from the surviving record: Pliny describes
Mago as "twice general" (*bis dictator*) at one point, which
some modern reconstructions read as evidence that Mago held the
[suffeteship](/institutions/suffetes) or other senior civic
office. Other readings treat the *bis dictator* as Pliny
misreading a Punic title or confusing Mago the agronomist with a
different Mago. The detail is too thin to weight heavily, but it
is consistent with the general impression that Mago wrote from
within the Carthaginian aristocratic class rather than from
outside it. He was an estate-owning Carthaginian aristocrat
writing for other estate-owning Carthaginian aristocrats; the
treatise's tone and concerns reflect that social position
throughout.

## The treatise

The twenty-eight books covered the full range of Mediterranean
agriculture as practiced on substantial Carthaginian estates.
The surviving fragments — recovered principally through Varro,
Columella, Pliny, Palladius, and the Geoponica — cluster into
several principal topic areas, each preserving glimpses of a
sophisticated technical tradition behind Mago's synthesis.

**Viticulture** is the most heavily represented topic in the
surviving citations. Mago on vine selection by soil type, vine
training and pruning techniques, the timing of harvest, pressing
methods, wine fermentation and storage. The fragments preserve
specific quantitative guidance — proportions of vine to acreage,
the time between harvest and pressing, the temperatures and
durations of fermentation — that suggest a technical literature
of considerable refinement. Columella's repeated citations of
Mago on vines are the closest surviving approach to the
Carthaginian viticulture of the 3rd century BCE in its own
practical vocabulary.

**Olive cultivation** is second-most-represented: olive grafting
techniques, the soil preferences of different olive varieties,
oil-pressing methods, oil quality grading. The fragments suggest
that Carthaginian olive culture was the principal source for
several techniques the Roman tradition adopted as standard.

**Livestock** is a third major area. Mago on cattle breeding,
sheep and goat husbandry, horse training, mule breeding, pig
farming. The veterinary fragments include treatment guidance for
specific animal diseases — practical, empirical material that
the Roman tradition preserved across multiple authors.

**Bees and honey** are a smaller but distinctive area, with
Mago credited in Columella and Pliny for specific apicultural
techniques (hive construction, queen behavior, the social
organization of the colony, harvest timing) that the Roman
tradition treated as foundational. The Mago-derived bee
material in Columella IX is the most extensively preserved
single topic area from the treatise.

**Estate management** runs across multiple books — overseer
responsibilities, slave-and-tenant labor organization, accounting
practices, the seasonal labor calendar, the division between
permanent and seasonal workforce, the relationship between the
estate and the surrounding rural population. These fragments
preserve glimpses of the Carthaginian *chora*'s social
organization that no other surviving source documents at
comparable depth.

**Soil science** appears in a fragmentary classification system —
Mago distinguished multiple soil types by physical and
agricultural characteristics — that the Roman tradition adopted
and elaborated. Pliny's soil typology in *Natural History* 17
draws extensively on the Mago tradition.

The fragments cite earlier Carthaginian authors whom Mago in turn
synthesized. The names that survive — most prominently a certain
*Hamilcar* who wrote on agriculture before Mago, attested in
Varro 1.1.10 — establish that Mago was working in an existing
Carthaginian agronomic tradition rather than founding one. The
pre-Mago Carthaginian agricultural literature is otherwise
entirely lost; we know of it only through Mago citing it. Mago
is therefore the apex visible piece of a deeper Punic technical
literature, almost all of which has not survived even in
fragments.

## The preservation order

The 146 BCE Roman senatorial decree is preserved in Pliny *NH*
18.5 with what Pliny presents as straightforward summary: after
the destruction of Carthage, the senate ordered that "the books
of Mago of Carthage be brought to Rome and rendered into our
language" (*Carthagine capta Magonis Carthaginiensis ... libros
... in Latinam linguam transferendos curarunt*). The translator
appointed was Decimus Silanus, a senator described as the
"foremost in nobility" of his generation; the work was done at
public expense over an extended period that Pliny does not
specify but that modern reconstructions estimate at several
years.

What is remarkable about the decree is what it is *not*. It is
not a general preservation of Carthaginian literature. The
libraries of Carthage — substantial collections that Mago's own
treatise presupposes — were not preserved at Rome. Pliny's
account is explicit: those libraries were given to the
[Numidian](/narratives/the-numidian-punic-interface) allies (*regulis Africae*). The
Numidians, by this point Roman clients with no particular
incentive or apparatus for preserving Carthaginian written
culture, are not heard from in the surviving record as a venue
through which Carthaginian literature was transmitted; the
practical effect of the gift was disposal. The Roman senate
chose what to keep, and they kept Mago.

Why? The most economical reading is the technical one. Late
Republican Roman agriculture was undergoing rapid transformation
toward larger estates, more specialized production, more
sophisticated labor organization — the configuration Mago's
treatise documented for Carthage a century earlier. The Roman
landowning class was the senate's primary political
constituency. Mago's technical material was directly useful to
that constituency in ways that Carthaginian theology, history,
political theory, or literature was not. The preservation was
selective on the criterion of immediate technical utility to a
specific Roman political-economic class. What was kept was kept
because it was useful; what was not kept was not.

This is the framing that the narrative wants to foreground.
Mago's preservation is not evidence that the Romans valued
Carthaginian culture in the abstract; it is evidence that the
Romans valued one technical area of Carthaginian production
because they could apply it. The political-economic and
intellectual-cultural Carthaginian literature that the Pliny
passage does not mention being preserved — the bulk of what
those libraries contained — is the cost of the selectivity.
Mago survives because the senate's constituency could use him.
The rest did not survive because no Roman constituency had use
for it.

## The transmission chain

Once translated into Latin, Mago entered the Roman agronomic
tradition through several principal vectors:

**[Varro](/sources/varro-rerum-rusticarum)**, in his
*Rerum Rusticarum* (37 BCE), cites Mago at multiple points and
treats him as one of the foundational authorities the Roman
agricultural literature builds on. Varro's access was primarily
through the Greek abridgment by Cassius Dionysius of Utica
(c. 88 BCE), who had translated Mago into Greek in twenty
books — itself a substantial work, also lost in its full form.
Diophanes of Bithynia further abridged Cassius Dionysius into
six books c. 50 BCE, producing a more accessible compendium.
Varro names all three transmitters; the chain is unusually well
documented for a lost work.

**[Columella](/sources/columella-de-re-rustica)**, in his
twelve-book *De Re Rustica* (c. 60 CE), is the most extensively
Mago-citing Roman agronomist. Columella names Mago dozens of
times across the work, on viticulture, olive cultivation,
livestock, beekeeping, and estate management. For many of the
surviving Mago fragments, Columella is the principal vehicle:
the technical material that reaches modern reconstructions comes
through his citations more than through any other channel.

**[Pliny the Elder](/sources/pliny-natural-history)**, in the
*Natural History* (77 CE), cites Mago across multiple books and
provides the most extensively preserved single piece of
contextual information about Mago's reception: the
*parens rusticationis* characterization at *NH* 18.22-23. This
is among the most striking acknowledgments by a Roman writer of
a Carthaginian intellectual — written more than two centuries
after the destruction of Carthage, by an author writing for the
Roman imperial elite, treating Mago as foundational without
qualification.

**Gargilius Martialis** in the 3rd century CE and
**Palladius** in the *Opus Agriculturae* (late 4th / early 5th
century CE) carry Mago's material into the late Roman period
in attenuated but continuous form. By Palladius, the citations
are typically at second or third hand — Mago via Columella, Mago
via the Greek abridgments — rather than directly from the Latin
translation, which had become harder to access. But the
material continued to circulate under Mago's name and to be
attributed to him.

**The Byzantine *Geoponica*** (10th century CE) is a Greek
agricultural compendium compiled at the court of Constantine VII
that preserves substantial Mago-derived material via the Cassius
Dionysius tradition. The Geoponica is the principal eastern
Mediterranean vehicle through which Mago's material entered the
medieval Greek and Arabic agronomic literature.

**Ibn al-Awwam's *Kitāb al-Filāha*** (the *Book of Agriculture*,
12th century CE al-Andalus) is the most substantial medieval
Arabic agronomic work and draws on Mago's material through the
Palladius and Geoponica transmission chains. Ibn al-Awwam was
working in Seville under late Almohad rule; his treatise
preserved Mediterranean agronomic knowledge — including Mago's
material — in a form that subsequently fed back into Latin
Europe through the Spanish translation tradition. The
[Carthaginian-Iberian](/narratives/the-barcid-iberian-state)
connection here is more than ironic: the agricultural knowledge
that Carthage built across a Phoenician-Punic Mediterranean
network returned to al-Andalus through Roman, Greek, and Arabic
intermediaries.

**Pier de' Crescenzi's *Liber Ruralium Commodorum*** (Bologna,
c. 1305) is the principal medieval Latin agricultural treatise
and cites Mago by name as one of the ancient authorities. From
Crescenzi forward, Mago's name remained a routine citation in
European agronomic literature through the Renaissance and into
the early modern period, when 18th and 19th-century
fragment-collection scholarship — culminating in the
*Magonis Carthaginiensis Fragmenta* (Mahaffy, 1889, and
subsequent collections) — assembled the surviving citations
into the form modern scholarship works from.

The chain is unusual in the ancient literary record for both
its length and its documentation. Most ancient technical
literatures that survive at all survive through one or two
transmission channels; Mago's material survived through at least
six independent vectors (Latin agronomic literature, Greek
abridgment literature, Byzantine compendium tradition, Arabic
agronomic literature, medieval Latin agronomic literature, early
modern fragment-collection scholarship), each of which preserved
a partly different selection from the original. The cumulative
result is that we have more of Mago than we have of essentially
any other Carthaginian author — in absolute terms a small body
of recovered text, but in relative terms enormously more than
the zero that almost everyone else preserves.

## What the survival reveals

The Mago story has been treated in modern Punic studies as a
window onto Carthaginian agricultural practice. It is also a
window onto Carthaginian intellectual culture more broadly —
specifically, onto what *kind* of intellectual culture survives
when the conquering power selects what to preserve on its own
terms.

Three observations follow from the surviving picture.

**First, Carthaginian intellectual culture was technically
sophisticated.** Mago's treatise presupposes a substantial prior
Punic technical literature — the *Hamilcar* Varro names, other
unnamed authors Mago cites, the implicit body of Carthaginian
agronomic work that Mago's twenty-eight-book synthesis would not
have been possible without. The Carthaginian aristocratic class
was producing extended written technical work, in Punic, across
multiple generations, in at least one specialized field. The
prior agronomic literature is entirely lost in original form
and known only through Mago's references; the same is almost
certainly true for any number of other technical fields the
Carthaginian intellectual culture supported (administrative
treatises, military manuals, religious-ritual literature,
medical writing, perhaps philosophical or historical work) whose
prior existence is not even attested by transmission through a
surviving synthesizer. We do not know what we have lost.

**Second, the surviving Carthaginian voice is a technical-
empirical voice.** The Mago that survives is not a polemicist,
not an apologist, not a theologian, not a historian. He is an
estate-owning Carthaginian aristocrat writing practical guidance
for other estate-owning Carthaginian aristocrats. The
self-representation that survives is therefore severely
narrowed: we hear from a Carthaginian about how to graft olive
trees and organize seasonal labor, not about how he understood
his city's gods, its political institutions, its place in the
Mediterranean, its relationship with Rome. The cultural-
historical-religious-political Carthaginian voice that other
genres would have preserved is not what the Roman selection
filter let through. The "Carthaginian voice" the Western canon
has is the technical one.

**Third, the survival itself is a metonym for the broader
loss.** What was kept was kept because the conquering power
found it useful. The selectivity of preservation tells us as
much about Rome — about what a 2nd-century-BCE Roman senate
considered worth translating, what a 1st-century-CE Roman
agronomic literature considered worth citing, what a 4th-century
late-Roman compendium considered worth preserving — as it does
about Carthage. The Carthaginian intellectual culture that
survives in the Greco-Roman transmission is the culture that
served Greco-Roman uses. Everything else was lost.

This is the central methodological point that the surviving
Mago material carries. He is what survived because Rome
considered him useful. The remainder of the Carthaginian
intellectual record is what did not survive because Rome did
not. The two are the same fact described from opposite ends, and
holding both ends in mind at once is the appropriate stance
toward the only sustained Carthaginian voice the Western
tradition has.

## Mago in the present

Mago remains in active use in two principal modern fields. The
first is the history of agriculture: the surviving fragments are
the principal evidence for several specific techniques
(beekeeping conventions, viticultural training methods, soil
classification systems) whose later European-Mediterranean
diffusion can be traced through the Mago transmission chain.
Historians of agronomy, viticulture, and beekeeping continue to
work the fragments because they are foundational source material
for fields whose later developments rest on Mago's recorded
practice.

The second is Punic studies. The treatise is the closest the
field comes to direct evidence for the Carthaginian *chora*'s
social and economic organization, the political economy of
Carthaginian estate agriculture, the institutional structure of
[Carthaginian slavery and tenancy](/themes/slavery-and-labor),
the agricultural-technical aspect of Carthaginian aristocratic
intellectual culture. Where the surviving Greco-Roman literary
record fails us — the great majority of what we would want to
know about how a Carthaginian aristocrat actually thought about
his world — Mago is the partial exception.

The combined effect is that Mago is the only Carthaginian whose
written voice the modern reader can encounter in something
approaching its original technical register, two thousand years
after his death. He is, in this narrow but substantial sense,
the surviving Carthaginian voice. What he saw fit to write down
about how to organize a vineyard or train a vine or supervise
slave labor on a large estate is what we have of how he
understood his world. The rest is silence.
