After the naval victory at Cape Ecnomus in 256 BCE, Rome did what it had
never done before and put a consular army ashore in Africa. The
landing force took Aspis on Cape Bon, and then the Senate recalled one
of the two consuls, Manlius Vulso, with much of the fleet and army,
leaving Marcus Atilius Regulus in Africa with a reduced command. With
it he beat the Carthaginians in the field, took Tunis, and encamped
within sight of Carthage itself. The city, pressed at the same time by
Numidian raiding out of the interior, fell into something close to
panic. For a winter it looked as though the war might be decided in
Africa in a single campaign.

## The peace that was refused

Carthage sued for terms, and this is the first turn the Roman
tradition tends to hurry past. Regulus, sensing the war won, pitched
his demands so high that the Carthaginian envoys judged outright
surrender no worse: the evacuation of Sicily and Sardinia, the release
of prisoners without ransom, payment of Rome's war costs, and a
reduction to dependency that would leave Carthage a single warship for
its own use. The envoys carried the terms home, and the city chose to
fight on. Polybius reads the episode as a lesson in the turns of
fortune and the danger of pressing an advantage too far. The narrower
point for the war's shape is that it was Carthaginian resolve and Roman
overreach together, not simple Carthaginian weakness, that kept the
conflict alive past 255.

## Xanthippus and the remaking of the army

Among the mercenaries who reached Carthage that season was a Spartan
named Xanthippus, schooled in the Lacedaemonian military tradition, who
said in plain terms what the city's own commanders had not: that the
Carthaginians had been beaten by their own generalship rather than by
Roman quality. They had thrown away the advantage of their cavalry and
their elephants by letting battle be joined on broken ground where
neither could tell. Given first a hearing and then command, he
retrained the army and brought it down onto the open plain, the one
kind of field on which Carthage's arms were superior. The moment is one
of the clearest illustrations of the Carthaginian army working as an
adaptable institution rather than a fixed levy, the reading the site
sets out in its take on the
[institutionally mixed army](/editorialTakes/carthaginian-army-institutionally-mixed).

## Bagradas

On the plain of the Bagradas in 255, Xanthippus drew the army up in the
order its weapons wanted. The elephants stood in a line before the
infantry, the cavalry massed on both wings, the citizen and mercenary
foot behind. The elephants broke the Roman center; the cavalry, far the
stronger arm, swept the Roman wings aside and closed the trap. Regulus's
army was destroyed where it stood. Perhaps two thousand men cut their
way back to Aspis, and Regulus himself was taken with a few hundred
others. The [Battle of the Bagradas](/events/battle-of-bagradas-255)
ended the African expedition as completely as any defeat in the war.

Xanthippus left soon after, his work done. The later traditions about
his going vary, some honorable, some dark, including a story that the
Carthaginians drowned him at sea out of jealousy. Polybius gives the
sinister versions no weight, and the safe statement is simply that he
had finished what he came to do and departed.

## The legend

Here the Roman tradition takes the story over. In the version Cicero,
Horace, and later writers made famous, Regulus was sent from Carthage
to Rome years afterward, on parole, to argue for a peace or an exchange
of prisoners. Instead he urged the Senate to refuse both, then kept his
oath by returning to Carthage, where he was tortured to death, his
eyelids cut away, his body sealed in a spiked cask. It made him the
Roman model of *fides*, faith kept past the point of self-destruction.

It is almost certainly fiction. Polybius, the closest source to the
events, knows nothing of the embassy or the torture. The gruesome death
surfaces only in later and heavily moralizing writers, and it may have
been constructed backward to match, or to excuse, a separate story that
Regulus's widow had two Carthaginian captives tortured in revenge for
his death in captivity. What survives under the name of Regulus is an
exemplum built for Roman use, not a report of what Carthage did to a
prisoner.

## What the expedition shows

Set the legend aside and the African expedition reads as the war's
plainest case of Carthaginian recovery. A state that stood at the edge
of surrender in the winter of 256 found a commander, rebuilt its army,
and annihilated the force that had brought it so low, then held on in
Sicily for another fourteen years. The lesson Rome drew, in the figure
of Regulus, was about Roman character. The lesson the campaign actually
teaches is about Carthage: its capacity to absorb a disaster and strike
back, which is the quality the Roman sources are least equipped, and
least inclined, to record.
