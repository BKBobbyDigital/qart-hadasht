Every general in the Hellenistic world claimed the gods were with him.
What makes Hannibal's case unusual is how specific the claim was, and
how much of it can still be traced. The god was
[Melqart](/deities/melqart), the lord of Tyre, whom Greeks had called
Heracles for centuries. The route was a route Heracles was supposed to
have taken. The historian who wrote it up was traveling with the army.
The site uses "self-presentation" for what Hannibal was doing, and
keeps "propaganda" for the reading of it Miles and
[Rawlings](/sources/rawlings-hannibal-and-hercules-2005) argue, because
the word assumes an answer to a question this piece leaves open.

## Beginning at the edge of the world

Before the army left New Carthage in 218, Hannibal traveled to Gades,
one of the oldest Phoenician foundations in the west, and there, Livy says
(21.21), he discharged his vows to Hercules and made new ones for the
success of the war. The place was chosen for its meaning as much as its
sanctity. The Heracleion at Gades was the great western sanctuary of
Melqart, and Greek myth set the island of Geryon, whose cattle Heracles
drove back east as his tenth labor, in the waters off it. A campaign
that set out from Gades toward Italy was, for anyone who knew the story,
setting out where the hero's return journey began.

The one surviving trace of what Silenus wrote about this is small but
telling. Strabo (3.5.7), discussing the famous spring in the Heracleion
whose flow was said to rise and fall against the tides, names
[Silenus](/sources/silenus-caleacte) among the writers who described
it. Miles takes the vows to be Silenus's episode and the spring to be a
fragment of his description of the sanctuary. Whatever else his history
contained, it spent time at the altar where Hannibal's war was
consecrated.

## The road Heracles built

The route mattered as much as the start. Greek tradition held that
Heracles, driving Geryon's cattle home, had crossed from Iberia through
the lands of the Celts and over the Alps into Italy. Diodorus (4.19)
has him turn a rough and nearly impassable mountain track into a road
fit for armies and baggage trains, and destroy the tribes who preyed on
travelers. A pseudo-Aristotelian collection of wonders (entry 85)
describes a "Heracleian road" from Italy to the Celts, Ligurians and
Iberians, on which travelers were protected by the peoples along it.
Nepos, writing nearly two centuries after the war, repeats the point in
its sharpest form: no army had crossed the Alps before Hannibal's except
that of Hercules.

This matters for what kind of claim Hannibal was making. If the
pseudo-Aristotle entry is as early as its likely source suggests, the
Heraclean road was an existing tradition before 218. Hannibal did not
invent a mythical route. He marched an army along one, and in doing so
made himself the second commander in history to do what only the hero
had done.

## A general who looked like Heracles

Miles's most striking argument concerns a passage of
[Dionysius of Halicarnassus](/sources/dionysius-roman-antiquities).
Dionysius tells the story of Heracles and Cacus twice. The first telling
is the myth: a hero resting with his cattle, a monstrous thief. The
second, which Dionysius calls the truer one (1.41–42), has been stripped
of the fabulous. Here Heracles is a general at the head of a great army
who has already conquered Spain. He forces the passes of the Ligurian
mountains against fierce resistance, overthrows oppressive tyrannies
and sets up lawful governments, defeats Cacus as a barbarian chieftain
who raided his camp, and carries his prisoners with him before settling
them on conquered land with a share of the spoils.

Set beside Hannibal the pattern is hard to miss. An army from Spain,
the Alpine passes forced against mountain tribes, a war presented as
liberation from an oppressive power, and a policy of releasing captive
allies to win their communities over, which is what Hannibal did with
Italian prisoners after Trebia, Trasimene and Cannae. Miles thinks the
correspondences too obvious to be chance, and points to Diodorus for a
Sicilian origin in the period of the war.

The caution is that Dionysius does not say where he found the version,
and rationalizing myths into history was a Hellenistic habit that did
not need Hannibal to prompt it. A Greek writer after the war could as
easily have shaped Heracles to resemble the most famous general of the
age as Hannibal's historians could have shaped the war to resemble
Heracles. The parallel is real. Its direction is an inference.

## The dream

The most famous piece of the program is a dream, and it survives in
two versions that are worth keeping apart. Cicero, who cites Silenus
through the Latin historian Coelius, gives the fuller one (*On
Divination* 1.49). After taking Saguntum, Hannibal dreamed he was
summoned to a council of the gods, where Jupiter ordered him to invade
Italy and gave him one of the council as a guide. Told not to look
behind him, he could not resist, and saw a vast beast wrapped in
serpents destroying everything in its path. The guide told him it was
the devastation of Italy, and that he should go on and not concern
himself with what lay behind.

Livy's version (21.22) is set on the march near the Ebro, and it is
softer: a youth of godlike appearance sent by Jupiter, and behind
Hannibal a huge serpent with a storm in its wake. Miles regards
Cicero's as the closer rendering of Silenus, and the difference is
instructive. The council of the gods and the divine guide are the
furniture of Greek epic. The dream as Silenus told it put Hannibal
among the Olympians, commissioned by the king of the gods, which is
exactly the kind of sanction a Hellenistic historian would give a
Hellenistic king.

## Who it was for

A message this elaborate had more than one audience, and each would
have heard something different.

To the Carthaginian and Libyan core of the army, and to the Punic
cities of Iberia, the god was Melqart, and the vows at Gades were an act
of ancestral piety by the son of a family that had sworn its oath at his
altar. To Greek communities in Italy and Sicily, the same god was
Heracles, benefactor and civilizer, and a general walking in his path
was a liberator rather than a barbarian invader. Hannibal's bilingual
record of his achievements at the
[sanctuary of Hera Lacinia](/sources/lacinian-inscription), in Punic
and Greek, speaks to the same double audience. Miles adds the peoples of
central Italy, among whom Hercules was a major cult figure, especially
in Samnium, whose long hostility to Rome made them obvious allies, and
the Celts, whose chiefs traveled with bards who sang their deeds.

The Barcid silver coinage of Iberia, with its portrait head that can be
read as Melqart, as Heracles, or as a Barcid in the god's guise, belongs
to the same visual language; the
[Barcid Iberian coinage](/artifacts/barcid-iberian-coinage) page sets
out the competing identifications. And the army itself was an audience.
Miles quotes the Greek military writer Onasander on soldiers fighting
harder when they believe the gods are with them, and the historian
Gregory Daly on Hellenistic armies whose cohesion rested on the aura of
a commander who seemed favored by heaven. An army of Libyans, Iberians,
Numidians, and soon Celts shared no language and no homeland. It could
share a general who had the gods on his side. This is the practical
edge of the program, and it fits the site's reading of the
[institutionally mixed Carthaginian army](/editorialTakes/carthaginian-army-institutionally-mixed):
something had to hold such a force together, and it was not a common
citizenship.

## Polybius says no

The best-informed surviving historian of the war would have none of
it. In a passage aimed squarely at writers like Silenus, Polybius
(3.47–48) mocks historians who first exaggerate the Alps into a
wilderness no army could cross and then, having trapped themselves,
bring in a god or hero to show Hannibal the way, like tragedians who
cannot end a play without a god lowered onto the stage. The Alps, he
points out, had been crossed many times by Gallic armies. Hannibal did
not need a divine guide. He needed good intelligence and careful
planning, and he had both.

Polybius's objection cuts two ways. It confirms that the divine
narrative existed and circulated widely enough to need refuting, which
supports the whole case that Hannibal's circle was telling this story.
It also shows the direction of Polybius's own sympathy: by stripping out
the gods he gives Hannibal the credit, a general whose achievement was
his own. That is a characteristic Polybian move, and it is the same man
who dismissed Silenus's colleague Sosylus as barber-shop gossip, a
rivalry traced under
[Hannibal's historians](/narratives/hannibals-historians).

## What can and cannot be said

Some of this is secure. Hannibal made vows at Melqart's sanctuary at
Gades before the war; his historian wrote about that sanctuary; his
historian narrated a dream in which the gods commissioned the invasion;
the route he took was one Greek tradition already assigned to Heracles;
and the claim that only Hercules had crossed before him was still being
repeated in Rome nearly two centuries later. Taken together, that is a
deliberate framing of the campaign, and the
[claim on the Heraclean presentation](/claims/hannibal-campaign-heraclean-presentation)
sets out the evidence for it and against it.

Two things are not secure. One is how much further the pattern went:
whether the rationalized Heracles of Dionysius was a product of
Hannibal's war or a later reflection of it. The other is what to call
it. A Carthaginian whose family had sworn its most solemn oath at
Melqart's altar, and who went to Gades before marching on Rome, may
well have believed every word Silenus wrote about the gods. Belief and
presentation were not opposites in the ancient world, and the evidence
does not require choosing between a pious Hannibal and a calculating
one. It shows a man who made sure that everyone who mattered, in every
language his war was fought in, heard that the gods were marching with
him.
