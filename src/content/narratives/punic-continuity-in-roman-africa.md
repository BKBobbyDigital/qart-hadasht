A reader new to Carthage often arrives believing — because the
literary record has so consistently said so — that the city was
destroyed in 146 BCE and that was the end of it. The literary
record's framing reads naturally as a closure: a war won, a
city razed, a population enslaved, a name passed into Roman
memorial vocabulary as the exemplary case of total Mediterranean
victory. Whatever was Carthaginian ends with the destruction;
what comes after is Roman.

The framing is residual hostile-tradition closure narrative. The
surviving evidence does not support it. Punic identity, language,
religion, and onomastic forms persisted across the Numidian
period, through the Roman imperial administration of North
Africa, into the late Roman period, and — in some senses — into
the early Islamic conquest era. The persistence is not a
residual incompleteness of Romanization; it is a substantive
historical fact attested directly in the Punic-side evidence
(neo-Punic inscriptions, religious-cultic continuity, onomastic
persistence) and indirectly in the Latin-side evidence (the
Carthaginian-born Latin writers who treat Punic identity as
their own).

This narrative walks the lines of evidence with attention to
non-Roman primary sources, treats Carthaginian-born Latin writers
as Carthaginian-cultural voices in Latin form rather than as
Roman observers, and resists hard-endpoint framing throughout.
The site's position is not that Punic identity ended at some
specific date later than 146; the position is that whatever
final transformation it underwent is not recoverable from the
surviving evidence, and 146 BCE is *not* the date it occurred.

The single immediate counterexample to the closure framing is
[Mago of Carthage's agricultural treatise](/sources/mago-treatise),
the 28-book Carthaginian work that the Roman senate specifically
ordered translated into Latin in 146 BCE and that ran
continuously through the Roman, Greek, Arabic, and medieval Latin
agronomic literature for the next two thousand years. The
transmission chain that the
[Mago narrative](/narratives/mago-of-carthage) documents is
itself proof that Carthaginian intellectual influence did not end
in 146. What follows is the broader picture of which Mago's
survival is one part.

## The Punic-language inscriptional record

The single most directly Punic-side evidence is the neo-Punic
inscriptional record. The late Punic script — derived from the
classical Punic alphabet, used continuously in North Africa
across the Numidian and early Roman periods — produced a
substantial inscriptional corpus that runs through at least the
3rd century CE.

The principal sites are well-documented. **Lepcis Magna** in
modern Libya has the largest neo-Punic civic-inscriptional
corpus: dedicatory inscriptions to traditional Punic deities
(Shadrapa and Milkashtart prominently), civic and architectural
inscriptions naming Punic officeholders, the surviving
bilingual Latin-neo-Punic dedications that demonstrate
side-by-side official use of both languages into the 1st-2nd
centuries CE. **Mactar** in modern Tunisia has produced
substantial neo-Punic funerary and votive inscriptions from the
2nd-3rd centuries CE. **Cirta** (modern Constantine) is the
site of the [Hofra Stele Cache](/artifacts/hofra-stele-cache),
the principal Numidian Punic religious-inscriptional corpus
that documents continued *molk*-formula dedications to Baal
Hammon and Tanit (the "Tanit Pene Baal" formula intact) under
Numidian and early Roman administration.

Across these sites and others (El-Hofra, Dougga, the broader
Tripolitanian and Tunisian Punic-cultural zone), the evidence
is direct, primary, and Punic-side: Punic-speakers, writing in
Punic script, addressing traditional Punic deities and
documenting traditional Punic civic forms, across at least four
centuries after the 146 BCE destruction. The
[Punic inscriptional record theme](/themes/punic-inscriptional-record)
treats the corpus as a category of evidence in its own right;
the neo-Punic late material is the load-bearing piece for the
post-146 continuation question specifically.

The substantive neo-Punic inscriptional record extends at least
through the 3rd century CE. The honest framing is that this is
the period for which we have positive attestation; the absence
of substantial neo-Punic inscriptions from later centuries does
not establish that neo-Punic writing ceased then. It may have.
The evidence may also have not survived, or the practice may
have continued in unmonumental forms (writing on perishable
materials) that did not enter the archaeological record. The
3rd-century date is a lower bound on demonstrable persistence,
not an endpoint.

## Religious continuity under Latin names

The two principal Punic deities continued in Roman-period
North African cult under Latin names. **Tanit became Iuno
Caelestis** — the same goddess in continuous cult, with the
same iconography (the sign of Tanit appears on Roman-period
North African mosaics and on coinage), the same characteristic
celestial-female-sovereign attributes, the same regional
distribution. The Iuno Caelestis cult was sufficiently
substantive in the 3rd and 4th centuries CE that it was
Christianized rather than suppressed — the last major North
African pagan cult to receive systematic Christian attention.
The [Tanit page](/deities/tanit) treats the continuation in
detail; the site reads "Iuno Caelestis" as the Latin name for
the Punic Tanit rather than as a separate Roman cult.

**Baal Hammon became Saturnus Africanus.** The Roman-period
North African Saturn cult is the most-attested non-imperial
cult in the surviving epigraphic record of Roman Africa,
attested across the former Carthaginian heartland and into
Numidia and Mauretania, on hundreds of votive stelae that
continue the Punic *molk*-dedication formula in Latin
translation. The
[Baal Hammon page](/deities/baal-hammon)
documents the continuation; what is striking from the Punic
side is that the formal vocabulary of the dedications — the
*votum solvit libens merito* and adjacent Latin phrases — runs
parallel to the formulae the surviving Punic-language stelae
preserve for the same deity in the pre-146 period. The
dedicators' descendants are addressing the same god in the
same way, with the language they actually speak.

The Saturnus Africanus cult is documented through at least the
4th century CE, with the principal evidence-density in the
2nd-3rd centuries. The cult's eventual decline tracks the
broader Christianization of North Africa rather than any
specifically Punic-cultural endpoint; Saturn was eventually
displaced not by Latin paganism but by Christianity.

The framing matters. "Saturnus Africanus" is what the Latin
literary tradition called the deity that Latin-speaking-but-
Punic-thinking dedicators experienced as the same Punic
deity their grandparents had served. The deity did not
become Roman; the language of the dedications changed.
Bénabou's
[*La résistance africaine*](/sources/benabou-resistance-1976)
is the principal modern scholarly treatment that reads the
continuity from this side rather than from the Roman
administrative side.

## Numidian-period architectural and royal-cult continuity

Before the Roman-period evidence, the Numidian kingdom under
[Masinissa](/people/masinissa) and his successors actively
preserved and elaborated Punic religious and architectural
forms. The
[Dougga Libyco-Punic Mausoleum](/artifacts/dougga-libyco-punic-mausoleum)
— a 2nd-century BCE monumental tomb in the Punic-Berber
cultural sphere with bilingual Libyco-Punic inscriptional
material — documents the Numidian elite's active continuation
of Punic-cultural forms after 146 BCE. The mausoleum is not
"Roman-influenced" architecture and is not "post-Carthaginian"
in the closure sense; it is Numidian-period Punic-cultural
architecture, building on the same Phoenician-Punic
architectural and inscriptional traditions that the
pre-Roman Numidian-Punic interface had produced.

The Numidian royal cult under Masinissa and his successors
continued worship of Baal Hammon, Tanit, and other Punic
deities through the 2nd and 1st centuries BCE, with the
royal house's adoption of Punic religious forms part of the
broader pattern by which the Numidian kingdom maintained its
Punic-cultural prestige even as it operated as a Roman ally.
The Hofra cache is the principal religious-inscriptional
evidence; the architectural record at Dougga and other sites
is the principal monumental-cultic evidence. The cumulative
picture is of a Numidian-period that did not represent a
break in Punic religious-cultural practice but a continuation
of it under different political authority.

## Onomastic persistence

The personal-name evidence is the most quantitatively
substantial single body of post-146 Punic-continuity material.
[Karel Jongeling's *North-African Names from Latin Sources*](/sources/jongeling-names-1994)
(1994) collects approximately 6,000 personal names of secure
Punic, Libyan, or Berber etymology attested in Latin-language
epigraphy and literary sources from Roman-period North Africa.
The corpus documents:

- **Theophoric Punic names** — the *ʿbd*-deity-name compounds
  (servant of), *gr*-deity-name (client of), *bn*-deity-name
  (son of) constructions that the Punic-language inscriptional
  record establishes for the pre-146 period continuing in
  Latin epigraphy through at least the 3rd-4th centuries CE.
- **Specifically Punic name-elements** — *Bod-*, *Him-*,
  *Han-*, *Bal-*, *Mago*, *Hamilcar*, *Hannibal*, and many
  others persisting as personal names of Roman citizens and
  non-citizens across imperial-period North Africa.
- **Geographic distribution** — the persistence is densest in
  the former Carthaginian heartland (modern Tunisia), the
  Numidian and Mauretanian zones, and the Tripolitanian
  coastal cities; sparser but present in inland and
  marginally Romanized areas.

The onomastic evidence is direct: Punic-cultural families
named their children in Punic forms even when the families
operated in Latin civic and religious contexts. Naming is one
of the most conservative cultural practices, and the
persistence of Punic theophoric naming across multiple
generations is direct evidence that Punic-cultural identity
was maintained at the family level across at least three
centuries after 146 BCE.

## Carthaginian-cultural voices in Latin form

Four Carthaginian-born Latin writers — **Tertullian** (c. 155-220
CE), **Apuleius** of Madauros (c. 125-170 CE), **Cyprian** of
Carthage (c. 210-258 CE), and **Augustine** of Hippo (354-430
CE) — wrote substantial Latin literary work from positions
rooted in North African Carthaginian-cultural environments. The
site treats them as Carthaginian-cultural voices in Latin form
rather than as Roman observers of Carthaginian survivals. Their
Latin literary framing filters their Punic-cultural position but
does not eliminate it, and at specific moments — Apuleius's
*Apologia* and Augustine's *Letter 17* — the position breaks
through and is directly visible.

**Apuleius's *Apologia*** (c. 158 CE) is the defense Apuleius
delivered at his own trial for alleged sorcery before the
Roman proconsul at Sabratha. In a famous passage (24.1) he
addresses the accusation that he is somehow disgraceful for
not being purely Greek or Roman: "I am half-Numidian and
half-Gaetulian. But what is shameful in that? Cyrus the elder
was half-Mede and half-Persian. A man is judged by his
character, not by his country." The passage is unusual in
Latin literature: an educated Latin literary figure embracing
rather than apologizing for his African and partly
Punic-Berber background, treating it as compatible with
philosophical and rhetorical accomplishment rather than as
something to be transcended. Apuleius is writing in Latin to
a Latin-administered court, but his cultural position is North
African and he wears it.

**Augustine's *Letter 17* to Maximus of Madauros** (c. 390 CE)
is the most direct surviving defense of Punic identity by a
Latin writer. Maximus, a pagan Latin-letters man at Madauros
(the same city that produced Apuleius), had written Augustine
mocking Punic names — treating them as ridiculous, evidence
of cultural backwardness. Augustine's reply explicitly defends
Punic: he tells Maximus not to mock Punic names, that they are
the names of Maximus's own ancestors in Madauros, that the
Punic language and cultural identity is a substantive African
inheritance that Latin literary culture has no business
dismissing. The letter is a Latin Christian bishop going to
bat for Punic identity against a Latin pagan's mockery — an
unusually direct articulation of Carthaginian-cultural
solidarity that the Latin-framing-of-the-text does not
obscure.

Across his sermons and letters,
[Augustine](/sources/augustine-letters) regularly references
Punic words, explains Punic etymologies, treats Punic-speaking
congregants as part of his expected audience, and proposes
Punic translations of biblical terms where Punic illuminates
the Hebrew underlying the Greek. Punic is a living language in
his pastoral environment in the early 5th century CE. He
treats this as unremarkable rather than as evidence of
incomplete Romanization.

**Tertullian** is the most polemical of the four and the most
directly engaged with pre-Christian Carthaginian religious
forms (which he treats as foil for Christian apologetic). His
*Ad Nationes* and *Apologeticus* address Carthaginian
religious practice as part of his city's own pre-Christian
inheritance rather than as a foreign curiosity. His position
on Carthaginian-cultural identity is not as direct as
Apuleius's or Augustine's, but he is writing from Carthage
in the early 3rd century CE, in a city whose pre-Christian
religious-civic forms he had grown up within.

**Cyprian** as bishop of Carthage in the 250s CE is similarly
positioned: a Latin Christian writer rooted in a North African
Carthaginian environment that he knew intimately. His
theological and pastoral letters reflect the rural-and-urban
North African Christianity of the mid-3rd century, which had
a substantial Punic-cultural substrate.

## The Maktar Harvester

A single inscription stands out as the closest surviving
Punic-cultural rural voice from Roman-period North Africa: the
**Maktar Harvester Inscription** (c. 260 CE, modern Tunisia),
a Latin verse self-epitaph by an anonymous peasant who rose
through agricultural labor to become a town magistrate at
Mactar. The speaker self-identifies as having grown up in
poverty in a rural area, worked as a seasonal harvester across
the Punic-cultural agricultural zone, accumulated savings,
acquired property, and eventually entered the local elite. The
inscription is in Latin but the speaker's social position and
geographic origin place him squarely in the rural Punic-
cultural population of mid-3rd-century Africa.

The Harvester's voice is the closest we get in any surviving
source to a non-elite Punic-background African voice in late
antiquity, and the substance — agricultural labor, regional
labor migration, slow social mobility through estate
agriculture — is exactly the world that
[Mago's treatise](/sources/mago-treatise) had described seven
centuries earlier. The continuity of agricultural-social
forms from Punic Carthage through Roman Africa is visible in
this single inscription with unusual directness.

## Christianization and the Donatist controversy

The Christianization of North Africa from the 2nd century CE
forward did not erase Punic-cultural identity; in some
respects it deepened the fault lines between Romanized urban
Latin-speaking Christian populations and rural Punic-Berber-
speaking Christian populations. The Donatist controversy of
the 4th century — the schism between the rigorist Donatist
church (rural, North African, partly Punic-Berber) and the
catholic-imperial Latin urban church — has been read by
[Bénabou](/sources/benabou-resistance-1976) and others as in
part a Punic-Berber rural cultural assertion against Latin-
urban-imperial Christian orthodoxy. The reading is contested
in modern scholarship; what is not contested is that the
Donatist church had substantial rural Punic-cultural support
through the 4th and into the 5th centuries CE, and that the
imperial suppression of Donatism was experienced by its
adherents as in part a Roman-cultural-imperial imposition on
African religious life.

The Augustine of *Letter 17* defending Punic against Latin
mockery and the Augustine prosecuting the Donatist church are
the same person, and the tension is real. The closure-narrative
reading of late Roman Africa treats the Latin-urban Christianity
as the surviving form and the Donatists as a residual
peculiarity. The Bénabou reading treats Donatism as the
substantive expression of Punic-cultural-rural African
identity in late-Roman Christian form. The site does not
adjudicate the question but flags it: the Punic-cultural
continuity through late antiquity may have included a
specifically Christian form that the surviving Latin-imperial
sources treat as heretical schism rather than as cultural
continuity.

## The non-endpoint question

This narrative deliberately does not assert when Punic identity
ceased. The surviving evidence supports several lines of
positive attestation through specific dates:

- Substantive neo-Punic inscriptional record through at least
  the 3rd century CE.
- Saturnus Africanus and Iuno Caelestis cults through at
  least the 4th century CE.
- Punic personal names in Latin epigraphy through at least
  the 3rd-4th centuries CE.
- Augustine's testimony to spoken Punic in the early 5th
  century CE.

None of these is a *cessation* date; each is the last point of
substantive positive attestation in a body of evidence whose
later survival is contingent on factors (manuscript
transmission, archaeological preservation, the destruction
or survival of late Roman / Vandal / Byzantine / early
Islamic North African sites) that the surviving record does
not control. Punic-cultural identity may have persisted
through the Vandal conquest of 439 CE and into the Byzantine
re-conquest of 533 CE; it may have persisted through the
Arab conquest of the late 7th century CE and contributed to
the Berber-Arabic synthesis of medieval North Africa; or it
may have ceased earlier than any of these. The surviving
evidence does not establish the answer.

What the surviving evidence does establish — directly,
through neo-Punic inscriptions and onomastic persistence and
religious continuity and the spoken-Punic testimony of
Augustine — is that 146 BCE is not the date Punic culture
ended. The closure framing is residual Greco-Roman
historiography. The Punic-side evidence consistently runs
against it.

This is the position the site takes. The site does not
replace one hard endpoint with another. It treats the
duration of Punic-cultural identity as a substantively
open question whose surviving evidence supports continuity
through at least late antiquity, with the precise
transformations that eventually attenuated it not recoverable
from the available record. The
[open question on Punic continuity](/openQuestions/duration-of-punic-cultural-identity)
treats the candidate framings explicitly.

## Closing — what 146 did not end

The destruction of Carthage in 146 BCE ended the Carthaginian
state. It ended Punic political-institutional life in its
specifically Carthaginian metropolitan form. It did not end
Punic culture, Punic language, Punic religion, Punic
intellectual influence, or Punic identity. Each of these
persisted, in transformed but recognizable form, across the
Numidian, Roman, and early late-antique periods, and entered
medieval North African and (through transmission via Roman,
Greek, and Arabic intermediaries) the broader Mediterranean
cultural inheritance.

Mago's transmission alone defeats the closure framing: a
Carthaginian voice running continuously from 250 BCE through
the medieval Arabic and Latin agronomic literature, never
fully out of circulation, still cited by name into the
European Renaissance. The transmission is not the only line of
continuity but it is the longest single one and the one whose
chain is best documented. Whatever else 146 BCE ended,
Carthaginian intellectual influence on the western
Mediterranean — and through al-Andalus and the Arabic
agricultural tradition on the wider medieval world — was
not part of it.

This is what the site means by closing one chapter and
opening another. Punic Carthage as a metropolitan polity did
end in 146. Punic culture, Punic identity, and Punic
influence did not. The story does not stop where the
literary record's closure-narrative says it stops; it
attenuates and transforms and persists, and the surviving
evidence does not let us see where (or whether) it ended.
