The standard Carthage-narrative pairs commerce and naval power as
two faces of the same thing: Carthaginians traded by sea, and the
warships protected the merchants. The pairing is not wrong, but it
collapses two substantively different institutional structures into
one. The Carthaginian commercial fleet was a private apparatus of
ship-owning aristocratic families, partnerships, and ad-hoc joint
ventures operating across the western Mediterranean network. The
Carthaginian war fleet was a state institution with shared physical
infrastructure (the military cothon), centralized command, and a
mobilization capacity that operated at scale only the state could
sustain. The site reads these as related but distinguishable
systems, and treats the naval system here on its own terms.

## The cothon as installation

The paired cothon basins at Carthage are the single most
substantive surviving evidence for Carthaginian naval
institutional infrastructure. The rectangular outer basin was the
commercial harbor: it received merchant traffic from across the
network, with quayside warehousing visible in the topographic
plan and the limited excavation evidence. The circular inner
basin was the military harbor: an artificial lagoon ringed by
ship-sheds, with a central island that housed the naval
administration and (per Appian's late description, 8.96) provided
elevated observation positions over the basin and its approaches.
The ship-sheds — wedge-shaped roofed bays opening onto the basin
— could accommodate approximately 170 warships under cover, with
additional capacity at the central island. The whole installation
was screened from external view by a perimeter wall: an observer
on the city side could not see what was in the basin, an observer
on the sea side could not see what was passing between commercial
and military harbors.

The architectural separation is itself substantive. The military
basin was not an extension of the commercial basin. It was a
distinct installation with its own access, its own administration,
and its own functional specialization. The state's naval capacity
was housed and managed separately from the private commercial
capacity, even though the two installations shared the same
maritime gateway. The arrangement reads as a deliberate
institutional choice: the war fleet under direct state
administration; the commercial fleet under aristocratic private
ownership; the physical separation of the two enforcing the
institutional separation.

The cothon's dating is contested in the archaeological literature.
The visible installation is substantially a late Punic structure
(probably 3rd or 2nd century BCE in its final form), and may
postdate the loss of the First Punic War fleet. An earlier
Carthaginian naval harbor must have existed — the war fleet of the
late 4th and early 3rd centuries BCE was not housed in nothing —
but its location and configuration are less securely identified.
The [cothon place page](/places/cothon) treats the installation in
its own right.

## The shipbuilding apparatus

The principal direct evidence for Carthaginian naval architecture
is the [Marsala Punic Warship](/artifacts/marsala-punic-warship)
hull, recovered off Lilybaeum in 1971 and probably a casualty of
the First Punic War in its final operations. The hull is
incomplete but preserves the construction method: mortise-and-tenon
joinery securing the hull planks, with the planks shaped to fit
one another and the frames inserted afterward (the "shell-first"
construction method standard in Mediterranean shipbuilding of the
period). What makes the Marsala find substantively informative
about Carthaginian state shipbuilding is the inscribed Punic
shipwright's marks on the timbers: individual planks bear
alphabetic letters indicating their assembly sequence. The
inscription pattern is the trace of a standardized production
system in which prefabricated components were cut, marked, and
then assembled in a specific order — the kind of production
discipline that scales up only when the same yard is building
many ships to the same specification.

The [Egadi Islands bronze warship rams](/artifacts/egadi-bronze-ram),
twenty-seven of which have been recovered since 2010 from the
seabed of the Battle of the Aegates Islands, extend the
construction-evidence record dramatically. Several rams carry
Punic-language inscriptions invoking Baal and naming
construction supervisors; others bear Latin inscriptions naming
Roman magistrates. The two corpora together preserve a snapshot
of the late First Punic War naval rebuild on both sides — Roman
and Carthaginian quaestors and shipyard officials named on the
hardware they oversaw. The Punic rams' inscriptions are among
the few surviving direct epigraphic attestations of Carthaginian
naval administrative offices in operation.

The Carthaginian shipbuilding tradition was Phoenician in
inheritance: the same mortise-and-tenon construction method is
documented in the late 14th-century BCE Uluburun wreck off the
Anatolian coast and continued without substantive change through
the Phoenician metropolitan period and into Carthaginian
practice. The Carthaginian innovation was not a new construction
method but an industrial-scale application of the inherited
method, with standardized production at state shipyards.

## Mobilization capacity

The Carthaginian state's capacity to mobilize naval power at
scale is documented at three principal moments where the
literary tradition gives numerical detail.

**Cape Ecnomus (256 BCE).** Polybius (1.25–28) reports the
Carthaginian fleet at the Battle of Cape Ecnomus as approximately
350 quinqueremes (he gives the Roman fleet as 330). The figures
are conventionally treated as somewhat inflated but indicate
fleet sizes of several hundred ships on each side. A quinquereme
required approximately 300 oarsmen plus 120 marines and deck
crew: 350 quinqueremes implies roughly 105,000 oarsmen and 42,000
marines, or about 147,000 total naval personnel in a single fleet.
The mobilization scale rivals or exceeds the Carthaginian field
army's manpower at any documented moment.

**The First Punic War fleet rebuilds.** Across the war's
twenty-three years, Polybius reports multiple catastrophic
Carthaginian fleet losses (Mylae 260, Cape Ecnomus 256, the
storm losses of the 250s, the Aegates Islands 241), each
followed by reconstruction. The shipbuilding apparatus that
supported this rebuild capacity is mostly invisible in the
literary record but must have been institutionally substantial:
the state could replace a fleet of several hundred ships within
a few years, repeatedly, across a generation of war. The fiscal
capacity required is the kind of thing that bankrupted the
Carthaginian treasury by the end of the war and produced the
indemnity-arrears that triggered the Mercenary War.

**The Third Punic War mobilization.** After the Treaty of 201
BCE capped the Carthaginian war fleet at 10 warships, the
state-managed naval shipbuilding apparatus was substantially
disassembled across the next fifty years. Yet when the Roman
demands of 149 BCE escalated to the *delenda* decision and
Carthage faced siege, the city reportedly built 120 warships
within two months from scratch, drawing on stockpiled timber
and the labor of the besieged population (Appian 8.93–96; the
figure is conventionally treated as exaggerated but indicative).
The fact that the rebuild was even attempted, on that timescale,
testifies to surviving institutional memory of how a war fleet
was produced — passed down through fifty years of formal
treaty-mandated disarmament.

The three moments together suggest a naval institutional system
that was structurally robust across centuries. Specific fleets
could be destroyed; the institutional capacity to produce another
one persisted.

## Command structure

The Carthaginian naval command structure is fragmentarily
attested. The principal Greek-source term for Carthaginian naval
command is *strategos* or *nauarchos*, applied to commanders
named in the surviving accounts: Hannibal son of Gisco (the
First Punic War commander, executed after Mylae), Hamilcar
Barca (in his late-First-Punic-War Sicilian command, which had
substantial naval components), Adherbal (the Drepana commander
who won the major Carthaginian naval victory of 249 BCE), and
others. Whether these commanders held a permanent naval office
or were appointed ad hoc for specific operations is not securely
attested. The surviving Punic inscriptional record uses the
title *rb* ("chief") with various qualifiers but does not
preserve a definitive "naval commander" title that maps onto
the Greek *nauarchos*.

The reporting structure that connected naval commanders to the
Carthaginian senate and the suffeteship is similarly
under-documented. The suffeteship as institution explicitly
separated civilian from military command (treated under the
[suffeteship arc narrative](/narratives/the-suffeteship-arc)),
which implies that naval commanders, like land commanders,
were appointed by the senate and operated outside the
suffeteship's civic office. The structural pattern — civilian
office holding civic authority; separately appointed military
and naval commanders holding operational authority — is one of
the substantive features of the Carthaginian constitution that
distinguishes it from the Roman magistral system, where
consuls held combined civic and military authority.

## The crew problem

The single largest unresolved question about Carthaginian
naval operations is who manned the ships. A fleet of 350
quinqueremes requires approximately 100,000 oarsmen alone, in
addition to marines. The Carthaginian citizen body was not
nearly large enough to provide this manpower from its own
population. The literary tradition's gestures toward
Carthaginian crews — mostly drawn from the broader Punic
population of the African hinterland, plus subject and allied
contingents from the Libyo-Phoenician coastal cities, plus
hired specialists — are plausible but under-evidenced.

The structural implication is that Carthaginian naval power
depended on the same broader-Punic and dependent-population
manpower system that the Carthaginian army depended on. The
political consequences of this dependence are not directly
attested but can be inferred: a naval mobilization disrupted
the agricultural and commercial labor of the same African
populations that the Carthaginian state depended on for
revenue and tribute. The capacity to mobilize was not
unbounded; the political-economic constraint on naval
operations was probably as significant as the fiscal
constraint that the surviving sources emphasize.

## The Treaty of 201 and the strategic constraint

The Treaty of 201 BCE that ended the Second Punic War
substantively dismantled Carthaginian naval capacity. The
state's war fleet was reduced to 10 warships; the rest of the
fleet was surrendered and burned in Carthage's harbor in the
ceremonial humiliation that Livy (30.43) and Appian (8.62)
both preserve. The treaty further prohibited Carthaginian
warfare anywhere without Roman authorization, which
substantively eliminated the conditions under which a Carthaginian
naval fleet would be needed in the first place. Carthage in
the 2nd century BCE was a commercial maritime power with state
naval power reduced to coastal defense and convoy escort. The
commercial fleet continued; the war fleet was substantially
absent.

The fifty-year period between the Treaty of 201 and the start
of the Third Punic War is the principal moment when the
historical link between Carthaginian commerce and Carthaginian
naval power can be tested. The result: commerce recovered to
pre-war levels within a generation (Cato's Carthaginian-fig
demonstration in the senate in the 150s BCE is the iconic
literary trace of this recovery); naval power did not return
in any institutional sense until the emergency rebuild of 149
BCE. The two systems were demonstrably separable. Carthaginian
maritime commercial power did not require a Carthaginian war
fleet to function under the Roman peace; what it required was
Roman acquiescence to commercial operations within the existing
network.

## What the system meant

Carthaginian sea power as institutional system is one of the
substantive Carthaginian state-building achievements of the
ancient Mediterranean. The cothon installation, the
standardized shipbuilding tradition, the mass-mobilization
capacity, and the separated civilian-naval command structure
together constituted a naval institutional apparatus that
operated at a scale and a durability that few contemporary
polities matched. The Roman naval rebuild capacity that won
the First Punic War was substantively a copy of the
Carthaginian system, learned from a captured Carthaginian ship
and reverse-engineered into Roman production at scale (Polybius
1.20). The Carthaginian system was the prototype; the Roman
system was the imitation. The asymmetry of the surviving
historical record — Roman naval achievement extensively
treated, Carthaginian naval achievement mostly subsumed under
"commercial wealth" — substantively understates what the
Carthaginian apparatus was and what it produced.

The site's treatment of Carthaginian seafaring under the
[seafaring and trade theme](/themes/carthaginian-seafaring-and-trade)
covers the commercial side of the maritime story; this
narrative is the institutional-naval companion piece.

## Sources

The principal direct evidence is archaeological: the
[Marsala Punic Warship](/artifacts/marsala-punic-warship), the
[Egadi bronze warship rams](/artifacts/egadi-bronze-ram), and
the topographic remains of the [cothon installation](/places/cothon).
The principal literary sources are Polybius (1.20–28 for the
First Punic War naval narrative; 6.51 for the institutional
comparison with Rome) and Appian (8.96 for the late description
of the cothon installation; 8.93–96 for the Third Punic War
emergency rebuild). The principal modern syntheses are Lancel's
*Carthage: A History* (1995) for the institutional overview,
Hoyos's *The Carthaginians* (2010) for the operational narrative,
and the specialist literature on the Marsala wreck (Frost's
excavation reports) and the Egadi rams (the Royal Ontario
Museum / RPM Nautical Foundation excavation publications).
The
[Krings-edited *Manuel de recherche*](/sources/krings-manuel-1995)
handbook chapters on Carthaginian military and naval
institutions remain the deepest specialist reference.
