## How the war reached Ecnomus

By 256 BCE the First Punic War was eight years old and had
substantially settled into a stalemate in Sicily. Roman armies
held most of eastern Sicily; Carthaginian armies held most of the
western half; a series of indecisive sieges and counter-sieges
through the 250s had not produced a path to either side's victory.
The Roman senate concluded that breaking the deadlock required
shifting the theater. The strategy: build a fleet capable of
moving the legions to Africa, force Carthage to fight on its own
ground, settle the war by direct pressure on the city.

The Roman fleet built for this purpose was unprecedented. The state
had no traditional naval capacity at the war's beginning in 264
BCE; the entire naval infrastructure had been improvised over the
preceding decade through emergency construction programs, foreign
shipwright recruitment, and the *corvus* boarding bridge that
converted Roman naval combat into close-combat fights. By 256 BCE
the Roman fleet was approximately 330 quinqueremes strong, with
the legions destined for Africa loaded aboard. The consuls of the
year, Marcus Atilius Regulus and Lucius Manlius Vulso Longus,
took the fleet south from Sicily in early summer 256 BCE.

The Carthaginian response was a fleet of approximately 350
quinqueremes under the joint command of Hamilcar (a different
Hamilcar from any of the others on this site) and Hanno (a
different Hanno from the others). The Carthaginian objective was
to intercept the Roman fleet before it reached Africa and engage
on terms favorable to Carthaginian naval doctrine: maneuver,
ramming, and the avoidance of close combat. The two fleets met
off Cape Ecnomus on the south coast of Sicily.

## What the fleets looked like

The scale of the engagement is preserved by Polybius (1.26). The
Roman fleet of approximately 330 quinqueremes carried full crews
plus the Roman legions intended for Africa; the Carthaginian
fleet of approximately 350 quinqueremes carried marines and rowers
without an additional embarked army. At standard Polybian crew
figures (300 rowers and 120 marines per quinquereme), the
combined force was approximately 290,000 men. The figure is the
basis for the engagement's claim to be the largest naval battle
of classical antiquity by combatant count.

Whether the standard manning was actually achieved at Ecnomus is
contested in modern scholarship; reduced estimates of 250-300 per
ship would lower the total to 150,000-200,000. Either way, the
engagement is exceptional in scale by any standard available for
ancient naval warfare. The 600+ ships involved is a number not
matched in any other preserved ancient naval engagement.

## How the engagement was fought

The Roman tactical disposition was a triangular wedge in four
squadrons. The first squadron, under Regulus's flagship,
constituted the apex: heavy quinqueremes intended to break the
Carthaginian center on contact. The second squadron, behind the
apex, was towing horse-transports inside the formation. The
third squadron, behind the second, was the reserve. The fourth
squadron, astern of the entire wedge, was the rearguard
protecting the formation's rear and flanks. The configuration's
purpose was to protect the transports while concentrating
offensive capability at the apex; it was tactically distinctive
to this specific operational requirement and was not used in
subsequent engagements.

The Carthaginian plan was envelopment. The Carthaginian center,
opposed to the Roman apex, was designed to give ground deliberately,
drawing the Roman wedge forward; the Carthaginian wings would
then close around the Roman flanks, while a detached squadron
attacked the Roman rearguard. The plan, as Polybius preserves it,
was tactically sound: it took advantage of the Roman wedge's
forward momentum to isolate the apex from the rest of the Roman
fleet.

The execution worked as designed in its first phase. The
Carthaginian center fell back under Roman pressure; the Roman
apex pursued; the Carthaginian wings closed; the Roman second-line
squadrons (towing transports) had to disengage from the transports
and engage the Carthaginian wings; the Carthaginian detachment
attacked the Roman rearguard. The single engagement broke into
three separate fights.

In each of the three fights, the Roman ships prevailed. The
operative variable, as Polybius repeatedly emphasizes, was the
*corvus*: once Roman ships were in contact, the boarding bridge
allowed Roman marines to swing onto Carthaginian decks and turn
the engagement into a close-combat fight. Roman legionaries in
close combat, on enemy decks, were what Roman naval doctrine of
this period was actually about. Carthaginian crews, equipped for
maneuver-and-ramming combat, were not equipped to win the
boarding fight that the *corvus* forced.

By the day's end the Roman fleet had sunk approximately 30
Carthaginian ships and captured 64 more, against approximately
30 Roman ships sunk and no Roman ships captured. The Carthaginian
withdrawal opened the path for Regulus and Vulso to land the
legions in Africa within weeks of the engagement.

## What followed

The African expedition of 256-255 BCE is treated separately on
this site. Its short version: Regulus and Vulso landed in
Africa in late summer 256, won early engagements, advanced into
the Carthaginian hinterland; Vulso was recalled to Rome with
part of the army, leaving Regulus with reduced forces; the
Carthaginian government, facing destruction, recruited the
Spartan mercenary general Xanthippus, who reorganized the
Carthaginian army around its African and elephant components and
defeated Regulus at the Battle of the Bagradas River in 255 BCE.
Regulus was captured. The campaign that Cape Ecnomus had opened
ended in catastrophic Roman defeat in less than a year.

The naval consequences were nearly as bad. The Roman fleet
attempting to evacuate the surviving forces from Africa was
caught in a storm off Camarina and lost approximately 280 of its
ships. Roman casualties from the storm exceeded those from any
single engagement of the war. The *corvus*'s contribution to ship
instability in heavy weather has been suggested by modern
scholarship as a partial cause; the device was abandoned in
subsequent Roman fleet construction.

The strategic outcome of the year that began with Ecnomus was
therefore catastrophic for Rome despite the naval victory. The
fleet that Ecnomus had vindicated was destroyed within months by
storm; the army it had landed was destroyed in Africa within a
year. The war continued for another fifteen years before the
Aegates engagement finally ended it.

## What Cape Ecnomus meant

Three things, distinct from the strategic outcome of 255 BCE.

**The Roman naval revolution worked.** A state with no traditional
naval capacity in 264 BCE had built, in twelve years, a fleet
capable of beating the principal Phoenician naval power of the
Mediterranean at scale, in fleet engagement, on terms favorable
to its own tactical innovations. The structural fact of Roman
naval mobilization in this period is the longer story Cape Ecnomus
demonstrates.

**The *corvus* was decisive in this period of the war.** The
boarding bridge that turned naval combat into close-combat
infantry fights was the single tactical innovation that converted
Roman naval improvisation into operational success. Subsequent
naval history would learn to neutralize the device and Rome
itself would abandon it; but in the 260s and 250s BCE, in fleet
engagements where Roman ships could close to boarding range, the
*corvus* won.

**Naval combat at this scale was sustainable for both states for
roughly one generation.** Ecnomus is followed by the Camarina
storm and by a series of Roman naval losses through the 250s and
240s that drove the state to private-subscription fleet financing
by 242 BCE. Carthage's capacity to replace fleet losses was
similarly stressed; by 241 BCE the Aegates fleet was undermanned
and supply-loaded because the city no longer had the cash flow or
the trained crews to mount a properly prepared engagement. The
naval war the two states fought between 264 and 241 BCE was at the
upper limit of what either could sustain. Ecnomus is the moment
when this was most visible.
