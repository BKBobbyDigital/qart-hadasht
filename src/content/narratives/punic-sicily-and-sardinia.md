The conventional Anglophone treatment of Punic Sicily and
Sardinia tends to read both islands through the wars: Sicily as
the contested ground of the Greco-Punic wars and then the First
Punic War, Sardinia as the strategic asset that Carthage
developed for grain and that Rome stripped off in 238 BCE
during the Mercenary War's aftermath. This is not wrong as a
political summary. It is, however, the wrong shape for the
cultural and material history of the two islands, both of which
sustained Punic urban civilizations of substantial scale,
substantial sophistication, and substantial duration. The
fourth and third centuries BCE are when those civilizations
reached their fullest development. The Roman conquests of 241
and 238 did not end them; they continued under Roman
administration with sustained Punic cultural and linguistic
signatures into the imperial period and, in Sardinia
particularly, into late antiquity.

The shape this narrative gives to the period is parallel to
the shape the site gives to the
[Numidian-Punic interface](/narratives/the-numidian-punic-interface)
and to the
[Phoenician colonial network theme](/themes/phoenician-colonial-network):
the central cultural reality is the integrated provincial
Punic world rather than the metropolitan center alone, and the
provincial Punic world had its own internal history that the
metropolitan-and-wars framing obscures.

## Punic Sicily: the western zone

The Carthaginian Sicilian footprint after the
[Battle of Himera](/events/battle-of-himera) in 480 BCE was
organized around four principal cities and a network of
secondary settlements. [Motya](/places/motya), the offshore
island settlement north of modern Marsala, was the historic
Phoenician anchor, founded probably in the eighth century BCE
and developed across the sixth and fifth centuries into a
fortified urban center with a tophet, substantial domestic
architecture, and the underwater causeway connecting it to the
mainland that the surviving archaeology has traced. Panormus
(modern Palermo) was the principal Carthaginian-period harbor
city on the north coast. Solus (Soluntum) and the inland
Elymian cities at Eryx and Segesta rounded out the network.

The destruction of Motya by Dionysius I in 397 BCE forced a
substantial reorganization. [Lilybaeum](/places/lilybaeum) was
founded in 396 on the western tip of Sicily, opposite Cap Bon
across the channel to Africa, as the new principal Carthaginian
Sicilian city. The site selection was strategic: a defensible
peninsula with deep-water harborage, positioned at the shortest
crossing to Carthage itself, with the existing inland network
of Punic and Elymian cities accessible by short overland
movement. Lilybaeum across the fourth and third centuries
developed into one of the major cities of the western
Mediterranean, with monumental walls (substantial sections of
which survive), a major harbor, and an urban population the
ancient sources treat as comparable in scale to the principal
Greek-Sicilian cities. The Roman siege of 250–241 BCE during
the [First Punic War](/events/first-punic-war) failed to take
the city by storm or sustained siege; Lilybaeum was surrendered
under the [Treaty of Lutatius](/events/treaty-of-lutatius)
rather than reduced.

The cultural production of western Sicily across the fourth
and third centuries was thoroughly mixed. The Elymian cities at
Eryx and Segesta were not Phoenician-Punic foundations; they
were indigenous Sicilian centers with their own pre-Phoenician
identity that the Carthaginian period overlaid without
replacing. The temple of Astarte at Eryx, attested across the
period and continued under Roman administration as the temple
of Venus Erycina, is the principal Sicilian example of the
Phoenician-Greek-indigenous religious synthesis that the period
produced. The cult itself was almost certainly Phoenician in
origin (Astarte's principal western sanctuary) and acquired its
Greco-Roman framing across the long contact period without
losing its Phoenician core; the Roman *Venus Erycina*
transferred to the Capitoline in 217 BCE was the same goddess
in a different cultic frame.

Sicilian Punic coinage is the other principal evidence base.
The Carthaginian Sicilian mint, operating from the late fifth
century onward, produced silver and bronze issues that
combined Carthaginian iconography (the palm tree, the horse and
palm, the Tanit head) with Greek metrological standards and
Greek inscriptional conventions. The
[Sicilian-Punic silver tetradrachms](/artifacts/carthage-tetradrachm)
of the fourth century, with their explicit *mhnt* and *mhshbm*
(camp / paymasters) legends in Punic script, are the earliest
substantive Carthaginian coinage and were produced specifically
for the Sicilian theater. The integration of Greek metrological
and Punic-script conventions on a single coinage is one of the
clearest pieces of evidence for the cultural-mixing character
of Sicilian Punic civilization.

## Punic Sardinia: the longer settlement

Sardinia's Phoenician presence is older and the Punic period
deeper than Sicily's. Phoenician trading posts on the southern
coast at [Nora](/places/nora) and [Sulci](/places/sulci) are
attested from the late ninth century BCE, predating the
traditional foundation date of Carthage itself. By the seventh
century the network extended along the western coast to
[Tharros](/places/tharros) on the Sinis peninsula, with inland
settlements at Monte Sirai and Pani Loriga developed across the
sixth and fifth centuries. The transition from a Phoenician
trading-post network to a Carthaginian-political domain is
conventionally dated to the
[Magonid conquest of Sardinia](/events/magonid-conquest-of-sardinia)
in the late sixth century, but the cultural-material record is
continuous; what changed was the political-tributary
relationship rather than the underlying urban culture.

The fourth and third centuries are when Punic Sardinia reached
its fullest urban and cultural development. Sulci on the
southwestern coast had become a major harbor city with its own
tophet (the Sulci tophet is one of the largest in the Punic
world, with thousands of stelae and urns recovered), monumental
public architecture, and a substantial population. Tharros,
similarly, developed in this period as the principal western
Sardinian center, with its harbor, its acropolis, its
necropoleis on the surrounding hills, and its own tophet
producing distinct regional iconographic conventions on the
stelae. [Caralis](/places/caralis) (modern Cagliari) developed
across the same period as the principal southern Sardinian
city, the role it would retain through the Roman period and
into the modern.

The Sardinian Punic interior was a different matter. The
indigenous Nuragic civilization, organized around the
distinctive stone-tower settlements that survive in extraordinary
density across the island, was not replaced by Punic
urbanization but coexisted with it across a complex
boundary. The Carthaginian-period inscriptions and material
record suggest a tributary-and-trade relationship between the
coastal Punic cities and the inland Nuragic communities, with
the coastal cities collecting agricultural surplus from the
inland communities and supplying manufactured goods and
luxury imports in return. The relationship was occasionally
violent (Carthaginian inscriptions document military
operations into the interior across the fifth and fourth
centuries) but not transformative; the Nuragic-cultural
interior persisted across the Carthaginian period and continued
into the Roman.

The Sardinian-Punic religious record is one of the richest in
the western Mediterranean. The Sulci and Tharros tophets
produced thousands of stelae across centuries, with regional
iconographic conventions distinct from the Carthage tophet's
and from each other's. The [Nora stone](/artifacts/nora-stone),
a Phoenician inscription dated to the late ninth or early
eighth century BCE, is one of the oldest Phoenician
inscriptions in the western Mediterranean and the earliest
direct evidence for Phoenician presence on the island. The
broader Sardinian Phoenician-Punic inscriptional corpus
provides much of the comparative evidence for reconstructing
Punic religious vocabulary and formulaic conventions.

## What the Roman conquests did not end

Sicily passed to Roman administration in 241 BCE under the
Treaty of Lutatius; Sardinia followed in 238 BCE after the
opportunistic Roman intervention during the
[Mercenary War](/narratives/the-mercenary-war). What the
Roman administrative transitions did not do was end the Punic
cultural and linguistic life of either island. The
inscriptional record demonstrates this directly. Punic and
Neo-Punic inscriptions continue across both islands through
the Roman Republican period and into the imperial period:
votive inscriptions in Punic at Sardinian sanctuaries through
the first century BCE and beyond, Latin-Punic and
Greek-Punic bilingual texts attesting Punic-speaking
populations under Roman administration, and theophoric
naming patterns that preserve Punic religious vocabulary in
Latin-language epigraphic contexts.

The Sulcis and Tharros sanctuaries continued in use under
Roman administration, with their Punic dedicatory practices
visible in the surviving stelae. The Eryx temple of Astarte
continued as Venus Erycina, with the Phoenician cultic
substrate visible underneath the Roman superstructure. The
Lilybaeum tophet continued into the Roman period. The
Sardinian rural sanctuary at Antas, dedicated to the
indigenous god Sardus Pater, has produced Punic inscriptions
of Roman date showing the persistence of Punic religious
vocabulary in a non-urban setting through the late Republican
and early imperial periods.

The broader pattern is the one the
[Punic continuity in Roman Africa narrative](/narratives/punic-continuity-in-roman-africa)
treats in the African context: Roman political conquest did
not produce immediate cultural rupture, and the Punic
cultural-religious-linguistic substrate persisted alongside
and underneath the Roman administrative overlay for centuries.
Sicily and Sardinia are the islands' versions of the same
phenomenon. The Punic-period evidence is less voluminous than
Africa's (and the Sardinian record more substantial than the
Sicilian, because Sicily's intensive Roman and post-Roman
occupation displaced more of the Punic material context), but
the pattern is the same.

## What this changes about the metropolitan story

Reading Hellenistic Punic Sicily and Sardinia as cultural
productions in their own right, rather than as Carthaginian
projections, changes the metropolitan story in two ways. First,
the political-economic base of Carthaginian power across the
fourth and third centuries was not just the African hinterland;
it was the integrated provincial network of which the western
Sicilian and Sardinian cities were the principal nodes. The
Carthaginian fiscal, military, and commercial capacity that
sustained the wars with Syracuse and the war with Rome
depended substantially on the provincial network's productivity
and on the cultural-political coherence that allowed the network
to function. The Mercenary War of 241–237 (which broke out in
Sicily and ran out of African resources before Roman
intervention complicated it) is a case study in what happened
when the provincial-metropolitan integration came under stress.

Second, the cultural production of the provincial Punic world
was not derivative. The Sardinian tophet iconographic
traditions, the Sicilian Punic coinage, the Lilybaeum and
Tharros monumental architecture, the religious-cultural
syntheses at Eryx and Antas, were Punic productions developed
in the provincial cities themselves with their own regional
character. What the metropolitan-and-wars framing treats as
peripheral or derivative is more accurately treated as the
productive cultural core of Punic civilization across the
period. The metropolitan Carthage was the largest single
node, but the cultural production was distributed across the
network, and the provincial nodes had their own internal
histories that did not run only through the metropolis.

The framing the site adopts is parallel to its framing of the
[Phoenician colonial network theme](/themes/phoenician-colonial-network):
the network is the unit, the nodes within it have their own
agency and their own internal history, and the metropolitan
center is the largest node within a larger system rather than
the system's organizing principle. Hellenistic Punic Sicily
and Sardinia are the strongest case for the framing, because
the material and inscriptional record from both islands is
substantial enough to reconstruct the provincial cultural
production directly, without having to infer it from the
metropolitan record alone.
