## Three groups, one coalition

The [Mercenary War](/events/mercenary-war) of 241–237 BCE
was not a single uprising. It was a coalition of separate
movements with overlapping but not identical grievances.
The mercenary contingents Carthage had brought back from
Sicily — under Gisco, the demobilization officer (a
different figure from the later
[Hasdrubal Gisco](/people/hasdrubal-gisco)) — carried a
pay dispute that the Carthaginian treasury, exhausted by
the war indemnity to Rome, could not settle promptly. The
Libyan African populations who lived under Carthaginian
tribute and conscription joined the revolt because the
indemnity had pushed those exactions past what they were
willing to absorb. The Gallic contingent within the
Sicilian veterans had its own position: a foreign fighting
force whose post-war fate depended entirely on what
Carthage chose to do.

Each group had its own leadership. Pulling all three
together into a coordinated rebellion is what made the
Mercenary War the most dangerous threat Carthage faced
before 149 BCE. The three commanders Polybius
foregrounds — [Spendius](/people/spendius),
[Mathos](/people/mathos), and
[Autaritus](/people/autaritus) — match those three groups.
Other names appear at lower resolution; Zarzas is one, and
Naravas fought on the rebel side before defecting to
Hamilcar in 238. But Spendius, Mathos, and Autaritus are
the leadership the surviving record actually lets us see.

## Spendius: the man who could not surrender

Polybius records (1.69, 1.79) what was at stake for
Spendius personally: he was an escaped Roman slave of
Campanian origin, and any settlement that put him back into
Roman or pro-Roman jurisdiction meant death. That fact is
the engine of much of what happens. When Gisco offered
terms on the pay dispute early in the revolt, Spendius led
the refusal. When the Carthaginian senate sent envoys to
discuss settlement, Spendius led the escalation that ended
in the envoys being killed — the moment that brought the
"truceless war" character into being, treated under the
[truceless-character claim](/claims/mercenary-war-truceless).
When Hamilcar began closing in during the war's last
phase, Spendius pushed to keep fighting rather than seek
terms. He had no exit. That made him the leader who held
the coalition committed past the points where the others
might have given way.

His background also gave him a particular standing among
the mercenary contingents. The Sicilian veterans included
Greeks, Italians, Iberians, Libyans, Gauls, and Balearic
islanders, and none of those groups had a common political
language. Spendius worked in Greek, the lingua franca of
the mercenary recruitment system. A man who had been a
Roman slave and was now leading an army against a major
Mediterranean state was, in a way other rebel leaders were
not, a figure who could speak to soldiers whose own
positions were socially precarious. The rebellion as a
coalition of the structurally marginal is what his
leadership most fully embodied.

## Mathos: the weight of Libya

Mathos's position rested on a different foundation. The
Libyan African populations were not a mercenary force; they
were Carthage's tributary subjects, and their decision to
join the revolt is what turned a mercenary mutiny into an
existential threat. Without them, the war could have been
contained locally. With them, it took up almost the whole
of Hamilcar Barca's military attention for nearly four
years.

Polybius records the Libyan adherence at 1.70, and the
[Libyan-revolt claim](/claims/mercenary-war-libyan-revolt)
treats the moment as the war's transformation. The Libyan
grievances were not the mercenary pay dispute: the
indemnity owed to Rome had pushed Carthage to extract more
from its tributary subjects, and joining the revolt had
become less risky than continuing under the existing tax.
Mathos led that constituency. His leadership gave the
rebellion its strongest claim to be a war of the African
populations against Carthaginian hegemony rather than a
mutiny by hired soldiers.

Polybius treats Mathos differently from Spendius. Where
Spendius's rigidity is explained by his personal stake,
Mathos appears as the leader Carthage most needed to
publicly defeat. His execution at Tunis, after the war's
end, is preserved in unusual detail: he was paraded
through the streets in a humiliating procession before
being put to death (1.88). The ceremonial character of the
execution is one of the few moments where the surviving
record opens any window into Carthaginian state ritual,
and the weight Carthage chose to give the moment is itself
a measure of how seriously the state took what Mathos had
led.

## Autaritus: the man who could speak to everyone

Autaritus had served long enough with Carthaginian forces
to speak Phoenician — Polybius writes Φοινικιστὶ, meaning
Punic as it was used in the Carthaginian command system
(1.80). He was the only rebel leader who could address the
whole coalition in the language of the state they had
revolted against. That capability mattered. The rebel
coalition spoke at least six languages: Greek, the Latin
and Italian dialects, Punic, Libyan, Iberian, and Celtic.
Autaritus's own Gallic contingent used Celtic among
themselves and Greek with the rest of the Sicilian
veterans. The ability to switch into Punic and speak
directly to soldiers who had served their whole working
lives under that command vocabulary was a real political
asset, and Polybius treats it as one.

His most visible role comes at Polybius 1.80, where the
coalition debated what to do with the Carthaginian
prisoners after the killing of Gisco and the envoys.
Autaritus argued for torture and execution rather than
return, and Polybius attributes the coalition's adoption
of that line partly to his ability to make the argument in
each of the three principal languages at once. The
multilingual leadership capacity was a concrete form of
political power inside the coalition, and Autaritus was the
one man who held it.

## The three as a working leadership

Polybius frames the Mercenary War as barbaric and impious
(the *aspondos polemos* line, the "truceless war"), and
the framing treats the rebel side as a chaotic mass of
marginal peoples united only by opposition to civilized
order. The leadership pattern preserved in his own account
shows something different: a coordinated coalition with
political organization, leadership mapped onto distinct
constituencies with distinct grievances, and the kind of
multi-language coordination needed to keep a multi-year
campaign together against the most militarily capable state
in the western Mediterranean.

This does not soften what the war actually was. The
reciprocal atrocities — the killing of the Carthaginian
envoys, the torture and execution of prisoners, the
Carthaginian counter-atrocities under Hamilcar, the
crucifixions of the captured rebel commanders at the close
— are not literary fabrication. The truceless character is
real, and the
[structural-atrocity editorial take](/editorialTakes/mercenary-war-atrocity-structural)
treats it as a product of the war's particular conditions
rather than of individual cruelty. But the picture of the
rebel side as politically incoherent does not survive
careful reading of the same Polybian account that gives us
the framing. Spendius, Mathos, and Autaritus held a working
coalition together for three years against Hamilcar.

## How it ended

[Hamilcar Barca's](/people/hamilcar-barca) operations broke
the coalition in 238 BCE. The
[Battle of the Saw](/events/battle-of-the-saw) trapped
Spendius's force at a defile and forced negotiations.
Polybius (1.85) records that Spendius and the other rebel
envoys were seized during the truce parley itself —
Hamilcar treated the parley as a tactical opening rather
than a settlement. Autaritus appears to have been taken in
the same sequence. Both were crucified outside the walls of
Tunis. Mathos held on a short while longer, until the final
engagement near Tunis, where he was captured. Carthage
paraded him through the streets and then executed him.

The collective treatment is what the sources actually
support. Each man, taken alone, appears at limited
resolution: Spendius in the most detail (Polybius gives him
substantial narrative weight), Mathos in moderate detail,
Autaritus least of all. Read together, they are the
leadership of the most dangerous conflict Carthage faced
before the destruction. The wider conditions of the war —
the pay dispute that started it, the Libyan adherence that
scaled it, the truceless character that defined how it was
fought, the Sardinian seizure that Rome extracted from
Carthage's exhaustion, the Barcid political authority that
Hamilcar's victory produced — are treated in the
[Mercenary War narrative](/narratives/the-mercenary-war).
This page is the companion treatment of the people who led
the other side.
