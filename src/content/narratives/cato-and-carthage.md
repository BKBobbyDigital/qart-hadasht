  Roman tradition remembers Cato as the man who talked Rome into

  senate gave in. The sentence in its famous form is modern, the refrain

  considerable: a veteran of the Hannibalic war who argued for war from

## A soldier of Hannibal's war

Marcus Porcius Cato was born at Tusculum in 234 BCE, into a family with
no senatorial ancestors, and reached the consulship as a *novus homo*, a
new man. He made his first campaign at seventeen, when Hannibal was in
Italy (Plutarch *Cato Maior* 1). In Cicero's dialogue on old age he
recalls going to Capua as a young soldier in 214, to Tarentum in 209,
and becoming quaestor in 204 (*De senectute* 10). Nepos adds a military
tribunate in Sicily and service under Claudius Nero at the Metaurus,
where Hasdrubal Barca was killed (*Cato* 1). Miles suggests that his
hostility to Carthage was born in these campaigns (p. 335), which is a
reasonable guess and not something any source says.

His quaestorship put him under Scipio in Sicily before the invasion of
Africa, and Plutarch has the two quarrel over Scipio's spending on his
troops (*Cato Maior* 3); Nepos says Cato disagreed with him for the rest
of his life. He was praetor in Sardinia in 198, consul in 195 with a
command in Hither Spain and a triumph, and censor in 184, the office
that fixed his reputation for severity. In the attacks on the Scipio
brothers in the 180s Livy calls him the foremost of Africanus's enemies,
says it was thought that the tribunes who prosecuted Africanus acted at
his instigation, and records that he spoke for the motion against
Lucius Scipio (38.54). The Scipionic quarrel and the Carthaginian
question were separate matters, but they involved the same men for
forty years.

## The embassy

The embassy on which Cato served went to Africa to arbitrate one more of
Masinissa's border disputes, and it arbitrated nothing. Masinissa agreed
to submit everything to the envoys. The Carthaginians, expecting no
justice from past experience, refused to have the treaty Scipio had made
reopened and asked only that breaches of it be judged, and the envoys
went home (Appian *Punica* 69). The date is usually given as 153; Appian
does not give one, and some modern accounts prefer 152.

What they saw mattered more. Appian's envoys found the country carefully
cultivated and the city grown in wealth and population since Zama, and
Cato said Rome's liberty would never be secure until Carthage was
destroyed. Plutarch's Cato found the city full of fighting men, wealth,
arms and military supplies, and told the senate that the defeats had cut
Carthage's recklessness rather than its power and would make it more
skilled in war, and that its war with Masinissa was a rehearsal for one
with Rome (*Cato Maior* 26). The summary of Livy's Book 47 has the envoys
report a store of ship timber at Carthage. In each version the recovery
is presented as rearmament, and this is the ground on which the
[editorial take on the destruction](/editorialTakes/destruction-not-weak-enough)
builds.

Then comes the fig, which survives in two versions. Plutarch has Cato
let a large Libyan fig fall from the folds of his toga and tell the
admiring senators that the land where it grew was three days' sail from
Rome (*Cato Maior* 27). Pliny has him hold up an early fig and ask when
they thought it had been picked, and when they agreed it was fresh, tell
them it was picked at Carthage *tertium ante diem*, the day before
yesterday by Roman counting (*Natural History* 15.74–75). Pliny adds that
Cato was anxious for the safety of his descendants. Modern
retellings, Miles's among them, tend to combine the two.

## The refrain

Plutarch says Cato added to his vote on any question whatever that "it
seems to me that Carthage must not exist" (27). Appian says it is said
that he voiced the opinion continually from then on (69). Pliny has him
shouting at every meeting of the senate that Carthage must be destroyed,
and Florus that he declared it even when consulted about something else
(1.31). All four wrote in the first or second century CE. The earlier
Latin texts give Cato's hostility without the refrain: in Cicero's
dialogue, set in 150, he says he will not stop fearing Carthage until he
learns it has been destroyed (*De senectute* 18), and the summary of
Livy's Book 49 has him urge that Carthage be removed and destroyed. The
phrases now quoted, *Carthago delenda est* and *ceterum censeo
Carthaginem esse delendam*, appear in no ancient text. The evidence is
set out in [the claim on the phrase](/claims/cato-delenda-est-historicity).

None of this makes the refrain false. A senator's habit is exactly the
kind of detail a speech tradition preserves, and Cato's speeches were
still read in the late Republic. It means the sentence was remembered
rather than recorded, and it reached its familiar form long after
antiquity.

## Nasica and the senate

The story of a man repeating himself until Rome did what he said leaves
out that the senate kept refusing. The summary of Livy's Book 48 has Cato
propose war three times, each time on a report of Carthaginian arming,
and each time the senate follows Scipio Nasica Corculum instead: it sends
envoys to see what is happening, then ten more to investigate, and then
resolves on war only if Carthage does not burn its fleet and dismiss its
army. Nasica's stated grounds are that nothing should be done rashly and
that no just cause for war yet seemed to exist. Plutarch says Nasica
ended his votes with the opinion that Carthage should be spared (27).

Nasica is better remembered for another argument, that Rome needed a
Carthage to fear to keep its people in hand. Plutarch, Appian, Florus
and Diodorus give it, and Plutarch pairs it with its mirror image: Cato
thought Rome, drunk with power, should be rid of external threats so it
could attend to its own failings. Both men arguing from the state of
Roman morals looks like the later tradition's framing, and Miles points
out that Diodorus wrote it knowing the civil wars that followed (p. 337).
The reason in Livy's summary has no hindsight in it, and it is an
objection to fighting without a pretext rather than to destroying
Carthage. [The claim on Nasica's opposition](/claims/nasica-opposition-to-delenda-est)
weighs the two.

Cato won in 149. Carthage had fought Masinissa in 150 without Roman
consent, the senate declared war, and Carthaginian envoys came to Rome to
surrender the city. According to the summary of Livy's Book 49, it was
Cato's opinion that prevailed: the decree of war was to stand and the
consuls to leave for Africa as soon as possible. Appian says the senate
had resolved on war after the embassy and waited only for a pretext (69).

## Cause or voice

The Roman tradition, Plutarch most of all, makes Cato the cause of the
Third Punic War (*Cato Maior* 27: "in this way Cato is said to have
brought to pass" the war). The evidence pulls both ways. For it: he was the
war's most persistent advocate for four years, and his motion carried
after the surrender, when the senate could have stopped. Against it: the
senate repeatedly preferred Nasica's caution, other senators urged war
alongside Cato, and what turned the argument was Carthage's war with
Masinissa, which Cato did not arrange. The site's
[editorial take](/editorialTakes/destruction-not-weak-enough) treats his
advocacy as real and its causal weight as something the sources do not
let us measure, and this narrative follows it.

## Death

Cato died in 149 at eighty-five, after the war had begun. The summary of
Livy's Book 49 has him in the senate that year praising the young Scipio
Aemilianus's service at the siege, and Plutarch gives the line he used,
from the *Odyssey*: he alone has understanding, the rest are flitting
shadows (*Cato Maior* 27). In the same year he spoke for the Lusitanians
whom Servius Galba had massacred (*Periochae* 49). Aemilianus, whom he
had praised, destroyed Carthage three years later.

## A note on sources

Plutarch's *Cato Maior* is the fullest ancient life, and Nepos's short
*Cato* and Cicero's *De senectute* add career details; the dialogue puts
Cicero's words in Cato's mouth and is evidence of how he was remembered,
not of what he said. Livy covers his career up to 167 in the surviving
books; for the 150s there is only the summary of the lost books. Appian
(*Punica* 69) gives the embassy, and Pliny and Florus preserve the Latin
form of the refrain. Cato's own *De Agricultura* survives complete, and
his speeches and *Origines* in fragments; none of them contains the
saying.
