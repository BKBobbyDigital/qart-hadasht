## Background and early career

Marcus Porcius Cato was born in 234 BCE at Tusculum, of a
modest Sabine landowner family. The political category for
his trajectory was *novus homo*, a new man entering the
Roman senatorial elite without ancestral consular precedent, and the cultural-political program he developed across his
long career was substantially the program of an outsider
asserting the moral authority of older Roman traditions
against the Hellenizing aristocratic culture he saw as
displacing them.

Cato's military service in the Second Punic War (he served
as a young military tribune from approximately 217 BCE) gave
him direct experience of the Hannibalic threat. His early
political career through the 200s and 190s BCE produced a
quaestorship under Scipio Africanus in 204 BCE (notably
hostile despite his subordinate role), a praetorship in 198
BCE (with command in Sardinia), and a consulship in 195 BCE
(with operations in Iberia that earned him a triumph). His
censorship in 184 BCE was the office that established his
political character most fully: stringent enforcement of
moral-economic restrictions on senatorial conduct, attacks
on Greek-Hellenizing imports and practices, and the
systematic political program against the Scipionic-aligned
political faction that the post-war Roman elite had
substantially become.

The specifically anti-Carthaginian phase of Cato's career
came late, in his sixties and seventies. The political
prosecutions of the Scipios in the late 180s BCE were the
moment Cato's authority was most consequential; the
*delenda est* campaign of the late 150s BCE was its
extension into a different political target.

## The embassy to Carthage (153 BCE)

The proximate origin of the *delenda est* campaign was a
senatorial embassy to Carthage in 153 BCE, of which Cato was
a member. The embassy's nominal purpose was mediation in a
boundary dispute between Carthage and Masinissa; its
substantive purpose was assessment of Carthaginian recovery.
Cato (then approximately 80) returned to Rome shocked at
what he had seen.

The Carthage of 153 BCE was substantially recovered from the
post-Zama settlement. The Lutatius indemnity had been paid
in full; the city's commercial network was operational; the
agricultural hinterland produced revenue at scale; the urban
fabric was prosperous. Cato is preserved as having
demonstrated this prosperity to the Roman senate in
characteristically dramatic fashion: he produced a fresh
Carthaginian fig in the senate chamber, allowed the senators
to admire its size and ripeness, and observed that the country
producing such fruit was three days' sail from Rome
(Plutarch, *Cato Maior* 27).

The figs episode is preserved across multiple sources and is
generally treated as historically substantial. Whether the
specific dramatic gesture (fresh figs as visible evidence)
occurred in the precise form preserved is partly later
reconstruction; the underlying political point, Cato's
post-embassy advocacy for Carthage's destruction was based on
his direct observation of the city's recovery, is firmly
attested.

## The *delenda est* campaign

From 153 BCE until his death in 149 BCE, Cato's senatorial
speeches reportedly ended with a call for the destruction of
Carthage. The Latin formulation tradition records as
*ceterum censeo Carthaginem esse delendam* ("furthermore, I
think Carthage must be destroyed") is later crystallization;
no contemporary source preserves the exact words Cato used.
The pattern of repetitive senatorial advocacy is firmly
attested.

The political-rhetorical technique was distinctive. Cato did
not argue for destruction in formal senate speeches dedicated
to the Carthaginian question; he attached the call to
speeches on other subjects, treating the destruction as
self-evident consequence rather than contested policy. The
technique made political opposition difficult: opposing the
*delenda est* required formal challenge to a position Cato
was not formally proposing.

Plutarch (*Cato Maior* 26-27) and the broader Roman
biographical tradition treat the campaign as the principal
political driver of Roman policy toward Carthage in the late
150s BCE. The senatorial faction aligned against Cato (led by
Scipio Nasica Corculum, the cousin of Africanus) advocated
preservation of Carthage on different but also strategic
grounds: Nasica's preserved argument was that Roman political
discipline depended on the persistent external threat, and
that destroying Carthage would remove the structural
condition for Roman moral seriousness. The two positions
contested the question through the 150s BCE; Cato's prevailed
by the time of the 149 BCE declaration of war.

## Cato as cause and as articulation

The simpler reading of the *delenda est* tradition makes
Cato the singular cause of Carthage's destruction. Modern
scholarship is generally cautious. Three observations.

The structural conditions were independent of Cato. The
Treaty of 201 BCE's allied-protection clause and Masinissa's
enabled encroachments had been preparing the legal-
strategic framework for war for fifty years. The specific
trigger (Carthage's military operation against Masinissa in
150 BCE in violation of the treaty) would have produced
Roman response with or without Cato's advocacy.

The senatorial decision was not Cato alone. The senate's
decision to pursue destruction in 149 BCE was reached after
extensive deliberation involving the broader senatorial
faction. Cato's *delenda est* campaign articulated a position
the senate was independently reaching; it did not produce that
position.

Cato's death (149 BCE) preceded the war's outbreak. The
formal Roman declaration came shortly after Cato's death.
The decision was Cato-aligned; the execution was not Cato's.
The destruction of Carthage in 146 BCE was carried out by
Scipio Aemilianus, born approximately 184 BCE — a man whose
political identity was substantially Cato-aligned but whose
actual command of the destruction was a generation removed
from Cato's life.

The Roman moral-political tradition's framing of Cato as
singular cause is best read as posthumous symbolic
construction. Cato was the most rhetorically prominent
advocate; he was not the structural cause. The simpler version
of the story is the one ancient moralizing biography
preferred; the more honest version requires distinguishing
Cato's articulation from the structural causation.

## Death (149 BCE)

Cato died in 149 BCE at age 85, just before the formal Roman
declaration of war on Carthage. The senatorial decision had
been reached but not yet executed at the moment of his
death. The Roman embassies that would deliver the escalating
demands at Carthage through 149 BCE (surrender of hostages,
surrender of weapons, abandonment of the city) followed
within weeks. The war that produced the destruction in 146
BCE was substantially Cato-aligned in its strategic
conception but executed by figures (the consuls of 149-147
BCE, then Scipio Aemilianus from 147 BCE) who had not been
his direct political associates.

Cato's funeral was conducted with the standard senatorial
honors. His writings, including the agricultural treatise
*De Agricultura* (the earliest preserved Latin prose work),
the historical *Origines* (preserved only in fragments), and
the speeches on which his political reputation rested
(preserved in fragments through Cicero and the rhetorical
tradition), entered the Roman literary tradition as
foundational texts of conservative Latin culture. The
political-cultural program he had articulated outlasted him
substantially: the Catonian tradition of Roman moralism,
agricultural primitivism, and resistance to Hellenizing
imports remained a recognizable element of Roman political
culture into the imperial period.

## Legacy and reception

Three observations on Cato's reception.

**The *delenda est* tradition is part of Latin literature
itself.** Cicero's references, Livy's preserved excerpts,
Plutarch's full biography (one of the most-read works of
the Plutarchan corpus), and the early modern reception
through Petrarch and others made Cato into a cultural
figure whose specific political program was largely subsumed
under his persona as exemplary Roman moralist. The
*delenda est* line became proverbial; it is now most often
encountered in modern political rhetoric where the original
context is largely lost.

The Carthage advocacy itself has been read variously.
Modern scholarship has been divided between treating Cato's
position as defensible strategic analysis (Carthaginian
recovery did represent a long-term strategic question for
Rome) and treating it as moral-political pathology (the
sustained advocacy of destroying a city Rome had defeated
half a century earlier suggests a pathological feature of
the late Republican Roman political culture).

The Cato-Africanus opposition is structurally important.
Cato's lifelong opposition to the Scipionic faction is one
of the principal political contests of the Roman second
century BCE. The opposition produced the Trials of the
Scipios that drove Africanus from public life; it produced
the *delenda est* campaign whose target was substantially
the Africanus-imposed peace of 201 BCE; it produced the
political configuration in which Scipio Aemilianus (Africanus's
adopted grandson, Cato-aligned in formation) would eventually
execute the destruction the Catonian program had advocated.
The two figures' political dialogue across two generations
substantially shaped Roman political life from Zama to the
Gracchan crisis.

## A note on sources

Cato is preserved principally in Plutarch's *Cato Maior*, the
most substantial single ancient biography of the figure;
in Cicero's references throughout the rhetorical and
philosophical works (Cicero treated Cato as a model of
older-Roman moral seriousness); and in the surviving
fragments of his own writings (most fully *De Agricultura*).
Livy 33-50 (largely preserved) and the surviving fragments of
later books cover the political career across the relevant
decades. Modern scholarship is extensive; Astin's *Cato the
Censor* (1978) is the standard scholarly biography; recent
work has substantially complicated the older "moralist
exemplar" framing.
