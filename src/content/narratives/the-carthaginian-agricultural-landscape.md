  through Mago, the named author whose twenty-eight-book treatise
  the Roman senate preserved after 146 BCE. Mago is load-bearing
  for what Carthaginian agricultural science was as a literate
  tradition, but he is not the agricultural landscape itself. The
  landscape is the Cap Bon peninsula, the Medjerda valley, the
  coastal plain of modern Tunisia, the inland steppes of the
  Sahel, and the broad belt of dependent Libyan agricultural

  and its army across eight centuries. This narrative treats what
  is recoverable about the landscape, the estates, the labor

  archaeological evidence (Kerkouane domestic-quarter excavations,

The Carthaginian state was sustained by an agricultural economy
that produced surplus for export, fed the city, supplied the
field army, and generated the tax revenue that funded the
maritime and military apparatus. The agricultural landscape that
produced this surplus is partly recoverable through archaeology
and partly under-attested in ways that should be acknowledged
explicitly rather than papered over with Greco-Roman literary
characterizations. What follows is what can be reconstructed
with reasonable evidentiary weight.

## The principal zones

The Carthaginian African territory at its 3rd-century BCE peak
covered approximately the area of modern northern Tunisia plus
parts of eastern Algeria and northwestern Libya. Within this
territory, four agricultural zones are distinguishable.

**The Cap Bon peninsula.** The peninsula jutting northeast from
the Carthaginian capital was the most intensively cultivated
zone in the Carthaginian heartland. Its Mediterranean climate,
its proximity to the city, and its archaeological richness make
it the principal direct evidence base for Carthaginian
agricultural practice. The site of [Kerkouane](/places/kerkouane)
on the peninsula's eastern coast preserves an entire
Carthaginian-period town with domestic-quarter excavations
intact — destroyed in the First Punic War and not subsequently
overbuilt. The Kerkouane evidence (treated below) is the single
richest body of direct material evidence for non-elite
Carthaginian-period rural life. Beyond Kerkouane, the peninsula
preserves dozens of villa and farm sites with oil-press
installations, wine-production facilities, and storage
installations.

**The Medjerda (Bagradas) valley.** The principal river system of
the Carthaginian hinterland runs from the Algerian highlands
through northern Tunisia to the sea near Utica. The valley
floor is the most fertile cereal-producing zone in the region.
The Carthaginian state developed it systematically; the Roman
province of Africa subsequently inherited the developed valley
as the principal grain belt of the early imperial Roman city.
The valley's agricultural success is one of the substantive
continuities across the 146 BCE break: the Romans did not
develop a new agricultural system in the Medjerda; they took
over the existing one and ran it.

**The coastal plain.** Between Cap Bon and the eastern Tunisian
coast, the coastal plain supported mixed agriculture (cereals,
orchards, olives, vines) with a long tradition of
mixed-population cultivation — Punic, Libyo-Phoenician, and
Libyan agricultural communities operating in proximity. The
plain's economic significance is partly its diversity: it
produced not a single export commodity but the full range of
agricultural products the Carthaginian internal economy
required.

**The inland Sahel and the steppe frontier.** South and west of
the coastal plain, the cultivation belt thinned into pastoral
and mixed-pastoral-agricultural territory before transitioning
to the steppe and ultimately the Sahara. The Carthaginian
territorial reach extended into this zone but with decreasing
agricultural intensity; the boundary between Carthaginian
cultivated territory and Numidian pastoral territory was a
gradient rather than a line. The Numidian populations that
played such a substantive role in the Punic Wars operated
substantially in this transitional zone.

## What was grown, in archaeological evidence

The agricultural-products record from Carthaginian sites
combines archaeobotany (carbonized seed evidence from
excavated deposits), archaeozoology (animal-bone evidence),
and the installation record (oil presses, wine vats,
storage facilities).

**Cereals** — wheat (predominantly emmer and durum) and barley
— are documented archaeobotanically across the Carthaginian
heartland. Cereal cultivation was the agricultural foundation,
and cereal surplus from the Medjerda valley and coastal plain
was the principal Carthaginian export commodity in the
commercial network.

**Olives** were a Carthaginian specialty. The peninsula and
inland Tunisia preserve large numbers of Carthaginian-period
olive-press installations — substantial stone-cut press beds
and storage facilities — that document oil production at
export scale. The Punic and Libyo-Phoenician coastal cities
appear to have specialized in oil export, and the Roman-period
documentation of African olive oil as a major Mediterranean
commodity is substantially the continuation of an
already-established Carthaginian industry.

**Wine** is documented through press installations, amphora
production at Punic kiln sites, and amphora finds across the
Mediterranean carrying Carthaginian wine. The Carthaginian wine
industry was less specialized than the olive industry but
substantial.

**Tree-crops and orchard fruit** — pomegranates (whose
botanical name *Punica granatum* preserves the Carthaginian
association), figs, almonds, dates from the southern margin —
are documented in archaeobotanical and literary references. The
pomegranate's prominence in Carthaginian religious iconography
(pomegranate motifs on votive offerings and tomb goods)
overlaps with its prominence in the agricultural landscape.

**Pulses and garden vegetables** rounded out the agricultural
range: chickpeas, lentils, broad beans, the standard
Mediterranean garden produce.

**Livestock** — cattle, sheep, goats, and horses — is documented
archaeozoologically. The horse-breeding tradition that
underwrote Numidian cavalry in the Punic Wars operated partly
within Carthaginian territory and partly in the Numidian
hinterland; the two were less institutionally separated than
the Greek-Roman literary tradition suggests.

## Kerkouane as evidence base

The single most substantively informative archaeological site for
non-elite Carthaginian-period domestic and agricultural life is
[Kerkouane](/places/kerkouane), on the eastern Cap Bon coast.
The town was destroyed in the First Punic War (probably 256 or
255 BCE during the Regulus invasion or its aftermath) and
abandoned permanently. The site was never overbuilt in the
Roman period and was rediscovered in 1952. Excavations have
exposed approximately ninety domestic houses, the town's
internal street grid, sections of the perimeter wall, and
substantial industrial-quarter installations.

What Kerkouane preserves for the agricultural-landscape question:

- **The standardized house plan.** Kerkouane houses are
  relatively uniform in their basic plan (entry vestibule with
  characteristic red-and-white plaster floor; central courtyard;
  surrounding rooms; bathroom installations with sit-down
  hipbaths in many houses). The uniformity suggests an
  administered settlement rather than an organic agglomeration,
  and the implication is that Punic-period rural settlements
  were planned to a substantial degree.
- **The bathroom installations.** Nearly every excavated house
  has a recognizable bathing facility with a sit-down hipbath
  and floor drainage. The density of bathing installations is
  the single most surprising feature of the site to modern
  excavators: it suggests a standard of domestic infrastructure
  substantially above what comparable Greek or Italian rural
  settlements of the period preserve. The cultural reading
  is consequential: Carthaginian rural life was not a poor
  imitation of urban life; it was a substantively developed
  domestic culture in its own right.
- **The mixed-agricultural-industrial economy.** Kerkouane's
  industrial quarter included fish-salting installations,
  purple-dye production (murex-shell middens), and metalworking
  alongside the agricultural infrastructure. The settlement was
  not purely agricultural; it was a mixed-economy rural town
  whose agricultural production was integrated with maritime
  and industrial production.

The Kerkouane evidence pushes against the standard
Greek-Roman characterization of Carthaginian rural life as
exploitative agricultural extraction from a downtrodden Libyan
peasantry. What the site shows is a substantial Punic rural
domestic culture with its own urban-infrastructural standards.
Whether the Libyan peasantry beyond Kerkouane shared in this
standard is a separate question; the Carthaginian-citizen rural
population at Kerkouane plainly did.

## The estate system

The Carthaginian agricultural surplus that fed the export
economy was produced substantially on aristocratic estates.
Greek-Roman literary sources (Diodorus, Polybius) characterize
the Carthaginian countryside as dominated by large estates
worked by dependent and slave labor. The archaeological record
broadly corroborates the literary characterization: substantial
villa sites are documented across the Cap Bon peninsula and
inland Tunisia, with the size and infrastructure of installations
indicating capital-intensive aristocratic operation rather than
smallholder agriculture.

The relationship between aristocratic estates and the smaller
Libyan agricultural communities is the most contested feature
of the Carthaginian agricultural landscape. The standard
reconstruction: a substantial dependent Libyan peasantry paid
tribute to Carthaginian landlords or to the Carthaginian
state, with the surplus extraction rates becoming punitive
during war emergencies (the 4:1 wartime increase that the
First Punic War's end produced, according to Polybius 1.72,
is the load-bearing literary attestation; it triggered the
Libyan participation in the Mercenary War of 241–238 BCE that
substantially threatened the Carthaginian state). The
reconstruction is plausible but the documentation is uneven:
the Libyan peasantry's voice does not survive in the
inscriptional record, and the surviving narrative is filtered
through Polybius's narrow focus on the moments where this
peasantry's grievance produced political-military
consequences.

The substantive observation is that the Carthaginian
agricultural economy depended on labor relationships with a
broader dependent population that was not fully integrated
into Carthaginian citizenship. The "Punic-Libyan interface"
that the
[Numidian-Punic interface narrative](/narratives/the-numidian-punic-interface)
treats culturally has an agricultural-labor dimension that
is equally substantive and equally under-documented.

## The agricultural calendar

What the agricultural year looked like in practical terms is
partly recoverable from a combination of the Mago fragments
preserved in Roman agronomists, the broader Mediterranean
agricultural pattern of the period, and the archaeological
installation record.

The Mediterranean dryland farming year operates around two
principal grain crops (autumn-sown wheat and barley harvested
in early summer) and a parallel tree-crop cycle (olive harvest
in late autumn and winter, vine harvest in late summer). The
labor calendar concentrates intensively at the harvest moments
and spreads out across the rest of the year. The Carthaginian
agricultural year would have followed the same pattern, with
specific North African variations: a longer growing season at
the coast than in the interior; the date harvest at the
southern margin as a distinctively North African element; the
olive harvest distributed across a longer window than in
mainland Greece because of the climate gradient.

The relationship between the agricultural calendar and the
religious calendar is partly recoverable from the inscriptional
record. The Carthaginian religious year included festivals
associated with agricultural seasonality (the spring fertility
festival associated with Tanit; the autumn vintage festivals
that the wider Phoenician-Punic pattern preserves); the
specific calendar of these festivals at Carthage is not
recoverable in detail but the structural relationship is
secure. The treatment of religious practice under the
[Punic religious practice narrative](/narratives/punic-religious-practice)
covers the festival side.

## The post-201 contraction and intensification

The Treaty of 201 BCE that ended the Second Punic War reduced
Carthaginian territory: Numidian-claimed territory was
transferred to Masinissa, and across the subsequent fifty
years Masinissa's incremental encroachment further reduced
what remained. The territorial reduction did not collapse the
Carthaginian agricultural economy. It intensified it: the
surviving Carthaginian territory was cultivated more
intensively, the export of agricultural products (especially
olive oil) continued and possibly increased, and by the 160s
BCE the Carthaginian recovery had restored fiscal capacity to
levels comparable to the pre-Zama period.

Cato's famous demonstration of the Carthaginian fig in the
Roman senate — fresh from Carthage, ostensibly proof of
Carthage's threatening proximity to Rome — is the iconic
literary trace of this agricultural recovery. The fig was the
political object, but the substantive point was the
agricultural-economic vitality the fig represented. Cato's
audience understood that a recovered Carthaginian agricultural
economy was a recovered Carthaginian fiscal base, which was a
potential reconstituted Carthaginian threat.

## The 146 break and the Roman continuity

The destruction of Carthage in 146 BCE did not destroy the
Carthaginian agricultural landscape. The territory was
substantially intact; the agricultural infrastructure (estates,
oil presses, irrigation, storage) was substantially intact;
the dependent agricultural population was substantially in
place. The Roman province of Africa that succeeded the
Carthaginian state inherited a developed agricultural economy
and ran it on substantially the same basis. The estate system,
the oil and wine specialization, the cereal export, the
dependent labor regime — all continued.

The narrative
[Punic continuity in Roman Africa](/narratives/punic-continuity-in-roman-africa)
treats this continuity in its broader cultural and religious
dimensions. For the agricultural-economic dimension
specifically, the continuity is substantively complete: African
Roman wealth in the 1st and 2nd centuries CE is the
continuation of Carthaginian wealth, on the same land, by
substantially the same population, running substantially the
same economic system.

## What we cannot recover

The labor regime details are imperfectly attested. The status
distinctions among agricultural workers (citizen
smallholders; tenant farmers on aristocratic estates;
dependent Libyan peasantry; chattel slaves of various
origins), their proportions, their internal stratification,
the conditions of their daily lives — all these are mostly
unknown. Mago's treatise apparently included substantial
material on managing slave labor, but the surviving Magonic
fragments are weighted toward technical agriculture rather
than labor relations. What can be recovered structurally
(the estate system existed; it depended on dependent and
unfree labor; the Libyan peasantry's grievance was
politically consequential at moments) substantially
exhausts what the evidence base supports.

The peasant calendar of daily life — what the agricultural
year looked like from the perspective of the rural Libyan
or Punic-rural worker rather than the aristocratic estate
owner — is essentially irrecoverable from the surviving
record. The asymmetry is structural: the literary tradition
preserves the aristocratic perspective; the inscriptional
record preserves the votive-religious perspective; the
archaeological record preserves the material infrastructure;
none of them preserves the experiential reality of
agricultural labor as it was lived.

## Sources

The principal direct evidence is archaeological: the Kerkouane
excavations (Fantar's publications are the principal reference;
[Kerkouane place page](/places/kerkouane) gives the orientation)
and the broader survey work on Cap Bon and inland Tunisian rural
settlement patterns. The Magonic fragments (collected in modern
editions and treated through the surviving Roman agronomists)
provide the technical-agricultural reference; the
[Mago of Carthage narrative](/narratives/mago-of-carthage)
treats the transmission chain. For literary sources, Polybius
(particularly 1.72 on the Libyan-tribute increase) and Diodorus
(scattered references to Carthaginian estates) are the principal
ancient sources. For modern syntheses, Hoyos's *The Carthaginians*
(2010) provides accessible English-language overview; Devillers
and Krings, "Mago de Carthage et l'agronomie punique" (in the
[Krings-edited handbook](/sources/krings-manuel-1995), 1995),
is the deepest specialist reference. The
[Bénabou *La résistance africaine*](/sources/benabou-resistance-1976)
treatment of the Roman-period continuity is the principal
post-146 BCE reference.
