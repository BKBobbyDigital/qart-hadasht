# Reading list

The bibliography that grounds *Qart-Hadasht*. A living document — additions welcome.

## Tier 1 — modern narrative & synthesis

These are the books to read first if you want a serious overview of Carthage.

- **Hoyos, Dexter.** *Hannibal: A Hellenistic Life.* Yale, 2003.
  Reads Hannibal as a Hellenistic-era general, not the older "alien Semitic enemy of Rome" frame. Strong on military analysis and the Barcid family's role in Carthaginian politics.

- **Hoyos, Dexter.** *The Carthaginians.* Routledge, 2010.
  Hoyos's broader treatment beyond Hannibal — institutions, religion, society, the empire. Companion volume to the 2003 biography.

- **MacDonald, Eve.** *Carthage: A New History.* Yale, 2015.
  Single-volume modern history grounded in recent archaeology. Foregrounds Carthage as a major Mediterranean power in its own right, not just Rome's antagonist.

- **Miles, Richard.** *Carthage Must Be Destroyed: The Rise and Fall of an Ancient Civilization.* Penguin, 2010.
  Wide-ranging cultural and political history. Particularly strong on Hellenistic-era Carthage and the religious dimensions of the conflict with Rome.

- **Lancel, Serge.** *Carthage: A History.* Trans. Antonia Nevill. Blackwell, 1995.
  By the archaeologist who directed excavations at Carthage for decades. Anchored in physical evidence in ways few general histories are.

- **Quinn, Josephine.** *In Search of the Phoenicians.* Princeton, 2018.
  Argues that "Phoenician" identity is largely a Greek and modern construction — the people we lump together didn't think of themselves that way. Affects how this project models ethnicity and identity.

- **Goldsworthy, Adrian.** *The Fall of Carthage — The Punic Wars 265–146 BC.* Cassell, 2003.
  Military history of the three Punic Wars, meticulous on tactics and operational decisions. The first stop for any battlefield question.

- **Aubet, María Eugenia.** *The Phoenicians and the West — Politics, Colonies and Trade.* 2nd ed. Cambridge, 2001.
  The standard synthesis on Phoenician expansion into the western Mediterranean — the context out of which Carthage grew.

- **Cooper, Paul M. M.** *Fall of Civilizations: Stories of Greatness and Decline.* Duckworth, 2024.
  Includes a substantial chapter on Carthage. Companion to the podcast.

## Tier 1 — primary sources

The ancient texts the encyclopedia repeatedly cites. Public-domain English translations linked from each source page on the site.

- **Polybius.** *Histories.* Books 1, 3, 6, 14, 15. (Shuckburgh, 1889 — [Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0234))
  The single most reliable surviving source. Greek hostage in Rome with access to Scipionic family testimony.

- **Livy.** *Ab Urbe Condita,* Books 21–30. (Roberts, 1912 — [Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0144))
  Fullest narrative of the Second Punic War. Working ~175 years after the fact, often from Polybius. Rhetorically heightened.

- **Appian.** *Punica* (Roman History, Book 8). (White, 1899 — [Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0230))
  Indispensable for the Third Punic War — our principal continuous source for the destruction of Carthage.

- **Diodorus Siculus.** *Library of History.* Books 11–14, 19–20, fragments of 21–32. (Oldfather, 1933 — [LacusCurtius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Diodorus_Siculus/home.html))
  The principal source for early Carthage, the Sicilian Wars, and Agathocles. Drawing on Timaeus, gives access to a tradition independent of the Roman annalists.

- **Aristotle.** *Politics,* Book II.11. (Jowett, 1885 — [Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0058))
  Contemporary 4th-century BCE analysis of the Carthaginian constitution. Indispensable for institutions; nothing comparable survives from the Roman tradition.

- **Justin.** *Epitome of Pompeius Trogus,* Book 18. (Watson, 1853 — [Attalus](https://www.attalus.org/translate/justin1.html))
  The principal preserved account of the Tyrian foundation legend of Carthage.

- **Cornelius Nepos.** *Life of Hannibal.* Short Roman biography, late Republic; preserves details and a sympathetic tone found nowhere else in Latin.

- **Hanno the Navigator** (attrib.). *Periplus.* (Schoff, 1912.)
  A Carthaginian record of an Atlantic-coast voyage along Africa, surviving in Greek translation. The closest thing we have to a Punic-authored text from the classical period.

- **Plutarch.** *Parallel Lives* — esp. Cato the Elder, Fabius Maximus, Marcellus, Aemilius Paullus. (Perrin, 1916 — [Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2008.01.0004))
  Greek biographer ~300 years after the events; preserves anecdotes and detail — especially around Cato and the case for the Third Punic War — found nowhere else.

Add as topics demand.

- Hoyos, Dexter (ed.). *A Companion to the Punic Wars.* Blackwell, 2011.
