# Reading list

The bibliography that grounds *Qart-Hadasht*. A living document — additions welcome.

## Tier 1 — modern narrative & synthesis

These are the books to read first if you want a serious overview of Carthage.

- **MacDonald, Eve.** *Hannibal: A Hellenistic Life.* Yale, 2015.
  Modern biography that situates Hannibal in the Hellenistic Mediterranean rather than the older Romano-centric frame. Strong on archaeology, the Iberian command, and Hannibal's post-war career.

- **MacDonald, Eve.** *Carthage: A New History.* Yale, 2015.
  Single-volume modern history grounded in recent archaeology. Foregrounds Carthage as a major Mediterranean power in its own right, not just Rome's antagonist.

- **Hoyos, Dexter.** *Hannibal's Dynasty: Power and Politics in the Western Mediterranean, 247–183 BC.* Routledge, 2003.
  Treats the Barcids as a political dynasty operating within Carthaginian factional politics. Strong on military analysis and Carthaginian politics.

- **Hoyos, Dexter.** *The Carthaginians.* Routledge, 2010.
  Hoyos's broader treatment beyond Hannibal — institutions, religion, society, the empire.

- **Miles, Richard.** *Carthage Must Be Destroyed: The Rise and Fall of an Ancient Civilization.* Penguin, 2010.
  Wide-ranging cultural and political history. Particularly strong on Hellenistic-era Carthage and the religious dimensions of the conflict with Rome.

- **Lancel, Serge.** *Carthage: A History.* Trans. Antonia Nevill. Blackwell, 1995.
  By the archaeologist who directed excavations at Carthage for decades. Anchored in physical evidence in ways few general histories are.

- **Quinn, Josephine.** *In Search of the Phoenicians.* Princeton, 2018.
  Argues that "Phoenician" identity is largely a Greek and modern construction. Affects how this project models ethnicity.

- **Goldsworthy, Adrian.** *The Fall of Carthage — The Punic Wars 265–146 BC.* Cassell, 2003.
  Military history. The first stop for any battlefield question.

- **Aubet, María Eugenia.** *The Phoenicians and the West — Politics, Colonies and Trade.* 2nd ed. Cambridge, 2001.
  Standard synthesis on Phoenician expansion into the western Mediterranean — the context out of which Carthage grew.

- **Cooper, Paul M. M.** *Fall of Civilizations: Stories of Greatness and Decline.* Duckworth, 2024.
  Includes a substantial chapter on Carthage. Companion to the podcast.

## Tier 1 — primary sources

The ancient texts the encyclopedia repeatedly cites. Public-domain English translations linked from each source page on the site.

- **Polybius.** *Histories.* Books 1, 3, 6, 14, 15. (Shuckburgh, 1889 — [Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0234))
- **Livy.** *Ab Urbe Condita,* Books 21–30. (Roberts, 1912 — [Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0144))
- **Appian.** *Punica.* (White, 1899 — [Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0230))
- **Diodorus Siculus.** *Library of History.* Books 11–14, 19–20, fragments of 21–32.
- **Aristotle.** *Politics,* Book II.11 — the Carthaginian constitution.
- **Justin.** *Epitome of Pompeius Trogus,* Book 18 — the foundation legend.
- **Cornelius Nepos.** *Life of Hannibal.*
- **Hanno the Navigator** (attrib.). *Periplus.* The closest thing to a Punic-authored text from the classical period.
- **Plutarch.** *Parallel Lives* — Cato, Fabius, Marcellus, Aemilius Paullus.

## Tier 2 — academic & specialist scholarship

The deeper bench: handbooks, foreign-language references, journal articles, and older works that remain cited.

### Handbooks and foreign-language references

- **Huss, Werner.** *Die Karthager.* Munich: C. H. Beck, 2004.
  The standard German-language reference. Often the deepest source for Carthaginian institutions, religion, and empire structure.

- **Sznycer, Maurice.** "Carthage et la civilisation punique." In Claude Nicolet (ed.), *Rome et la conquête du monde méditerranéen* vol. 2, pp. 545–593. PUF, 1978.
  Major French synthesis chapter by a leading editor of Punic inscriptions. Authoritative on the Punic-speaking world on its own terms.

- **Krings, Véronique** (ed.). *La civilisation phénicienne et punique.* Brill, 1995. (French; the major reference handbook.)

- **Hoyos, Dexter** (ed.). *A Companion to the Punic Wars.* Blackwell, 2011.

### Identity, perception, and historiography

- **Gruen, Erich S.** *Rethinking the Other in Antiquity.* Princeton, 2011.
  Counterweight to the "Carthage as alien Semitic enemy" reading; argues ancient representations of foreigners are more often curious and integrative than antagonistic.

- **Barceló, Pedro.** "The Perception of Carthage in Classical Greek Historiography." *Acta Classica* 37 (1994): 1–14.
  On how the surviving "Carthage" of the literary tradition is substantially a Greek conceptual artifact.

### Foundation date and Punic epigraphy

- **Krahmalkov, Charles R.** "The Foundation of Carthage, 814 B.C.: The Douïmès Pendant Inscription." *Journal of Semitic Studies* 26.2 (1981): 177–191.
  Argues for epigraphic support of the traditional 814 BCE foundation date. Reading is contested but essential for the question.

### Older standard works (still cited)

- **Picard, Gilbert Charles, and Colette Picard.** *Daily Life in Carthage at the Time of Hannibal.* Trans. A. E. Foster. Macmillan, 1961.
- **Picard, Gilbert Charles, and Colette Picard.** *The Life and Death of Carthage.* Trans. Dominique Collon. Sidgwick and Jackson, 1968.
- **Warmington, B. H.** *Carthage.* Barnes & Noble, 1969 (2nd ed.).
  For decades the standard English-language single-volume history; superseded but still a reference point.

### Topical specialists (to add as topics demand)

- Schwartz, Jeffrey H. et al. — papers on the Tophet and the infant-sacrifice debate.

To add as topics demand:
